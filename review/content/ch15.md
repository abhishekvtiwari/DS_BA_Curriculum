# Ch 15 — Data Visualization Principles
Verdict: One of the strongest chapters in Part II. The principles are accurate, well sourced (Cleveland–McGill, Anscombe, Tufte, WCAG), and anchored on Riverstone numbers that almost all recompute correctly. The real-world story is excellent. For a first-time reader the problems are structural. §15.14 teaches pandas and matplotlib before Python exists (M.2), and Python also turns up in the Tools line, the project, and three exercises. The box-plot SQL uses `PERCENTILE_CONT … WITHIN GROUP`, which Ch 12–13 never taught (M.6). Quartiles, IQR and correlation are used as if known, but the book teaches them in Ch 21. The prerequisite claim that Ch 4 taught "spread" is false. One factual slip in the story: the note says the Q4 plan assumed 33% growth, but that figure applies to October only.
Size: ~1,050 lines, ~27 pages (pp. 220–246). Time needed stated: 12–15 hours over two weeks. Plausible? Yes. The reading is light and the heavy part is the 25 exercises and the five-chart project. Removing the Python material from §15.14 saves about an hour. Adding a by-hand quartile box and a correlation paragraph (below) adds about as much back.

## What works (keep)
- Each principle is taught before-and-after on one real dataset, with the SQL that produced every chart's data and outputs that reconcile (segment shares, month × year pivot, histogram counts summing to 46,356, targets table).
- The "Start from the question" chooser (§15.2) and the levels-of-measurement table tie back to Ch 1 exactly as the author's step-by-step principle intends.
- §15.12's truncated-axis example reuses Ch 14's wrong-vs-clean branch numbers (Delhi ₹101,831,086 vs Bengaluru ₹101,788,506; clean gap ₹14.7 million matches Ch 14), so the lesson connects across chapters.
- The real-world story ("the October that looked like a collapse") separates *attainment* from *demand*. That is a genuinely senior-analyst lesson, backed by numbers that recompute (22.7% YoY, 33% October target uplift, Q4 94.7%).
