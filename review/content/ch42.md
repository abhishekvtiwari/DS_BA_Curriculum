# Ch 42 — Recommender Systems & Ranking
Verdict: A clear, well-argued chapter. It keeps comparing every method against cheap baselines, and it runs an honest leave-one-out test, which is exactly the right habit to teach. Its central metric, though, is mislabelled. Every "precision@5" figure is actually the **hit rate@5**: the share of accounts whose single hidden product appears in the top 5, which is the same as recall@5 here. This contradicts the chapter's own definition, under which one hit in five slots scores 20%. The error runs through the body, the summary table, the real-world story, the exercises and the recap. Matrix factorization, TruncatedSVD and implicit ALS are black boxes with no by-hand example. The `implicit` library is used without an install step. One answer (Ex 9) prints a crash and then discusses results as if the code had run. Recall@k and MAP are not taught at all.
Size: ~1,020 lines / 25 pp (262–286). Time needed stated: 6–9 hours over one week. Plausible? Borderline yes for the current text. Once a by-hand factorization, an install section and metric corrections are added, 8–10 h is more honest.

## What works (keep)
- §42.2 builds item-item cosine similarity on a 5×4 toy, by hand in NumPy, before the full matrix. The numbers are right: 0.980 and 0.000 ✓.
- One evaluation harness (`evaluate`) is shared by every method. It excludes owned items and compares against a popularity baseline, which is exactly Ch 36 §36.9's baseline habit applied to ranking.
- The narrative is honest about failures. More SVD/ALS factors do worse. The 50/50 hybrid doesn't beat item-CF. Content-based loses on its own but is the only thing that works in cold start (§42.6).
- The segment-level popularity finding (§42.7, Ex 8) and the real-world story teach the business-analyst lesson well: test the free version first.
