# Ch 37 — Supervised Learning Algorithms
Verdict: This is a strong, honest practitioner's chapter. Every output I could check against the text or against another output agrees: the Gini, entropy, sigmoid and Naive Bayes hand calculations are right, the eight-row boosting outputs can be reproduced, and repeated numbers match across sections and exercises. Its ending, where the simple model with good features wins and boosting doesn't beat logistic regression on leads, is exactly the lesson a data scientist needs. For a first-time reader it moves too fast. Only four of the nine families get a real by-hand step (sigmoid, Naive Bayes, Gini, boosting), and the boosting one can't be followed by hand because the eight x-values are never shown. Linear regression, regularization, k-NN, random forests and SVMs have none. Most code blocks do four to eight new things at once, and dozens of hyperparameters in the library calls are never explained (`subsample_freq`, `n_jobs`, `thread_count`, `verbose=-1`, `random_seed`, `num_leaves`, `gamma`…). Several cross-references are wrong (statsmodels/CI → Ch 22; AutoML → Ch 40). The "engineered features beat boosting" headline rests on features taken from knowledge of the generator.
Size: ~1,940 lines, 46 book pages (87–132), 24 code blocks in the body plus 9 in the answers, 16 exercises. Time needed stated: 14–18 hours over two to three weeks; plausible? No. That is already tight for nine algorithm families, three new libraries, learning curves, two tuning methods and a project. Once the by-hand steps below are added, restate it as 20–24 hours over three weeks, in two parts (37.47).

## What works (keep)
- The honest comparisons. Regularization is shown to barely help here (§37.2). C barely matters (§37.3). The fold spread is used to judge differences (§37.10–37.11). The test set is scored once (§37.11). §37.12 concludes "keep logistic regression for lead scoring". The AutoML story teaches judgment, not tools.
- The by-hand checks that exist are exact and tied to real output: sigmoid(−2.124) = 0.1068 matched to `predict_proba`, the Naive Bayes posterior checked against the raw crosstab (40 of 50 = 80%), and the Gini split computed by hand and then by code.
- Figure 37.2 (tree depth vs train/valid AUC) and the learning curves in §37.10 make bias and variance visible. The follow-on "fixing bias with features" is a good piece of teaching.
- The "Which algorithm when" table and the Common-mistakes table are excellent reference material for interviews.
