# Ch 57 — LLMOps
Verdict: A strong operations chapter with the right lessons in the right order: prompts as versioned, scored code; a golden set that fails the build on any unparseable reply; the "0 of 60" provider-change demonstration; meter, cache, fallback; refusal rate as the monitor for a system with no accuracy. The problem for a first-time reader is that almost every block imports its machinery (`PROMPTS`, `evaluate`, `Meter`, `Cache`, `call`, the error classes) from companion modules that are never shown, so the "prompt registry", "meter" and "cache" are named and used but never built; the cost figures depend on an exchange rate that appears nowhere; and a few numbers disagree with Chapter 54 (prompt v1 = 21 of 60 here, 12 of 60 there) or overclaim (81% cache hit "on a realistic day" is an artefact of 60 distinct emails; "escalation rate is blind to nothing"). The `parse` function has a real bug on untagged ``` fences.
Size: ~920 extracted lines, 23 pages (pp. 122–144), Time needed stated: 14–18 hours over three weeks; plausible? Yes for the chapter + 18 exercises + 10-step project, provided the reader is given the hidden companion code to read (today, much of the time would go on reverse-engineering `pipeline.py`/`provider.py`). If the internals are added as proposed below, keep 14–18 h but split into three sittings: §57.1–57.4 (registry, gate, provider change), §57.5–57.8 (meter, cache, latency, fallback), §57.9–57.11 + project.

## What works (keep)
- §57.1's table mapping Chapter 56's five versioned things onto an LLM system, and the two additions ("the prompt is config that behaves like code"; "the model is a dependency with its own release schedule"). A compact, correct bridge from Ch 56.
- §57.4 / Figure 57.1: the same prompt, a provider update nobody triggered, 47 → 0 → 47 of 60, and "The model was not worse. A contract we assumed and never enforced had changed." The six-step upgrade playbook, including "re-check the cost … a different tokenizer … can change the bill without changing the price".
- §57.3's second condition (`unparseable == 0`) and the explanation of why accuracy on the parseable subset can hide a format break; §57.8's different responses to rate limits vs timeouts, and `None, "failed"` as a counted outcome, not a swallowed exception.
- §57.9's refusal-rate time series (15–20% → 42% with nothing changed) and the "two signals wearing one coat" split into content gaps vs threshold problems; §57.11's "every complaint becomes a golden-set case". The Monday post-mortem story ("the parser fix took twenty minutes; the two process fixes took ten") is exactly the right lesson.
