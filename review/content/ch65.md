# Ch 65 — FinOps: The Economics of Data Platforms
Verdict: A clear, well-motivated chapter. Its two big lessons land: the warehouse, not the AI, dominates the bill, and a cost NFR must say whether it means average or marginal cost. But the numbers the chapter is built on don't hold together. The reserved-pricing base (₹22,674) is the wrong sum. The defect-model unit cost is ₹1.65 in the output and ₹0.17 in the table. ₹198/day is called "₹198 per order". The showback shares don't match the bill. The sensor-archive volume is about 40× Chapter 49's estimate. And the headline diagnosis ("marginal cost is close to ₹0.50") is asserted with no calculation and contradicts the chapter's own ₹0.62-per-email figure. All unit economics also come pre-computed from a CSV, so the reader never learns how to allocate costs to units, which is the skill the chapter promises.
Size: ~636 lines / 17 pages (pp. 104–120). Time needed stated: 8–10 hours over a week. Plausible? Yes, including the project. Adding the proposed allocation worked example keeps it within 10 h.

## What works (keep)
- The "household budget" analogy and §65.1's four meters (compute, storage, requests, transfer) are a clean, beginner-friendly model of a cloud bill.
- Figure 65.1's finding "the database everyone takes for granted costs more than the AI everyone worries about" is memorable and correct on the chapter's own numbers (66.3% vs 1.9%).
- §65.3's diagnosis of Chapter 60's NFR (fully-loaded vs marginal) and the dual-metric corrected NFR are an excellent lesson in writing checkable requirements. The story's change-log entry models professional honesty.
- Showback before chargeback, and an honest "shared" category instead of false attribution (§65.4).
