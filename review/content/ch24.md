# Ch 24 — Requirements, Storytelling & Stakeholders
Verdict: A clear, humane and practical chapter. The six clarifying questions, the bottom-line-up-front before/after, the memo template and the "can you just change the number?" decision flow are exactly what a beginner analyst needs, and there is no code to stumble on. Its main problem is a dependency error. The whole chapter is built on "Chapter 23's December 2025 revenue diagnosis" and says Ch 23's worked example "began exactly this way", but Ch 23's worked example is May→June. The November→December figures exist only in Ch 23's exercise answers, so a reader who follows the stated prerequisite (§23.11) meets unfamiliar numbers and the answer key is spoiled. There are also logic slips in the model memo ("not fewer in number" when a third of the fall was fewer orders), a character inconsistency (who asked: Anita or Vikram), and wrong interview-bank cross-references.
Size: ~630 lines, 13 book pages (413–425), 0 code blocks, 22 exercises. Time needed stated: 10–12 hours over a week. Plausible? Yes. The reading is about 2 hours and the writing exercises (memo, three slides, grid, pushback) reasonably take 8–10.

## What works (keep)
- §24.1's six clarifying questions and the "ask → left unclarified → clarified" table. It is concrete and immediately usable, and the "Watch out: clarifying questions are not stalling" script is excellent.
- §24.4's side-by-side bottom-up vs BLUF openings of the same finding, and Figure 24.2 (how it was built vs how it's told). This is the clearest demonstration of the pyramid principle a beginner is likely to see.
- §24.8's three honest responses to pushback (fix the error / show both / hold the number), with the test "would you be comfortable explaining this change … to the person who first asked for the number?"
- The real-world story, in which a meeting is cancelled because the answer arrived first, and the takeaway that the valuable outcome came from a question the clear answer made possible.
