# Ch 22 — Statistics Without Fooling Yourself
Verdict: An excellent, honest chapter for a working analyst. Every headline number I recomputed matches: the t-interval, Wilson intervals, the chi-square p-values, the sample-size table, the peeking simulation (reproduced exactly: 4.9 / 14.3 / 23.9%) and the Simpson's standardization. For a first-time reader it is too fast. Confidence intervals, the t-distribution, chi-square, power, Welch/Mann-Whitney, Pearson/Spearman and standardization all arrive as library calls with no formula explained symbol by symbol and no by-hand or spreadsheet step. Several code blocks do four to six new things at once. The A/B write-up claims a pre-declared primary metric (revenue per recipient) that the chapter never tests. Every "Where this leads" cross-reference is wrong. Decision D3 adds the book's first regression teaching here; it is specified in detail as 22.30.
Size: ~716 lines, 16 book pages (378–393), 15 code blocks, 29 exercises + timed challenge. Time needed stated: 15–18 hours over two weeks. Plausible? Yes for the current content. With the by-hand/spreadsheet steps below and the new §22.10 regression section (about 4 hours), restate it as 20–23 hours, over two and a half weeks, in two parts (22.31).

## What works (keep)
- The peeking simulation (§22.4): the reader sees false positives rise from 4.9% to 23.9% instead of being told. The code reproduces the printed numbers exactly with seed 2223.
- The Simpson's paradox transporter example (§22.6) and its real-world story. The numbers are internally exact (79.7/91.1 overall; 86.4/85.0 standardized), and the story's lesson ("the headline number measures the mix") is exactly right.
- "What a p-value is not" table, "Report the effect size and its interval first", and the five-part write-up template (§22.9). These are senior-level habits presented plainly.
- The sample-size table (§22.3), which makes the order-rate test's powerlessness concrete. All seven values are reproduced exactly.
