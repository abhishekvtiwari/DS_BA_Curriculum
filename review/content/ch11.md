# Ch 11 — The Spreadsheet, Mastered: Excel & Google Sheets
Verdict: Excellent professional content. The numbers reconcile across every method (SUMIFS, pivot, FILTER, Power Query, DAX, QUERY all give ₹4,335,471), and the month-end-pack story is one of the best in the book. For a reader who finished Chapter 10 two weeks ago, though, it's two or three chapters in one: ~150 functions and features, M code, DAX, and a competition case. Several code blocks do four or five new things at once, and some are unrunnable as written: the "spare sheet" examples in §11.3, the cross-workbook merge in §11.7, and the table names in the §11.8 model. It also carries five SQL preview boxes, an SQL column in Figure 11.3, and a warm-up exercise that can only be answered with SQL (M.10).
Size: 2,237 extracted lines, about 46 pages (pp. 41–86). 43 exercises, a 9-step project, two timed cases. Time needed stated: 25–30 hours over three to four weeks. Plausible? No. A beginner needs ~20 h for §11.1–11.6, ~10 h for §11.7–11.10 (Power Query, Power Pivot, what-if, Sheets), 4–6 h for the project, 3–4 h for §11.14 and its 45-minute case, and ~6 h for 43 exercises: **35–45 hours over five to six weeks**, unless the chapter is split or its advanced parts are marked "second pass" (11.1).

## What works (keep)
- One dataset and one total through six tools: SUMIFS, pivot (Fig 11.3), FILTER, Power Query refresh test (₹3,895,647.50 without December), DAX, QUERY. The reader learns that methods must agree, and every total I checked does.
- The story "the month-end pack that was almost right". Four planted errors (short range −₹136,023, double paste +₹46,555, SUM including a cancelled order +₹24,800, typed number +₹72) partly cancel to −₹64,596. Each is found with a named audit step from §11.12, and the fix is a process, not cell edits. Exercise 23 lets the reader find them first.
- The watch-outs are exactly the ones that bite in real work: SUMIF vs SUMIFS argument order, the two commas before XLOOKUP's match_mode, lines vs distinct orders in a pivot, deleting Power Query's automatic Changed Type, "last means last in the list", forgetting the GROUPBY filter.
- The lookup section explains every XLOOKUP argument in a table, and then shows INDEX/MATCH built from MATCH alone → INDEX alone → combined. This is the line-by-line pattern the rest of the chapter should copy.
