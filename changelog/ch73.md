# 3 October 2026 · Section 73.8, the predict-the-number round

The third bank brought to the standard approved for 71.11 — and the first where the obvious
material was already in the chapter.

## What was cut, and why

The first draft had twelve core questions. Four were dropped before anything was inserted, because
Chapter 73 already covers them, with simulations:

| Drafted | Already in the chapter |
|---|---|
| A 99% accurate test for a 1-in-100 disease | **Q73-005**, with a natural-frequency table |
| The birthday problem | **Q73-007** |
| Twenty A/A tests at alpha 0.05 | **Q73-028**, plus Q73-029 on Bonferroni |
| Peeking and stopping at the first significant result | **Q73-025**, which simulates it and reports 16.3% |

Three rapid-fire rows went the same way: independent against mutually exclusive events (Q73-001,
Q73-002), the 5,200/4,800 split (Q73-026) and the novelty effect (Q73-027).

Keeping them would have been padding, and worse, the peeking question would have printed 26.2%
beside Q73-025's 16.3% — both correct for their own number of looks, and together a contradiction
a reader would have had to untangle for no benefit.

This is worth recording for the remaining banks: **Chapter 73 was already the best-covered of the
fourteen.** The conceptual statistics traps were all there. What was missing was something else.

## What went in

**Section 73.8, "Predict the number: the arithmetic that quietly goes wrong."** 4,785 words, 8
core questions (Q73-038 to 045) and 11 rapid-fire rows (Q73-046 to 056), taking the chapter from
37 questions to 56. Nothing renumbered; the section appends after 73.7.

These are not conceptual traps. Each is an analysis that runs without error, produces a plausible
number, and is wrong, because a tool made a choice and did not mention it:

| | |
|---|---|
| Q73-038 | `np.std` divides by n, `pd.Series.std` by n − 1: 2.000 against 2.138 on the same eight numbers |
| Q73-039 | The mean of three daily conversion rates is 36.7%; the real rate is 10.8% |
| Q73-040 | What "95% confidence" counts, with a coverage simulation: 94.9% of 5,000 intervals |
| Q73-041 | Halving the detectable effect: 14,744 per arm becomes 57,756, then 228,547 |
| Q73-042 | p-values are uniform under the null — ten bands of about 10%, and 5.0% below 0.05 |
| Q73-043 | Anscombe's quartet: identical mean, sd, correlation and slope to three decimals |
| Q73-044 | SE of a difference is 1.03, not 1.45: variances add, standard errors do not |
| Q73-045 | `y = x²` over a symmetric range gives a Pearson correlation of exactly 0.000000 |

Two of them deepen ground a rapid-fire row already touched, and say so in the first line:
Q73-040 points at Q73-019, Q73-041 at Q73-020.

## Verification

Every figure was produced by running the code, with the chapter's own setup cell and
`rng = np.random.default_rng(73)` reseeded per cell, on numpy 2.4.3, scipy 1.18.0, statsmodels
0.14.6 and pandas 3.0.2. The rapid-fire claims were checked separately rather than reasoned about:
`binom.pmf(5, 10, 0.5)` is 0.2461, the median and mean of `[1, 2, 3, 4, 100]` are 3 and 22, and
numpy's nine percentile methods give six different answers for the 90th percentile of 1 to 10.

Two things were fixed during verification. The Anscombe code block referenced a dictionary it never
defined, so the data is now in the block and it runs as printed. And a KS test against the uniform
distribution was dropped from Q73-042: it returned p = 0.034 on 5,000 genuinely uniform p-values, a
one-in-thirty fluke that would have read as evidence against the very point the question makes.
