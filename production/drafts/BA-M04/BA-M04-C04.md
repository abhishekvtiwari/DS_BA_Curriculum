## Purpose

This chapter develops the controlled theory for process measures within end-to-end business process analysis. It connects purpose, evidence, notation, measurement, governance, and improvement while preserving the distinction between an observed process, an approved model, and a proposed design.

The chapter contains Phase 1 theory only. It does not contain MCQs, exercises, assignments, implementation labs, guided projects, case studies, capstone work, or release artifacts.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to define every controlled concept, select it for an appropriate analytical question, identify required evidence and governance, recognize common failure modes, and explain its relationship to end-to-end process value.

## BA-04-C04-T01 - Cycle Time

### Concept

Cycle time is the elapsed time from the start to completion of a defined unit of work or process segment. Definitions vary, so the measurement boundary and treatment of waiting must be explicit.

### Analysis and application

Specify start and end events, unit, population, calendar, business hours, pauses, rework, percentiles, and data source. Median and tail measures often reveal more than an average.

### Quality checks and limitations

Do not compare cycle times with different boundaries or exclude difficult cases silently. Link speed to quality and outcome so faster failure is not mistaken for improvement.

## BA-04-C04-T02 - Lead Time

### Concept

Lead time is the total elapsed time experienced from a demand or request point to fulfillment. It commonly includes queueing and waiting that internal cycle-time measures omit.

### Analysis and application

Define whose clock is measured, the trigger, fulfillment condition, exclusions, and segmentation. Decompose lead time into processing, waiting, transport, approval, and rework to identify causes.

### Quality checks and limitations

Check abandoned, rejected, paused, and reopened cases. A reduced internal cycle time may not reduce customer lead time if work waits elsewhere.

## BA-04-C04-T03 - Throughput

### Concept

Throughput is the number of completed units produced by a process per unit of time under defined conditions. It reflects completions, not work started or inventory moved between teams.

### Analysis and application

Specify the completion definition, time interval, unit type, mix, quality threshold, and seasonality. Segment heterogeneous work and reconcile counts with inflow and work in progress.

### Quality checks and limitations

Higher throughput is not automatically valuable if demand, defects, or downstream capacity differ. Avoid incentives that encourage easy-item selection or premature closure.

## BA-04-C04-T04 - Capacity

### Concept

Capacity is the sustainable amount of work a process or resource can complete under specified conditions. It depends on skills, equipment, schedules, variability, dependencies, maintenance, and quality expectations.

### Analysis and application

Distinguish theoretical, planned, demonstrated, and effective capacity. Model constraints and product mix rather than multiplying headcount by nominal hours. Include resilience and recovery requirements.

### Quality checks and limitations

Do not plan every resource at maximum occupancy. Validate assumptions with observed performance and explain buffers, cross-training, and specialist bottlenecks.

## BA-04-C04-T05 - Utilization

### Concept

Utilization is the proportion of available capacity consumed over a period. It can describe people, equipment, or systems, but the denominator and productive-use definition must be explicit.

### Analysis and application

High utilization can increase queues and lead time when demand varies. Interpret it with throughput, service levels, variability, and bottleneck location rather than optimizing every resource independently.

### Quality checks and limitations

Avoid treating breaks, training, maintenance, control work, or accessibility support as waste. Check whether local utilization damages end-to-end flow.

## BA-04-C04-T06 - WIP

### Concept

Work in progress (WIP) is the number or amount of started but unfinished items within a defined process boundary. It includes queued, waiting, blocked, and actively processed items.

### Analysis and application

Define item identity, boundary, status, snapshot or average method, and aging. Little's Law connects average WIP, throughput, and lead time only under stable, consistently measured conditions.

### Quality checks and limitations

Monitor aging and blocked work, not only totals. Starting more work can raise apparent activity while lengthening delivery and hiding defects.

## BA-04-C04-T07 - Service Levels

### Concept

A service level states a measurable commitment or target for availability, response, completion, quality, or support. It must identify scope, population, threshold, time basis, exclusions, and evidence.

### Analysis and application

Distinguish customer promise, internal operating target, and legal or contractual obligation. Use percentile or distribution measures where averages conceal poor experiences, and define priority categories before measuring them.

### Quality checks and limitations

Check clock stops, gaming, unequal performance by group, and exclusions. Meeting a service level does not prove the service meets the underlying need.

## BA-04-C04-T08 - Defects

### Concept

A defect is a failure of an output or process result to meet a defined requirement or acceptance condition. Defect measures depend on opportunity, severity, detection point, and unit of analysis.

### Analysis and application

Define taxonomy, source, severity, escape point, rework, duplicate handling, denominator, and ownership. Distinguish process defects from changing expectations and from incidents with wider impact.

### Quality checks and limitations

Track first-pass yield and escaped defects alongside counts. Avoid blaming individuals or reducing reporting incentives; defect data should support learning and control.

## Integrated process-analysis model

Begin with the need, customer, outcome, and boundary. Establish the current state from multiple sources, select a representation appropriate to the decision, and retain assumptions and variants. Define measures before proposing change. Analyze constraints, handoffs, rework, risks, controls, data, and affected groups. Design a future state as a governed hypothesis, then trace transition and monitoring requirements to authorized outcomes.

## Common misconceptions

- A process is not the same as a department, procedure, system, or diagram.
- A model is a selective representation, not unquestionable operational truth.
- Detail is not quality unless it supports the intended decision.
- Local speed or utilization does not prove better end-to-end performance.
- Automation and standardization do not remove ownership or exceptions.

## Ethics, controls, and professional boundaries

Process change redistributes effort, delay, access, discretion, monitoring, and risk. Include workers, customers, disabled people, external parties, and indirectly affected groups as relevant. Protect personal and operationally sensitive data, distinguish observation from judgment, and preserve dissent. Analysts structure evidence and decisions but do not replace legal, safety, security, privacy, accessibility, statistical, or workforce specialists.
