## Purpose

Business analysis helps people make better choices about change. It starts before a team writes detailed requirements or selects technology. It asks what is happening, whose needs matter, why change may be necessary, what value is expected, which conditions shape the situation, and how evidence should influence a decision.

This first chapter establishes a durable definition of business analysis. It explains the purpose and value of the practice, separates business analysis from neighboring roles, describes the mindset expected of a professional, and shows how evidence and judgment work together. These foundations are necessary before learning elicitation, requirements engineering, process analysis, analytics, product work, testing, strategy, or formal BRD, PRD, and FRD creation.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Define business analysis without reducing it to documentation, meetings, or software requirements.
- Explain why organizations use business analysis before, during, and after change.
- Describe value from the perspectives of different stakeholders and contexts.
- Distinguish the practice from common job titles and adjacent disciplines.
- Recognize the behaviors of a disciplined, ethical, curious, and outcome-focused analyst.
- Separate evidence, assumptions, interpretations, recommendations, and authorized decisions.

## A simple model of business analysis

The International Institute of Business Analysis describes business analysis as a practice that enables change by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver stakeholder value in context. Its Business Analysis Core Concept Model connects six ideas:

```text
Context shapes stakeholders and needs.
Needs motivate change.
Change introduces or alters a solution.
A solution is valuable only when it addresses needs for stakeholders in context.
Evidence tests every connection.
```

The six concepts are not a sequence in which one can be ignored after moving to the next. They form a system. A solution that satisfies one stakeholder may reduce value for another. A change in regulation can alter context, create a need, constrain possible solutions, and change how value is judged. Business analysis repeatedly examines these relationships as new evidence appears.

## BA-01-C01-T01 - Definition

### Plain-language definition

Business analysis is the disciplined work of understanding a situation, identifying needs, clarifying desired outcomes, evaluating possible changes, and helping authorized people choose solutions that can produce value.

The word **business** does not limit the practice to commercial companies. A business can be understood as an organized system that pursues outcomes. Government agencies, charities, hospitals, universities, internal departments, community organizations, and digital platforms all have stakeholders, needs, constraints, capabilities, processes, decisions, and forms of value.

The word **analysis** does not mean merely breaking information into pieces or calculating numbers. It includes discovering relevant information, finding relationships, examining causes and consequences, comparing alternatives, identifying uncertainty, testing assumptions, synthesizing a coherent view, and communicating what a decision-maker needs to know.

### The six foundational concepts

**Change** is a deliberate transformation in response to a need. It may alter a process, policy, role, product, service, capability, technology, organizational structure, data flow, supplier arrangement, or customer experience. Change can be small or enterprise-wide.

**Need** is a problem or opportunity that may justify action. A need is not automatically a request. “Add a dashboard” is a proposed response; the underlying need might be faster detection of service failures. Analysts look beneath requested features to understand the condition that motivates them.

**Solution** is a particular way of satisfying one or more needs. A solution may include technology, but it may also involve training, policy, workflow, organization design, data, communication, or no new system at all. Business analysis should not assume that every need requires software.

**Stakeholder** is an individual or group with a relevant relationship to the need, change, or solution. Stakeholders include more than sponsors and direct users. They may include customers, employees, operations, regulators, suppliers, support teams, affected communities, auditors, security specialists, and people who experience indirect consequences.

**Value** is the importance, usefulness, or worth of something to a stakeholder in a context. Value can involve revenue, savings, safety, access, compliance, time, quality, trust, resilience, learning, reputation, employee experience, or harm avoided. Different stakeholders can value the same outcome differently.

**Context** is the set of relevant circumstances surrounding the change. It includes strategy, culture, markets, laws, technology, history, skills, timing, resources, politics, risks, customer behavior, and operating conditions. A solution that works in one context may fail in another.

### Business analysis as a practice

Business analysis is a practice rather than one fixed procedure. The exact activities depend on the situation. An analyst considering enterprise strategy may study capabilities, investment choices, and external forces. An analyst improving a process may observe work, measure delays, and model handoffs. A product analyst may study user needs, product behavior, and outcome metrics. A systems analyst may define data, rules, interfaces, and functional behavior.

These are different expressions of the same underlying discipline: understand needs in context, enable reasoned change, and pursue stakeholder value.

### What business analysis is not

Business analysis is not simply:

- Taking meeting minutes.
- Writing whatever stakeholders request.
- Producing a BRD, PRD, FRD, or user stories.
- Managing a project schedule.
- Designing screens without understanding user needs.
- Producing reports without connecting them to decisions.
- Translating business words into technical words.
- Defending a solution selected before the problem was understood.

These activities may appear in business analysis work, but none defines the practice by itself.

### Conceptual example

A retailer reports that online orders are frequently canceled. One manager asks for a new customer notification system. Another requests more warehouse staff. A business analyst first defines the situation: when cancellations occur, which products and customers are affected, what causes are suspected, what data is available, how the current process works, and what outcomes matter.

Evidence may reveal that inventory is reserved too late, causing accepted orders to compete for the same stock. The need is reliable inventory commitment, not merely additional notifications. Possible changes could include reservation rules, data synchronization, process timing, supplier controls, customer communication, or combinations of these. Business analysis keeps the organization from treating the first suggested feature as the confirmed problem.

## BA-01-C01-T02 - Purpose

### Why organizations need business analysis

Organizations face more possible changes than they can fund or absorb. They also operate with incomplete information, competing goals, limited capacity, and stakeholders who see different parts of the situation. The purpose of business analysis is to improve the quality of decisions about change.

This purpose can be expressed through six questions:

1. What is happening now?
2. What need, problem, opportunity, or obligation deserves attention?
3. What outcome would count as improvement?
4. Who is affected, who contributes knowledge, and who has decision authority?
5. Which changes are feasible, desirable, responsible, and valuable?
6. How will the organization know whether value was delivered?

Business analysis makes these questions explicit. It does not guarantee a successful decision, but it improves the evidence, reasoning, shared understanding, and accountability behind the decision.

### Before change

Before an initiative begins, business analysis helps determine whether action is justified. It can clarify strategy, identify a capability gap, quantify a problem, explore an opportunity, identify affected stakeholders, define desired outcomes, compare options, and expose constraints or risks.

This early work prevents teams from launching projects whose purpose is unclear. It can also conclude that no project should begin, that more evidence is required, or that a policy or process change is preferable to new technology.

### During change

During delivery, business analysis preserves alignment between intent and implementation. It refines needs into requirements, models processes and information, supports prioritization, resolves ambiguity, evaluates impacts, links requirements to tests, and helps stakeholders make trade-offs.

The purpose is not to freeze the original plan. New learning can alter scope or solution choices. The analyst makes changes visible, connects them to evidence and authority, and helps the team understand their consequences.

### After change

Business analysis continues after implementation because delivery is not the same as value realization. A system may meet its specification but fail to improve the business outcome. Users may adopt workarounds. Costs may shift elsewhere. A control may reduce one risk while creating another.

Post-change analysis compares expected and actual outcomes, investigates gaps, identifies unintended effects, evaluates solution limitations, and recommends further action. This closes the reasoning loop between the original need and realized value.

### Purpose at different levels

At the **strategic level**, business analysis helps decide which capabilities, markets, services, policies, or investments deserve attention.

At the **initiative level**, it helps frame the problem, define outcomes and scope, evaluate options, and align stakeholders.

At the **delivery level**, it helps specify behavior, data, rules, quality, acceptance, and operational readiness.

At the **operational level**, it helps measure performance, diagnose causes, improve processes, and evaluate whether the solution remains fit for purpose.

The techniques change, but the purpose remains enabling evidence-based change that can deliver value.

## BA-01-C01-T03 - Value

### Value is contextual

Value is not synonymous with revenue. A public service may value equitable access. A hospital may value safety and timely treatment. A bank may value regulatory compliance and fraud reduction. A customer may value convenience while an operations team values reliability. A change can create value for one stakeholder and impose cost or risk on another.

Business analysis helps make these perspectives visible rather than hiding them behind a single score.

### Forms of value

Value may be:

- **Financial:** revenue, margin, savings, avoided loss, cash flow, or return on investment.
- **Operational:** speed, capacity, quality, reliability, accuracy, or reduced rework.
- **Customer or user:** accessibility, convenience, confidence, safety, satisfaction, or successful task completion.
- **Strategic:** differentiation, market entry, capability growth, learning, or flexibility.
- **Risk-related:** reduced fraud, improved resilience, regulatory compliance, privacy protection, or safer operations.
- **Social or public:** fairness, inclusion, transparency, health, education, or environmental benefit.
- **Employee-related:** reduced burden, better tools, safer work, clearer decisions, or skill development.

Tangible value is easier to observe or measure directly. Intangible value may be indirect but still important. Trust, reputation, morale, and strategic flexibility should not be ignored simply because measurement is difficult. They should be represented honestly, with appropriate evidence and uncertainty.

### Potential, planned, delivered, and realized value

**Potential value** is the benefit that might exist if a need is addressed. **Planned value** is the outcome incorporated into an approved change. **Delivered value** refers to capabilities or outputs made available. **Realized value** is the benefit actually experienced in operation.

Confusing these states produces false success. Launching a dashboard delivers an output. It realizes value only if people use trustworthy information to make better decisions and the relevant outcome improves.

### Value and trade-offs

Resources are limited, so business analysis supports comparison. An option may deliver more benefit but take longer. Another may reduce risk but constrain user experience. A low-cost change may create operational burden. An apparently valuable feature may benefit only a small group while excluding others.

A disciplined comparison states criteria, evidence, stakeholder perspectives, uncertainty, dependencies, time horizon, and consequences. The analyst should not manipulate criteria to justify a preferred solution.

### Preventing solution bias

Solution bias occurs when attention moves too quickly from a need to a favored answer. It narrows discovery and encourages selective evidence. Analysts counter it by asking:

- What outcome is the proposed solution expected to produce?
- What evidence supports the problem definition?
- Which assumptions connect the solution to the outcome?
- What alternatives exist, including process or policy changes?
- Who benefits, who bears cost, and who may be excluded?
- How will value be measured after implementation?

### Example of competing value

A company wants to shorten its customer identity checks. Customers value speed, compliance values accuracy, fraud teams value detection, operations values manageable workload, and executives value growth. Removing checks could improve completion but increase fraud and regulatory exposure. Adding checks could reduce risk but exclude legitimate customers.

Business analysis does not declare one perspective correct. It makes the value dimensions and constraints explicit, supports alternatives such as risk-based verification, and helps authorized stakeholders select an acceptable balance.

## BA-01-C01-T04 - Role boundaries

### Practice, role, and job title

Business analysis is a practice. A business analyst is one role that performs it. People with titles such as product manager, product owner, systems analyst, process analyst, data analyst, consultant, architect, project manager, service designer, operations manager, or subject-matter expert may also perform business analysis activities.

Conversely, not everything assigned to a person titled Business Analyst is business analysis. The title may include administration, testing, product support, reporting, coordination, or project controls. Clear boundaries should be based on responsibilities and authority rather than title alone.

### Business analyst and project manager

The business analyst focuses primarily on needs, outcomes, requirements, options, stakeholder value, and the impact of change. The project manager focuses primarily on organizing delivery through scope, schedule, resources, cost, risk, governance, and coordination.

The roles overlap in stakeholder engagement, risk, scope, planning, and communication. A useful distinction is that project management asks, “How do we deliver this initiative effectively?” while business analysis also asks, “Are we addressing the right need, and will the change produce value?” One person may perform both roles, but the questions and accountabilities remain distinct.

### Business analyst and product manager or product owner

Product management owns product direction, market or user value, prioritization, and lifecycle choices. Product ownership often includes maximizing product value and managing the product backlog within a delivery framework. Business analysis contributes structured discovery, requirement analysis, models, evidence, traceability, impact assessment, and decision support.

In some organizations a product manager performs most business analysis. In others, a business analyst partners with product leadership. The analyst should not assume priority authority unless it has been delegated.

### Business analyst and systems analyst

A systems analyst concentrates on how information systems behave and interact. This can include system boundaries, data, interfaces, functional behavior, technical constraints, and integration impacts. Business analysis can operate at a broader business level that includes policy, process, organization, capabilities, and value.

The roles often meet where business needs become system requirements. A business analyst should understand enough technology to communicate consequences and ask sound questions, but should not claim architecture or engineering authority without the relevant competence and mandate.

### Business analyst and data analyst

A data analyst obtains, prepares, analyzes, and presents data to answer questions or support decisions. A business analyst frames needs, stakeholders, processes, requirements, options, and change. Both rely on evidence and business context.

The distinction is one of emphasis rather than a rigid wall. A business analyst may analyze data, while a data analyst may clarify stakeholder needs. In this curriculum, Data Analyst is a branch of the Data Science pipeline, while Business Analyst remains a separate main pipeline. Shared skills should be cross-referenced rather than duplicated.

### Business analyst and subject-matter expert

A subject-matter expert knows a domain deeply. The analyst knows how to elicit, structure, test, connect, and communicate knowledge for change. The analyst should respect expertise without treating every expert statement as complete or universally representative. The expert should inform decisions; the analyst should help expose assumptions, exceptions, stakeholder differences, and impacts.

### Business analyst and decision-maker

An analyst supports decisions but does not automatically own them. The analyst may recommend an option, identify criteria, explain evidence, and challenge weak reasoning. Authorization may remain with a sponsor, product owner, risk authority, policy owner, steering group, or other accountable role.

The analyst must clearly distinguish analysis from approval. Presenting a recommendation as if it were an authorized decision damages governance and trust.

### Healthy boundaries

Healthy role boundaries include:

- Stating who owns each decision.
- Distinguishing recommendation from approval.
- Escalating conflicts that exceed delegated authority.
- Seeking specialist review for legal, security, privacy, accessibility, accounting, clinical, or engineering questions.
- Refusing to certify evidence the analyst cannot verify.
- Collaborating across disciplines without protecting artificial territory.

Boundaries should enable cooperation, not create excuses. “That is not my job” is weak when a material risk is visible. A professional raises the issue and connects it to the appropriate owner.

## BA-01-C01-T05 - Professional mindset

### Curiosity with discipline

Curiosity drives the analyst to ask why a condition exists, how work really happens, what exceptions occur, and what has not yet been considered. Discipline prevents curiosity from becoming uncontrolled scope. Questions are connected to the approved problem, decision, and context.

### Outcome orientation

An outcome-focused analyst asks what improvement must occur, not merely what artifact must be delivered. Requirements, diagrams, reports, and workshops are means. Their quality matters because they support outcomes, but completing them does not prove success.

### Systems thinking

Systems thinking considers relationships among people, process, policy, information, technology, incentives, suppliers, and environment. Local optimization can damage the wider system. Automating one team’s task may shift work to customers or another department. Faster approval may create downstream rework.

The analyst looks for feedback loops, delays, dependencies, unintended effects, and boundaries. This does not require modeling everything. It requires awareness that changes rarely act in isolation.

### Intellectual humility

Analysts work with incomplete information. Professional confidence means being able to act responsibly under uncertainty, not pretending uncertainty is absent. Useful statements include:

- “The available evidence supports this interpretation.”
- “This remains an assumption and has the following impact if false.”
- “These stakeholders disagree, and this role owns the decision.”
- “I do not have the specialist authority to confirm that claim.”

Changing a view when stronger evidence appears is a strength.

### Constructive challenge

Business analysts should question unclear needs, unsupported constraints, solution bias, conflicting requirements, unmeasurable outcomes, and missing stakeholders. Challenge should be directed at reasoning and evidence, not at personal status.

A constructive challenge combines respect with specificity: “The requested dashboard may help, but we have not yet established which decision is delayed, which data is trustworthy, or how success will be measured.”

### Facilitation and inclusion

The loudest participant is not necessarily the most informed or most affected. Analysts create conditions in which relevant perspectives can be heard. They adapt language, formats, timing, and channels; recognize power differences; include accessibility needs; and distinguish consensus from suppressed disagreement.

### Ethical responsibility

An analyst may encounter pressure to hide risk, exaggerate benefits, exclude inconvenient stakeholders, manipulate measures, or approve unsupported claims. Professional responsibility includes transparency about evidence, uncertainty, harm, privacy, confidentiality, and conflicts of interest.

Ethics is not a final compliance check. It affects which questions are asked, whose outcomes count as value, which data is collected, how alternatives are compared, and what consequences are accepted.

### Communication and traceability

Professional communication is audience-aware without becoming misleading. An executive summary can be concise while preserving material risk. A technical specification can be precise without burying the underlying need. Traceability enables different levels of communication to remain connected.

### Continuous learning

Business contexts, technology, laws, methods, and stakeholder expectations change. A professional analyst develops domain literacy, analytical techniques, facilitation, written communication, data literacy, and technical awareness. Learning should improve judgment rather than produce a collection of fashionable tools.

## BA-01-C01-T06 - Evidence and judgment

### Why both are necessary

Evidence without judgment is raw information. Judgment without evidence is vulnerable to bias and unsupported confidence. Business analysis combines the two.

Evidence can include interviews, observations, process measures, financial records, system logs, customer research, experiments, policies, regulations, contracts, defects, market information, and prior decisions. Judgment determines which evidence is relevant, credible, sufficient, current, and applicable to the decision.

### A hierarchy of statements

Analysts should distinguish:

- **Observation:** what was directly seen or recorded.
- **Reported statement:** what a person or source claims.
- **Data finding:** a result produced from defined data and method.
- **Interpretation:** an explanation of what information may mean.
- **Assumption:** a condition provisionally treated as true.
- **Hypothesis:** a testable proposed explanation.
- **Requirement:** a necessary capability or condition within scope.
- **Recommendation:** an advised course of action based on analysis.
- **Decision:** an authorized choice.

For example, “42% of sampled applications required manual correction” is a data finding only if the sample, period, definition, and method are known. “The form is confusing” is an interpretation. “Changing the form will reduce correction by half” is a hypothesis. “Revise the form” is a recommendation until an authorized role decides.

### Evidence quality

Evidence should be assessed for:

- **Relevance:** Does it address the question or decision?
- **Credibility:** Is the source dependable and appropriately qualified?
- **Validity:** Does the method measure what it claims to measure?
- **Reliability:** Would the method produce reasonably consistent results?
- **Representativeness:** Does it reflect the affected population and contexts?
- **Recency:** Is it current enough for the decision?
- **Completeness:** What material information is absent?
- **Traceability:** Can the source, transformation, and interpretation be examined?
- **Bias:** What incentives, sampling effects, framing, or exclusions may distort it?

No evidence source is automatically perfect. A policy may be authoritative but outdated. A large dataset may encode a poor definition. An expert may know common cases but overlook rare exceptions. User feedback may overrepresent people motivated to complain. Triangulation across independent sources often produces a stronger view.

### Judgment under uncertainty

Organizations rarely have complete evidence. Waiting for certainty can itself cause harm or lost opportunity. The analyst helps decision-makers understand what is known, unknown, assumed, disputed, and time-sensitive.

Proportionate judgment considers the consequence of error. A reversible, low-cost experiment may proceed with limited evidence. An irreversible change affecting safety, rights, privacy, or large investment demands stronger assurance.

### Bias and reasoning traps

Common traps include:

- **Confirmation bias:** seeking evidence that supports a favored answer.
- **Anchoring:** giving excessive weight to the first number or solution proposed.
- **Availability bias:** treating memorable examples as typical.
- **Authority bias:** accepting a senior person’s statement without examination.
- **Survivorship bias:** studying visible successes while ignoring failures.
- **Sunk-cost reasoning:** continuing because resources have already been spent.
- **False precision:** presenting uncertain estimates with unjustified exactness.
- **Metric substitution:** optimizing an easy measure that does not represent the real outcome.

Analysts reduce these risks by defining criteria before comparing options, recording alternatives, seeking disconfirming evidence, exposing assumptions, reviewing with diverse participants, and separating facts from interpretations.

### Conceptual decision record

Consider a team choosing whether to automate refund approval. A responsible analysis would record the current workload, error and fraud rates, customer delay, policy rules, exception patterns, staff impact, system capabilities, privacy concerns, and alternative options. It would state data limitations and assumptions. The recommendation might support automation only for low-risk cases with human review elsewhere.

The accountable owner decides. Later, outcome measures test whether the decision delivered value. If the evidence changes, the decision can be revisited without pretending the earlier reasoning never existed.

## Common misconceptions

### “A business analyst writes requirements after others make the important decisions”

Too narrow. Business analysis can begin before an initiative exists and continue through value evaluation after delivery. Requirement writing is one part of the practice.

### “The stakeholder knows exactly what is required”

Stakeholders hold essential knowledge, but each sees the situation from a particular position. Requests may contain assumptions, preferred solutions, or incomplete awareness of downstream effects. The analyst collaborates with stakeholders rather than acting as either a passive recorder or an unaccountable expert.

### “Business means financial value only”

False. Value can include safety, access, compliance, trust, quality, learning, resilience, and public outcomes.

### “Analysis removes uncertainty”

False. Good analysis makes uncertainty visible, reduces it where worthwhile, and supports proportionate decisions where it remains.

### “Technology knowledge turns the analyst into a developer”

False. Technical literacy helps an analyst ask better questions and understand impacts. Role authority and responsibility remain distinct.

## Advanced synthesis

Business analysis can be viewed as an assurance function for organizational reasoning. It creates a traceable argument connecting context, evidence, needs, stakeholders, desired value, selected change, solution behavior, and measured outcomes.

This argument is never purely technical. It includes human interests, organizational power, incentives, ethics, uncertainty, and trade-offs. It is also never purely conversational. Important claims and decisions require durable evidence, defined ownership, and controlled communication.

The strongest analyst does not merely produce more artifacts. The strongest analyst improves the quality of collective understanding. They help an organization notice when it is solving the wrong problem, measuring the wrong outcome, ignoring an affected stakeholder, treating a preference as a constraint, or claiming value before it has been realized.

### How the practice adapts to context

The foundations remain stable, but their expression changes with the environment. In a predictive project, significant scope and requirements may be analyzed before delivery commitments are made. In an adaptive product environment, analysis is continuous: broad outcomes are clarified early, while detailed needs and acceptance conditions emerge in smaller increments. Neither approach removes the need for evidence, value, stakeholder understanding, or decision authority.

In a regulated environment, traceability, formal review, segregation of duties, retained evidence, and approved baselines may be mandatory. In a small internal experiment, the control system may be lighter because the change is inexpensive and reversible. Proportionate practice does not mean casual practice. Even a short experiment should state its hypothesis, users, measure, risk, decision owner, and stopping conditions.

Business analysis also changes according to the object being changed. A policy initiative may focus on public outcomes, legal authority, eligibility rules, and unintended consequences. A process initiative may focus on flow, variation, handoffs, capacity, controls, and customer experience. A data initiative may focus on definitions, grain, quality, lineage, access, interpretation, and decision use. A product initiative may focus on user problems, behavior, prioritization, adoption, and learning. A systems initiative may focus on functions, interfaces, states, data, quality attributes, and operational behavior.

These variations do not create unrelated professions. They reveal different parts of the same reasoning system. The analyst must understand enough of the domain to recognize material questions while collaborating with specialists who hold deeper authority.

Organizational maturity also affects the work. In a mature environment, policies, measures, process models, decision records, and requirements repositories may provide strong starting evidence. In an immature environment, terminology may differ across teams, ownership may be unclear, data may be unreliable, and critical rules may live only in personal knowledge. The latter situation requires more discovery and careful confidence statements; it does not justify inventing certainty.

Remote, international, and supplier-based work adds further context. Language, time zones, contracts, organizational incentives, access restrictions, and cultural expectations can affect elicitation and decision-making. The analyst designs communication and review so that physical absence or contractual boundaries do not hide disagreement and risk.

### Quality characteristics of business analysis

High-quality analysis is not defined by a single template. It displays a set of observable characteristics.

**Relevant analysis** stays connected to the authorized decision and desired outcome. Interesting information that cannot affect the current decision may be recorded for later rather than expanding scope.

**Evidence-based analysis** distinguishes verified information from testimony, assumptions, estimates, interpretations, and preferences. It makes the basis of important claims examinable.

**Complete-enough analysis** covers the material stakeholders, needs, outcomes, constraints, risks, dependencies, alternatives, and consequences required for the current decision. “Complete” is relative to purpose and lifecycle stage, not a claim that nothing else can be learned.

**Consistent analysis** uses terms, rules, measures, boundaries, and models without hidden contradiction. When sources disagree, the conflict is explicit rather than silently normalized.

**Traceable analysis** connects conclusions and recommendations to evidence and connects approved needs to later requirements, delivery, tests, and outcomes. Traceability supports challenge and change; it is not merely an audit decoration.

**Proportionate analysis** spends effort according to uncertainty, complexity, reversibility, and consequence. A high-impact decision with weak evidence requires more investigation than a low-risk reversible trial.

**Inclusive analysis** considers people who experience the change, including those with less organizational power or less common needs. It does not equate the easiest participants to reach with the whole stakeholder population.

**Actionable analysis** gives decision-makers a clear understanding of options, criteria, implications, uncertainty, and next steps. A large volume of information without a decision structure is not actionable.

**Maintainable analysis** can be updated as evidence and decisions change. Ownership, state, version, and links are clear enough that future participants can determine what remains current.

Quality characteristics can conflict. Increasing detail may improve precision but reduce accessibility for executives. A fast decision may sacrifice evidence depth. Broad participation may extend the timeline. The analyst makes these trade-offs visible and recommends a proportionate approach rather than maximizing one characteristic blindly.

### BA-01-C01-T01 - Definition: deep reference

Business Analysis is the disciplined work of understanding a situation, defining a need, evaluating options, and enabling a change that creates value. It is broader than requirements writing. Requirements are one important output, but analysis also includes discovery, evidence evaluation, problem framing, process understanding, decision support, validation, and benefits thinking. The work can be performed before a project exists, during product discovery, during delivery, after implementation, or as part of continuous improvement. A useful definition must therefore describe both the thinking and the change it enables. A Business Analyst should be able to explain the work to a stakeholder without relying on jargon. The analyst should also state what is not yet known. A definition that makes the role sound like administrative documentation creates incorrect expectations and weakens the value of analysis. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C01-T01 in practice

Real-life scenario. A hospital introducing online appointment booking needs more than a feature list. The analyst studies how patients currently book, where staff spend time, which appointment types are eligible, how identity is verified, and what happens when a slot changes. The analysis connects a patient need to a workable service change. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C01-T02 - Purpose: deep reference

The purpose of Business Analysis is to improve the quality of decisions and change. Analysis makes needs visible, creates shared understanding, reduces avoidable uncertainty, and links work to outcomes. It helps an organization decide whether to act, what to change, what not to change, and how to know whether the change worked. Purpose changes by context. In product discovery the emphasis may be customer value and learning. In regulatory work it may be compliance and traceability. In transformation it may be capability and operating-model change. In delivery it may be clarity, acceptance, and coordination. The underlying purpose remains the same: make change more purposeful, feasible, and verifiable. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C01-T02 in practice

Real-life scenario. A retailer sees abandoned carts increasing. The purpose of analysis is not automatically to request a new checkout screen. The analyst compares customer behavior, payment failures, delivery charges, device types, and support contacts to determine which change could improve completed purchases. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C01-T03 - Value: deep reference

The value of analysis is the improvement created by better understanding and better choices. It can appear as avoided rework, faster decisions, improved adoption, better customer outcomes, reduced risk, stronger compliance, lower cost, or clearer prioritization. The value of analysis should not be judged only by the number of documents produced. Value is credible when the analyst can show a connection among evidence, decision, change, and outcome. For example, a requirements review may prevent an expensive defect, while a process analysis may reveal that automation is not the best answer. Value may be indirect and difficult to isolate, so the analyst should state the contribution and the limits of attribution honestly. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C01-T03 in practice

Real-life scenario. A bank spends weeks clarifying a regulatory reporting change before developers build. That analysis may appear slow, but it prevents incorrect reports, remediation cost, and possible penalties. Its value is partly visible as avoided harm. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C01-T04 - Role boundaries: deep reference

Role boundaries clarify accountability. The Business Analyst may facilitate, analyze, model, recommend, document, and support validation, but the accountable decision-maker still owns the decision. A product owner may own product priority. An architect may own technical architecture. A data owner may own data access and definition. A compliance officer may own a compliance interpretation. Boundaries should be agreed rather than assumed. A responsibility matrix, decision log, or simple role statement can make them visible. Boundary confusion creates gaps and duplicated work. It can also cause the analyst to approve a requirement without authority or cause stakeholders to assume that an analyst has guaranteed a benefit that nobody owns. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C01-T04 in practice

Real-life scenario. A product manager decides which customer problem is most important, an architect decides the target integration pattern, and the analyst clarifies needs, constraints, rules, and acceptance conditions. If the analyst silently makes the architecture decision, accountability becomes unclear. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C01-T05 - Professional mindset: deep reference

The professional mindset combines curiosity, neutrality, empathy, skepticism, clarity, structure, and accountability. Curiosity asks what is really happening. Neutrality prevents premature commitment. Empathy recognizes different experiences. Skepticism tests claims without attacking people. Structure makes complexity manageable. Accountability makes assumptions and limitations visible. The analyst should be comfortable saying “we do not know yet.” This is not weakness; it is a control against false certainty. The analyst should also be willing to revise a conclusion when evidence changes. Professionalism means protecting the quality of the decision process even when a preferred answer is politically attractive. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C01-T05 in practice

Real-life scenario. A senior executive says customers hate the new portal. A professional analyst does not dismiss the statement or accept it as proof. The analyst treats it as an important signal, then checks complaints, completion rates, usability evidence, and customer segments. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C01-T06 - Evidence and judgment: deep reference

Evidence supports or challenges a claim. Judgment interprets evidence and chooses a direction under uncertainty. The two are related but not identical. An interview statement is evidence of a stakeholder’s experience or belief; it may not prove the size or cause of a problem. A dashboard number is evidence of a recorded measure; it may not explain why the number changed. A strong analysis labels facts, observations, interpretations, assumptions, recommendations, and decisions separately. It identifies source, date, reliability, limitations, and conflicting evidence. When judgment is required, the analyst records the criteria used and the risk of being wrong. This makes the decision reviewable rather than presenting opinion as fact. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C01-T06 in practice

Real-life scenario. A call-center manager reports that most delays are caused by a slow database. Logs show that the database is slow only during one batch window, while missing customer information causes delays throughout the day. The analyst separates the manager’s experience, the log evidence, and the conclusion. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.
