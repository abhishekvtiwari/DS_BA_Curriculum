## Purpose

Problem framing turns an unclear concern, opportunity, mandate, or proposed solution into a structured basis for investigation and decision. It does not promise certainty before discovery. It establishes what is known, what is believed, what matters, where the boundaries lie, and what must be learned next.

Weak framing causes teams to solve symptoms, defend preselected solutions, measure activity instead of outcomes, and discover essential constraints too late. Strong framing keeps alternatives open long enough to understand the situation while giving stakeholders enough focus to act. This chapter explains nine connected ideas: situation, symptom, problem, cause, solution, outcome, scope, assumptions, and constraints.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Describe a business situation without prematurely judging it.
- Distinguish observable symptoms from a framed problem.
- Write a neutral, evidence-linked problem statement.
- Treat causes as hypotheses until evidence supports them.
- Separate solution ideas from needs and desired outcomes.
- Define outcomes with useful measures and safeguards.
- Establish scope as a decision boundary rather than a complete description of reality.
- Record, prioritize, and test assumptions.
- Distinguish genuine constraints from preferences and inherited habits.

## The framing chain

Problem framing is iterative. New evidence can change every element.

```text
Situation provides context.
Symptoms signal that attention may be needed.
A problem statement defines the consequential gap.
Cause hypotheses explain why the gap may exist.
Outcomes describe the improvement sought.
Solution options propose ways to influence causes or conditions.
Scope sets the investigation and change boundaries.
Assumptions expose what is provisionally believed.
Constraints define conditions that limit feasible action.
Evidence tests and revises the entire frame.
```

The order is not rigid, but the distinctions matter. A team may hear a proposed solution first, discover a symptom through data, or begin with a regulatory mandate. The analyst moves among the elements while maintaining traceability.

## BA-01-C03-T01 - Situation

### Situation as neutral context

A situation is the relevant set of circumstances in which a need, concern, opportunity, or decision exists. It describes what is happening before the analyst concludes what the problem is or what should change. A situation may include stakeholder goals, current performance, processes, technology, organization structure, policies, history, market conditions, regulation, behavior, events, and prior decisions.

Neutral description is important. “The support team is inefficient” embeds a judgment. “Median response time rose from 6 hours to 14 hours during the last two quarters while ticket volume increased 18 percent” separates observation from interpretation. The second statement can still be incomplete, but it is easier to examine.

### Building a situation overview

An early overview should answer enough of the following questions to guide discovery:

- What triggered attention now?
- Which stakeholders experience or influence the situation?
- What outcomes are they trying to achieve?
- How does the current work or journey operate?
- What evidence describes performance and variation?
- What changed recently, and what remained stable?
- Which systems, policies, suppliers, locations, or channels matter?
- What previous attempts were made and with what results?
- What is uncertain, disputed, or missing?

The overview should be proportionate. Its purpose is orientation, not exhaustive documentation.

### Events, trends, and conditions

An event is a bounded occurrence, such as an outage, audit finding, competitor launch, or regulatory change. A trend is a pattern over time, such as declining retention or rising rework. A condition is an existing state, such as fragmented ownership or limited network access. Analysts identify which type they are observing because each supports different questions.

An event may expose a persistent condition rather than cause it. A sudden failure during peak demand might reveal an older capacity weakness. Conversely, a long trend can result from multiple events or changing measurement. Time order alone does not prove causation.

### Multiple perspectives

There is rarely one complete account of a situation. Customers may experience delay; operations may experience demand volatility; finance may see rising cost; compliance may see control exposure; technology teams may see unsupported infrastructure. These perspectives can all be valid.

The analyst does not force early agreement by deleting disagreement. The analyst records whose perspective is represented, where accounts differ, what evidence supports each, and which decision requires resolution.

### Baselines and comparison

A baseline describes starting performance against which change can be evaluated. Useful baselines define the measure, population, period, source, calculation, and relevant segmentation. A single average may hide seasonal variation, rare severe harm, or unequal outcomes.

Comparison can be historical, target-based, benchmarked, or between groups. Each has limits. Last year may not represent current context. A target may be arbitrary. An external benchmark may use different definitions. Analysts explain the comparison instead of presenting it as self-evident.

## BA-01-C03-T02 - Symptom

### What a symptom is

A symptom is an observable sign that a situation may contain an underlying problem or opportunity. Examples include complaints, missed targets, errors, declining conversion, duplicated work, long queues, workarounds, audit findings, employee turnover, customer abandonment, or unexpected cost.

Symptoms matter because they reveal impact, but they do not automatically identify the cause. Treating the visible sign as the entire problem can lead to temporary relief or displacement.

### Signal, noise, and measurement effects

Not every apparent symptom represents meaningful deterioration. A reported increase in incidents may result from better detection. A drop in call volume may result from customers giving up rather than improved self-service. A rising average handling time may reflect more complex cases after simple cases moved online.

Analysts examine definitions, collection methods, missing data, changes in population, seasonality, and incentives. They seek corroboration across quantitative and qualitative evidence.

### Symptom clusters

Several symptoms may share a cause, and one symptom may have several causes. Late deliveries, refund requests, overtime, and negative reviews may cluster around inventory accuracy, supplier reliability, routing, unrealistic promises, or multiple interacting conditions.

A symptom map can show where signs appear, who experiences them, timing, frequency, severity, and possible connections. It is an organizing aid, not causal proof.

### Impact and urgency

Symptoms can be evaluated by scale, severity, frequency, duration, distribution, reversibility, and risk. A rare safety incident may deserve more urgency than a frequent minor inconvenience. An issue affecting a small vulnerable group may be hidden by aggregate metrics but still be critical.

Urgency should not eliminate disciplined framing. When immediate containment is necessary, the team can stabilize harm while continuing to investigate causes.

### Example: repeated password resets

An organization sees a 40 percent increase in password-reset calls. “Too many reset calls” is a symptom. Possible explanations include a new password policy, confusing messages, failed identity verification, an application defect, phishing-related lockouts, or a reporting change. Buying a chatbot may reduce some contacts but leave account access and security problems unresolved.

## BA-01-C03-T03 - Problem

### Problem as a consequential gap

A problem is a meaningful gap between a current condition and a desired or required condition, creating adverse consequences or preventing value. The gap may involve performance, experience, capability, risk, compliance, equity, cost, or strategic fit.

The word “problem” does not imply blame. It frames a condition that warrants attention. Opportunities can also be framed as gaps between current and attainable value, but analysts should not exaggerate an aspiration into a defect.

### Elements of a strong problem statement

A useful statement identifies:

- The affected stakeholders or system.
- The current condition, supported by available evidence.
- The desired or required condition.
- The consequences of the gap.
- The context and time period.
- Important uncertainty or limitations.

For example: “During the last six months, 23 percent of eligible applicants using the mobile channel abandoned identity verification, compared with 8 percent on desktop. Interviews and support logs indicate difficulty capturing acceptable documents on older devices. This delays access, increases assisted-service demand, and disproportionately affects mobile-only applicants. The contribution of device performance versus message clarity remains uncertain.”

The statement is specific without prescribing a solution.

### Avoiding solution-shaped problems

“We need a new CRM” is a solution proposal. Possible underlying problems include inconsistent customer history, duplicated data entry, weak follow-up, poor reporting, or fragmented ownership. Each might require different action.

Analysts can reframe by asking what outcome the proposed solution is expected to enable, who experiences the current difficulty, what evidence shows it, and what would still be wrong if the requested tool were delivered.

### Problem ownership and system boundaries

A person who reports a problem does not necessarily own every decision needed to address it. The condition may cross departments, suppliers, policies, and systems. Analysts clarify sponsors, affected stakeholders, operational ownership, decision rights, and escalation.

Framing should avoid blaming individuals for behavior shaped by workload, incentives, unclear rules, inadequate tools, or conflicting targets. Human error can be real, but it is not an adequate stopping point when system conditions make the error likely.

### Problem statement as a living control

The statement is versioned as evidence changes. Revisions should be visible, with reasons recorded. A useful statement narrows inquiry while remaining open to correction. It is not a slogan frozen at project approval.

## BA-01-C03-T04 - Cause

### Cause as an explanatory relationship

A cause is a factor or condition that contributes to an observed effect. In complex organizations, outcomes usually arise from interacting causes rather than a single root. Analysts should use language such as “contributing factor,” “causal hypothesis,” and “enabling condition” when evidence does not justify certainty.

Correlation, sequence, and stakeholder confidence do not prove causation. A factor can precede a symptom without producing it. Two measures can move together because both respond to another condition.

### Cause levels

It is useful to distinguish:

- **Immediate cause:** directly precedes the event or failure.
- **Contributing condition:** increases likelihood or impact.
- **Systemic cause:** arises from design, governance, incentives, capability, or environment.
- **Trigger:** initiates an event in an already vulnerable system.
- **Protective factor:** reduces likelihood or impact.

For an incorrect payment, the immediate cause might be an invalid rate applied by a rule. Contributing conditions might include unclear ownership and weak test coverage. A trigger might be a policy update. A protective control might be post-payment reconciliation.

### Techniques and their limits

Five Whys can encourage deeper questioning but can falsely imply one linear cause. Fishbone diagrams organize hypotheses by categories but do not validate them. Process maps locate failure points but may omit incentives and external context. Pareto charts prioritize frequent categories but not necessarily causes or severity.

The technique does not create evidence. Analysts combine methods and seek logs, observation, experiments, comparisons, document review, specialist analysis, and stakeholder knowledge.

### Testing cause hypotheses

A cause hypothesis should state what relationship is expected and what observation would weaken it. Analysts look for alternative explanations, negative cases, timing, mechanism, dose or exposure patterns, and consistency across sources. Where controlled experiments are unsafe or impossible, they use the strongest feasible evidence and state limitations.

### Stopping rules

Analysis can continue indefinitely. A practical stopping rule asks whether the team understands enough to compare interventions, whether additional evidence could materially change a decision, and whether risk justifies deeper investigation. Safety, legal, financial, or irreversible consequences require stronger evidence than a low-cost reversible trial.

## BA-01-C03-T05 - Solution

### Solution as one response to a need

A solution is a particular way of addressing a need or influencing causes and conditions to produce desired outcomes. It may involve process, policy, roles, skills, information, technology, organization design, communication, supplier arrangements, or deliberate non-action.

A solution is not proven valuable because it is popular, modern, requested by a senior stakeholder, or successful elsewhere. Its suitability depends on need, context, evidence, feasibility, risk, cost, and stakeholder consequences.

### Divergence before convergence

Early analysis should allow multiple options. Divergence explores different intervention points and approaches. Convergence compares them using agreed criteria and evidence. Premature convergence produces confirmation bias; endless divergence prevents decisions.

Options may include improving the current arrangement, removing unnecessary work, changing a rule, strengthening a capability, buying or building technology, partnering, piloting, or stopping an activity. A credible “do minimum” or “do nothing” option provides a baseline and exposes the cost of inaction.

### Solution requirements versus problem evidence

Detailed requirements should follow enough problem understanding to justify investment. Teams can prototype or test early, but they should remain clear whether an artifact explores a hypothesis or commits to a design.

Traceability should link solution features to needs, cause hypotheses, outcomes, constraints, risks, and measures. A feature without a credible link may be unnecessary even if stakeholders like it.

### Feasibility and unintended consequences

Analysis considers operational, technical, financial, legal, ethical, schedule, and organizational feasibility. It also examines how stakeholders may adapt. A tighter control may create workarounds. Automation may shift exceptions to already overloaded staff. Faster service may increase demand beyond capacity.

### Recommendation and authorization

The analyst may recommend an option based on evidence and criteria, but the authorized decision-maker owns the choice. The recommendation should state alternatives considered, assumptions, expected outcomes, costs, risks, confidence, and conditions for review.

## BA-01-C03-T06 - Outcome

### Outcome versus output

An output is produced by work; an outcome is a resulting change in condition, behavior, performance, or experience. A new form is an output. Fewer incomplete applications without excluding users is an outcome. Completing training is an output. Safer and more consistent decisions are outcomes.

Outcome framing prevents delivery activity from becoming the definition of success.

### Outcome statements

A strong outcome statement specifies who or what should experience change, the direction of improvement, relevant context, expected time horizon, and how success will be judged. It avoids pretending that uncertain targets are facts.

For example: “Within six months of rollout, eligible mobile applicants should complete identity verification at a rate comparable to desktop users, while fraud loss, assisted-service demand, accessibility failures, and false rejection do not materially worsen.”

### Measures, baselines, and safeguards

Measures need operational definitions, reliable sources, segmentation, ownership, frequency, and baselines. Targets should reflect desired value and realistic uncertainty.

Safeguard measures detect harm created while optimizing a primary result. Reducing call duration may worsen first-contact resolution. Increasing approval speed may increase error or fraud. Analysts pair speed, cost, or adoption measures with quality, risk, inclusion, and sustainability.

### Leading and lagging indicators

Leading indicators change earlier and may predict progress, such as successful completion of a critical step. Lagging indicators confirm later results, such as annual retention or realized savings. A leading indicator is useful only when evidence supports its connection to the outcome.

### Benefits and attribution

Outcomes may result from several changes or external forces. Analysts distinguish contribution from proof of sole attribution. They establish baselines, record concurrent changes, segment results, and define review periods. Benefits should have owners after delivery; otherwise measurement often stops at launch.

## BA-01-C03-T07 - Scope

### Scope as a deliberate boundary

Scope defines what an investigation, decision, change, product, or release includes and excludes. It creates focus and sets expectations. It does not imply that out-of-scope factors are irrelevant; dependencies and consequences may cross the boundary.

Scope can describe stakeholders, outcomes, capabilities, processes, locations, channels, products, data, systems, time periods, decisions, and organizational units. “Improve onboarding” is too ambiguous until these dimensions are clarified.

### Problem space and solution space

Problem scope defines what the team will understand. Solution scope defines the capabilities and changes included in a proposed response. Delivery scope defines what a particular initiative or release will implement. Confusing them can exclude necessary discovery because a solution boundary was chosen too early.

### In scope, out of scope, and interfaces

An effective scope statement lists inclusions, exclusions, and interfaces. Interfaces describe where in-scope work depends on or affects out-of-scope elements. If payment processing is excluded from a checkout change but payment status determines order completion, the interface remains analytically important.

### Scope change

New evidence may justify expansion, reduction, or reframing. Scope change is not automatically failure. Uncontrolled change is the risk. Analysts record the proposed change, reason, impact, decision owner, and implications for outcomes, resources, risk, and schedule.

### Avoiding artificial boundaries

Departmental ownership, funding lines, and system architecture can create convenient but misleading boundaries. Customer journeys and value streams often cross them. The analyst highlights when the selected scope prevents an end-to-end outcome from being understood or achieved.

## BA-01-C03-T08 - Assumptions

### What an assumption is

An assumption is a condition provisionally treated as true for analysis, planning, or decision-making despite incomplete proof. Assumptions are unavoidable. Hidden assumptions are dangerous because they influence choices without being examined.

Examples include expected demand, user access to a channel, availability of staff, accuracy of source data, willingness of a partner, approval of a policy change, or belief that a feature will alter behavior.

### Assumption versus fact, hypothesis, and dependency

A fact is supported to a defined level by evidence. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation or prediction. An assumption is temporarily accepted so work can proceed. A dependency is a required relationship or external condition. One statement may move among categories as evidence and commitments change.

Analysts label the category, evidence, owner, and status instead of using “known” casually.

### An assumption register

A useful register records:

- A clear, testable statement.
- Why it matters and which decision relies on it.
- Existing evidence and confidence.
- Impact if false.
- Test or validation method.
- Owner and review date.
- Current status and resulting decision.

The register should drive action rather than become an archive.

### Prioritizing assumptions

Analysts prioritize assumptions by uncertainty and consequence. A highly uncertain belief with little impact may be monitored. A plausible but catastrophic failure may require specialist review or safeguards. Assumptions that determine whether the solution is desirable, feasible, viable, lawful, safe, or inclusive should be tested early.

### Learning and revision

Invalidating an assumption is useful learning, not an analytical embarrassment. Teams should create psychological safety for reporting disconfirming evidence. Decisions must be revisited when a critical assumption changes.

## BA-01-C03-T09 - Constraints

### Constraint as a limiting condition

A constraint is a condition that limits available choices, timing, resources, design, or operation. Constraints may be legal, regulatory, contractual, ethical, financial, technical, physical, organizational, environmental, schedule-based, or capability-related.

Constraints shape feasible options but do not automatically dictate one solution. “Data must remain within an approved jurisdiction” is a constraint. “We must use Vendor X” may be a contract constraint, a policy, a preference, or an unsupported assumption; the analyst verifies which.

### Hard and soft constraints

A hard constraint cannot be changed within the relevant authority or timeframe, such as a statutory deadline or physical safety limit. A soft constraint can potentially be negotiated, funded, redesigned, or escalated, such as an internal procedure or provisional budget allocation.

Hardness is contextual. A contract may be fixed for the current release but renegotiable for a later operating model. Analysts record who confirmed the constraint, its source, duration, and change mechanism.

### Constraint versus requirement and risk

A constraint limits the solution space. A requirement states a necessary capability or condition. A risk is an uncertain event or condition that may affect objectives. A fixed legal retention period may create a constraint and generate requirements. Uncertainty about pending legislation is a risk. Clear classification improves decisions.

### Challenging inherited constraints responsibly

Some constraints are organizational habits expressed as absolutes: “security will never approve cloud,” “customers will only call,” or “the process must remain unchanged.” Analysts challenge these through evidence and the proper authority, not by ignoring valid controls.

### Designing within constraints

Constraints can stimulate better options when made explicit. The analyst evaluates interactions: a fixed deadline may require reduced scope; a limited skill base may require training or managed service; accessibility obligations may change channel design; data-quality limits may restrict automation. Trade-offs should be authorized and traceable.

## Integrated conceptual example

A retailer asks for artificial intelligence to forecast store staffing. The **situation** includes volatile demand, local events, scheduling rules, absenteeism, and high manager workload. **Symptoms** include overtime, understaffed periods, queues, and unused labor hours. The **problem** is an inconsistent match between available skills and time-specific demand, harming service and cost.

Possible **causes** include weak demand signals, late schedule publication, rigid contracts, inaccurate availability data, local overrides, and promotion changes. An AI forecast is one **solution** option, not the problem definition. Desired **outcomes** include better service coverage, less avoidable overtime, fair schedules, and manageable planning effort, with safeguards for employee wellbeing and discrimination.

The **scope** may include selected store types, scheduling decisions, relevant data, and manager workflows while excluding payroll replacement. **Assumptions** include adequate historical data and managers acting on recommendations. **Constraints** include labor law, union agreements, privacy, skill certifications, and a fixed pilot window. The frame supports a decision about what to investigate and test without promising that AI is the answer.

## Common misconceptions

### “The first request is the problem”

Requests often express a preferred response. Analysts trace them to stakeholders, evidence, consequences, and desired outcomes.

### “A symptom is only noise until the root cause is known”

Symptoms provide important evidence and may require immediate containment. The mistake is treating them as complete causal explanations.

### “Every problem has one root cause”

Complex organizational outcomes commonly emerge from interacting conditions, triggers, controls, and behavior.

### “Good analysts remain solution-free until discovery ends”

Solution ideas can help learning through comparison and prototypes. The discipline is to treat them as hypotheses rather than commitments.

### “Scope should never change”

Evidence can justify controlled scope change. Stability without learning is not quality.

### “Assumptions are weaknesses to hide”

Assumptions are inevitable. Explicit, prioritized, testable assumptions improve decisions.

### “Constraints are whatever stakeholders say cannot change”

Constraints need sources, authority, duration, and classification. Some are preferences or inherited habits.

## Advanced synthesis

### Framing quality characteristics

A high-quality frame is neutral enough to permit alternatives, specific enough to guide discovery, evidence-linked, outcome-oriented, bounded, transparent about uncertainty, inclusive of affected perspectives, and revisable. It preserves a chain from observed situation to authorized action.

### Problem framing as risk reduction

Framing reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, optimizing one department at another’s expense, excluding important users, discovering infeasibility late, and declaring success at delivery. The appropriate depth depends on consequence and reversibility. A low-cost reversible change can be tested quickly; a safety-critical or irreversible decision requires stronger evidence and specialist authority.

### A framing review

Before moving to detailed requirements, ask:

- Is the current situation described without unsupported blame?
- Are symptoms supported by evidence and appropriately segmented?
- Does the problem statement show a consequential gap without prescribing a solution?
- Are cause statements labeled and tested as hypotheses?
- Were multiple solution options considered, including non-technology choices?
- Are outcomes measurable and protected by safeguards?
- Are problem, solution, and delivery scopes distinguished?
- Are critical assumptions owned, prioritized, and testable?
- Are constraints verified, classified, and connected to authority?
- Is the frame versioned and traceable to the next decision?
