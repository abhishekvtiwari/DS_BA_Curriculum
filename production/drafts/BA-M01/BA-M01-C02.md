## Purpose

A business analyst cannot understand a proposed change by studying requirements in isolation. Every requirement exists inside an organization that serves people, offers products or services, divides work among functions, operates through a network of capabilities and processes, and expects some form of value. Business and organizational literacy is the ability to read that system well enough to ask relevant questions and connect local changes to wider outcomes.

This chapter provides a practical vocabulary for that work. It explains business models, customers, products, services, functions, operating models, and value creation. The purpose is not to turn the learner into a strategist, marketer, accountant, or organization designer. It is to help the learner recognize how the major parts of an organization fit together, where assumptions hide, and how a change in one part can create consequences elsewhere.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Explain how an organization creates, delivers, and sustains value through a business model.
- Distinguish customers, users, buyers, beneficiaries, sponsors, and other stakeholders.
- Separate products from services while recognizing that many offerings combine both.
- Describe common organizational functions without assuming that every organization uses the same structure.
- Explain an operating model as the coordinated arrangement used to execute strategy.
- Trace value from stakeholder need through organizational activity to measurable outcomes.
- Identify cross-functional dependencies, tensions, assumptions, and boundary risks relevant to change.

## A system view of an organization

An organization is a coordinated social system. It combines people, decisions, information, capabilities, processes, technology, assets, relationships, policies, and money to pursue outcomes. The organization may be commercial, public, charitable, cooperative, professional, or community-based. Its definition of success may emphasize profit, public value, safety, mission, access, resilience, learning, or a combination.

A useful high-level view is:

```text
Purpose and strategy guide choices.
A business model explains the logic of value exchange and sustainability.
An operating model arranges capabilities, people, processes, information, and technology.
Functions provide specialized responsibility and expertise.
Products and services enable outcomes for customers and other stakeholders.
Evidence shows whether value was actually created, delivered, captured, or lost.
```

These elements are connected but not interchangeable. A strategy describes choices and direction. A business model describes the logic by which an organization serves stakeholders and remains viable. An operating model describes how the organization is arranged to execute. A process describes a flow of work. A capability describes an ability the organization must possess. An organization chart describes reporting relationships. Treating these as synonyms produces weak analysis.

## BA-01-C02-T01 - Business models

### What a business model explains

A business model explains how an organization creates value for selected stakeholders, delivers that value through an offering and operating arrangement, and sustains the activity economically or institutionally. In a commercial setting, sustainability normally includes revenue and cost. In government or nonprofit settings, it may include appropriations, grants, donations, mandates, public legitimacy, and responsible use of resources.

A business model is not merely a pricing plan, a process map, an organization chart, or a statement that the organization will use a particular technology. It is a set of connected choices and hypotheses. It should make clear whom the organization serves, which problems or aspirations matter, what it offers, how stakeholders reach and experience it, what resources and activities are necessary, which partners matter, what costs arise, and how the organization receives the resources required to continue.

### A nine-part lens

The Business Model Canvas provides one widely used lens with nine connected components:

- **Customer segments:** distinct groups the organization chooses to serve.
- **Value propositions:** the benefits or outcomes offered to those groups.
- **Channels:** how the organization communicates, delivers, sells, or provides access.
- **Customer relationships:** how relationships are established, supported, and retained.
- **Revenue streams:** how financial value is received from each segment.
- **Key resources:** assets required to make the model work.
- **Key activities:** essential work performed to create and deliver the offering.
- **Key partnerships:** external relationships that provide resources, activities, access, or risk sharing.
- **Cost structure:** the major costs created by the model.

The canvas is useful because it exposes dependencies. Changing a customer segment can require a different value proposition, channel, support model, partner, and cost structure. Lowering a price may increase demand but also change service capacity, cash flow, support cost, and perceived positioning. A business analyst uses the model as a structured set of questions, not as proof that the answers are correct.

### Business models are testable claims

A business model contains assumptions about behavior and causality: a segment has a need; the offering addresses it; the stakeholder will adopt or fund it; the chosen channel can reach them; delivery is feasible; the economics or mandate are sustainable. Evidence may disprove any link.

Analysts should date and version business-model descriptions, record important assumptions, and distinguish an existing model from a proposed one. They should ask which elements are observed, which are inferred, and which are aspirations. A polished diagram can conceal weak evidence.

### Multiple models in one organization

Large organizations often operate more than one business model. A software firm may sell subscriptions directly, license technology through partners, and provide professional services. A university may combine tuition, research funding, grants, donations, and public obligations. A platform may serve two or more interdependent groups, such as buyers and sellers. Analysis must identify the model relevant to the change instead of describing the organization as though it served everyone in the same way.

### Example: changing the channel

Suppose an insurer proposes moving claims entirely from telephone support to a mobile application. The request appears to be a channel change. Business-model analysis reveals wider questions: which customer groups can use the channel, whether trust changes, what happens to complex claims, whether call-center demand truly falls, how fraud controls change, and whether app development and support costs exceed anticipated savings. The analyst connects the proposed feature to the entire value logic.

## BA-01-C02-T02 - Customers

### Customer is not a universal synonym

A customer is generally a person or organization that selects, receives, buys, funds, or maintains a relationship with an offering. The exact meaning depends on context. In a simple retail purchase, the buyer, payer, user, and beneficiary may be one person. In enterprise software, procurement may buy, executives may sponsor, administrators may configure, employees may use, and customers of the purchasing company may experience the consequences.

Useful distinctions include:

- **Buyer:** selects or contracts for the offering.
- **Payer or funder:** provides the money or budget.
- **User:** directly interacts with the product or service.
- **Beneficiary:** receives an intended benefit, even without direct interaction.
- **Sponsor:** authorizes or champions an initiative.
- **Client:** receives professional or contracted services.
- **Consumer:** uses or consumes an offering, often in a market context.
- **Citizen or service user:** interacts with a public service and may have legal rights rather than market choice.
- **Affected party:** experiences a consequence without being a chosen target or direct user.

These roles can overlap, but analysts should not merge them without evidence. Each may define value differently and hold different power.

### Segmentation with purpose

Segmentation groups customers or users by characteristics that matter to a decision. Possible bases include needs, behaviors, circumstances, risk, value sought, frequency of use, geography, organization size, accessibility requirements, channel preference, or stage in a relationship.

Good segmentation is actionable and relevant. Age alone may be a poor segment if it does not explain the behavior or need under study. A segment such as “users who cannot reliably receive a one-time password while traveling” may be more useful for authentication analysis than a broad demographic category. Segments should be treated as analytical models, not stereotypes.

### Needs, wants, requests, and outcomes

A request is what someone asks for. A need is a problem, opportunity, or condition that may justify action. A want is a preferred response. An outcome is the result the person is trying to achieve. These are connected but different.

For example, “send me a weekly spreadsheet” is a request. The need may be early awareness of inventory shortages. The desired outcome may be enough time to rebalance stock before orders fail. If the analyst records only the spreadsheet request, the team may automate a report without improving the decision.

### Understanding the whole experience

Customers experience an organization across a journey, not according to internal departmental boundaries. A person may discover an offering through marketing, purchase through sales, receive delivery from operations, seek help from support, dispute a charge with finance, and exercise privacy rights through a compliance process. A failure at any handoff affects the perceived offering.

Analysts therefore examine channels, touchpoints, waiting, handoffs, information repetition, accessibility, exceptions, emotional peaks, recovery after failure, and the parts of a journey outside the organization’s direct control. They also seek evidence from a varied set of people, including those who abandon the journey or need support.

### Customer evidence and ethics

Customer knowledge may come from observation, interviews, surveys, analytics, complaints, support logs, transaction data, experiments, and frontline staff. Every method has limitations. High-volume behavior data may show what happened but not why. Interviews may explain experience but cannot automatically estimate prevalence. Complaints reveal serious friction but not the views of silent users.

Ethical analysis respects consent, privacy, accessibility, fair representation, and possible harm. The analyst should identify who is absent from the evidence and who may be disadvantaged by optimization for the majority.

## BA-01-C02-T03 - Products

### Product as a managed value proposition

A product is an offering or coherent set of capabilities managed through a lifecycle to enable outcomes for a defined group. It may be physical, digital, informational, financial, or hybrid. Products are more than a list of features. They normally have a purpose, users or customers, boundaries, ownership, lifecycle decisions, quality expectations, costs, risks, and measures of performance.

A product can exist at several levels. A company may describe a broad product family, a platform, an application, a module, or a specific paid plan as a product. Analysts must clarify the unit of analysis. Otherwise stakeholders may use the same word while referring to different boundaries.

### Product lifecycle

Products move through states such as exploration, introduction, growth, maturity, renewal, consolidation, and retirement. The labels vary, but the underlying principle is stable: decisions change over time.

Early work emphasizes validating needs, desirability, feasibility, and viability. Growth may emphasize adoption, capacity, reliability, and differentiation. Mature products may emphasize efficiency, retention, compliance, modernization, and portfolio fit. Retirement requires migration, communication, data retention, contractual handling, support, and decommissioning. A requirement that makes sense during rapid growth may be wasteful or risky during retirement.

### Product outcomes and outputs

An output is something produced, such as a feature, report, release, or device. An outcome is a resulting change in behavior, condition, performance, or experience. Shipping a recommendation engine is an output. Increasing successful discovery of relevant items without creating unfair exclusion is an outcome.

Product literacy helps the analyst connect requirements to outcomes. This involves identifying the target behavior, baseline, success measure, risks, leading and lagging indicators, and assumptions connecting the output to the result. Adoption alone does not prove value if the product increases errors, cost, harm, or support demand.

### Product boundaries and dependencies

A product depends on capabilities and services around it: identity, payments, data, infrastructure, customer support, legal agreements, training, distribution, security, and vendor relationships. Some dependencies are shared platforms; others are external. A local product decision can create enterprise-wide consequences.

The analyst should document ownership and service expectations at boundaries. If a product relies on customer data from another system, questions include data meaning, quality, consent, latency, availability, correction, lineage, and responsibility when the data is wrong.

### Product portfolio thinking

Organizations manage groups of products. Two products may compete, share technology, serve overlapping segments, or exist because of past acquisitions. Improving one in isolation can duplicate capabilities or shift cost elsewhere. Portfolio awareness helps analysts ask whether to enhance, combine, standardize, replace, partner, or retire rather than assuming every product should grow.

## BA-01-C02-T04 - Services

### Service as outcome enablement

A service is a coordinated means of helping users achieve outcomes through interactions, work, information, and supporting resources. Services unfold over time. They may include people, digital channels, physical locations, policies, communications, decisions, suppliers, and backstage operations.

A service is not identical to its interface. An online application form is one touchpoint in a service that may also include eligibility rules, identity evidence, review, notification, payment, appeal, support, and record management. Improving the form while ignoring delayed review may not improve the overall outcome.

### Products and services together

The boundary between product and service is often porous. A physical product may include installation, financing, warranties, maintenance, software, and support. A digital product may be consumed as an ongoing service. A professional service may use a productized method or platform.

The distinction remains useful because it changes analytical attention. Product analysis often emphasizes lifecycle, reusable capabilities, feature choices, adoption, and portfolio position. Service analysis often emphasizes end-to-end journeys, demand, capacity, queues, handoffs, service levels, variability, recovery, and co-production with the user. Many situations require both views.

### Frontstage and backstage

Frontstage activities are visible to the user, such as a conversation, screen, delivery, or notification. Backstage activities support the experience but may be invisible, such as verification, routing, reconciliation, scheduling, risk review, or data synchronization. Supporting processes and systems may sit further behind both.

Service problems often originate backstage and appear frontstage. An unclear status message may reflect fragmented ownership or inconsistent source data rather than a wording problem. A service blueprint can connect user steps, frontstage interactions, backstage work, supporting systems, evidence, and failure points.

### Demand, capacity, and variability

Service performance depends on the relationship between demand and capacity. Average demand can conceal peaks. Average handling time can conceal difficult cases. Increasing utilization toward 100 percent can create long queues when work arrives unevenly or varies in effort.

Analysts consider demand type, arrival patterns, case complexity, skills, scheduling, service levels, prioritization, rework, failure demand, and escalation. “Failure demand” is demand created because the service did not work correctly or clearly the first time, such as repeated calls about an unexplained delay. Reducing it can improve both user outcomes and cost.

### Service recovery

No service is perfect. Mature analysis considers how failures are detected, explained, corrected, compensated, learned from, and prevented from recurring. Recovery is especially important where users are vulnerable, deadlines matter, or errors affect safety, rights, finances, or access.

## BA-01-C02-T05 - Functions

### What an organizational function is

A function is a grouping of related responsibilities and expertise that the organization needs to perform. Common examples include finance, operations, sales, marketing, human resources, procurement, legal, risk, compliance, technology, data, customer support, and product management.

Functions provide specialization, standards, career communities, controls, and accountability. However, functional names and boundaries vary. “Operations” in a logistics company differs from operations in a bank. A small organization may combine responsibilities; a large organization may distribute them across regions, business units, shared services, and centers of excellence.

### Function, capability, process, and team

These concepts should be separated:

- A **function** groups responsibility or expertise.
- A **capability** is an ability the organization must possess, such as fraud detection or order fulfillment.
- A **process** is a repeatable flow of activities that produces an output or outcome.
- A **team** is a group of people organized to perform work.
- A **role** is a set of expected responsibilities assigned to a person or position.

A capability can require several functions. A process can cross multiple teams. One function may own standards while another performs daily work. An organization chart alone cannot explain how value flows.

### Functional perspectives and tensions

Different functions legitimately optimize different concerns. Sales may prioritize responsiveness and growth. Operations may prioritize stability and throughput. Finance may prioritize control and sustainable economics. Legal and compliance may prioritize obligations and defensibility. Security may prioritize exposure reduction. Product may prioritize outcomes and coherent evolution.

Conflict does not automatically mean one function is obstructive. It may reveal a real trade-off or a missing enterprise decision. The analyst helps make the perspectives, evidence, consequences, and decision rights visible. The analyst should not silently resolve a conflict beyond delegated authority.

### Cross-functional work and handoffs

Most customer outcomes cross functions. Handoffs introduce delay, information loss, duplicated checks, unclear ownership, and local optimization. A department may meet its own target while the end-to-end outcome worsens.

Analysts trace work across boundaries and examine input quality, entry and exit criteria, queues, rework, exceptions, control points, system ownership, and feedback. Measures should include end-to-end outcomes as well as local performance.

### Governance and decision rights

Governance defines how direction, accountability, oversight, and decisions are exercised. Useful questions include who recommends, who approves, who must be consulted, who performs, who bears risk, and who can stop or escalate work. A responsibility matrix can clarify participation, but it does not replace discussion of actual authority and incentives.

## BA-01-C02-T06 - Operating models

### From strategy to execution

An operating model is the coordinated arrangement through which an organization executes its strategy. It explains how capabilities, processes, people, organization structures, information, technology, locations, partners, governance, and performance management work together.

An operating model is broader than a process map and more operational than a business model. The business model explains the logic of serving stakeholders and sustaining the organization. The operating model explains how the organization is configured to make that logic real.

### Core design dimensions

Analysts commonly examine:

- **Capabilities:** what the organization must be able to do.
- **Processes and journeys:** how work and value flow end to end.
- **Organization and people:** structures, roles, skills, capacity, incentives, and culture.
- **Information and data:** what is needed, where it originates, how it is governed, and how it supports decisions.
- **Technology:** applications, platforms, infrastructure, integration, automation, and technical constraints.
- **Locations and channels:** where work occurs and how stakeholders interact.
- **Partners and sourcing:** which activities or resources depend on external parties.
- **Governance and controls:** how decisions, risk, compliance, and accountability operate.
- **Performance management:** how outcomes, service, health, and improvement are measured.

The dimensions should be designed as a system. Automating a process without changing roles, skills, controls, data, and exception handling may transfer work rather than remove it.

### Current, future, and transition states

Current-state analysis explains how work operates now, including variations and workarounds. Future-state analysis describes the capabilities and conditions needed to achieve desired outcomes. The transition state explains how the organization moves between them while continuing to operate.

A future-state diagram is not a delivery plan. Analysts identify gaps, dependencies, sequencing, temporary processes, migration, training, communications, data conversion, parallel operation, control changes, and retirement of legacy arrangements. Transition risk may exceed the steady-state risk.

### Centralization, decentralization, and standardization

Operating models make choices about where decisions and work sit. Centralization can improve consistency, scale, control, and shared expertise. Decentralization can improve local responsiveness, context sensitivity, and autonomy. Standardization can simplify integration and measurement but may suppress necessary variation.

There is rarely one universal answer. The analyst clarifies which activities require uniformity, which require local adaptation, what coordination cost each design creates, and what evidence supports the choice.

### Formal model versus lived reality

Policies and diagrams show intended operation. Actual work may include spreadsheets, informal approvals, shadow systems, personal networks, duplicated records, manual reconciliations, and exceptions known only to experienced staff. These are not automatically bad; some are adaptive responses to gaps. Analysts compare documented and observed operation without blaming individuals for systemic conditions.

## BA-01-C02-T07 - Value creation

### Value is produced through a chain of conditions

Value creation is the process by which resources and activities enable outcomes considered worthwhile by stakeholders. It is not guaranteed by completing work or delivering an output.

```text
Resources enable activities.
Activities produce outputs.
Outputs support stakeholder behaviors or changes.
Those changes contribute to outcomes.
Outcomes create, protect, transfer, or destroy value.
Context and unintended effects influence every link.
```

This chain contains assumptions. Training may produce completed sessions but not improved performance. A dashboard may provide information but not change decisions. Faster approval may improve customer experience but weaken a necessary control. Analysts make the causal links explicit and test them.

### Creating, delivering, capturing, and protecting value

**Value creation** produces a potential benefit or useful capability. **Value delivery** makes it accessible and usable by the intended stakeholder. **Value capture** allows the organization or another stakeholder to retain part of the benefit, such as revenue, learning, legitimacy, or capacity. **Value protection** avoids loss through safety, compliance, security, resilience, quality, or trust.

An organization can create value for users but fail to capture enough resources to sustain the service. It can capture revenue while shifting costs or harm to employees, communities, or future operations. Strong analysis presents the distribution of value, cost, risk, and harm rather than a single total.

### Value streams and end-to-end flow

A value stream is an end-to-end set of activities through which a stakeholder receives value. It crosses functional and system boundaries. Looking at the stream helps expose waiting, rework, bottlenecks, unnecessary approvals, lost information, and measures that reward local performance at the expense of the whole.

The analyst begins with a triggering need and follows the work to a meaningful outcome. The purpose is not merely to draw boxes. It is to understand where value is added, delayed, diluted, protected, or destroyed.

### Measures and balanced evidence

Measures should connect operational activity to stakeholder and organizational outcomes. A balanced set may include:

- **Outcome measures:** whether the intended condition improved.
- **Experience measures:** whether stakeholders can complete their goals effectively and fairly.
- **Operational measures:** time, quality, capacity, reliability, error, and rework.
- **Financial or resource measures:** cost, revenue, margin, funding use, or avoided loss.
- **Risk and control measures:** exposure, incidents, compliance, resilience, and recovery.
- **Learning measures:** whether critical assumptions were tested and uncertainty reduced.

Metrics require definitions, ownership, baselines, targets, frequency, segmentation, and interpretation. A target can cause distortion when people optimize the measure instead of the underlying purpose. Analysts watch for gaming, exclusion, averages that conceal harmed groups, and improvements caused by unrelated factors.

### Trade-offs and value conflict

Stakeholders may value speed, assurance, affordability, privacy, choice, consistency, personalization, employee sustainability, and profitability differently. Some tensions can be improved through better design; others require an explicit decision.

A disciplined value discussion identifies who benefits, who pays, who carries risk, the timing of benefits and costs, reversibility, uncertainty, and non-negotiable obligations. The analyst supports comparison but leaves authorization with the proper decision-maker.

### Worked conceptual example

A hospital proposes self-service appointment scheduling. The intended outcomes are easier access and reduced call-center workload. Business-model questions address funding and which patient groups are served. Customer analysis distinguishes patients, caregivers, clinicians, administrators, and payers. Product analysis considers the scheduling platform and its lifecycle. Service analysis follows the full journey from referral through reminders, attendance, rescheduling, and support. Functional analysis exposes dependencies among clinical operations, reception, IT, privacy, finance, and accessibility. Operating-model analysis addresses roles, data, integration, exception handling, and governance.

Value is not proven by launching the portal. Evidence must show whether patients can obtain appropriate appointments, whether underserved groups are excluded, whether no-shows change, whether call demand falls or merely shifts, whether clinical capacity is used safely, and whether staff workloads improve. Organizational literacy turns a narrow feature request into a coherent analysis of change.

## Common misconceptions

### “A business model is a process map”

False. A process map describes how work flows. A business model describes the connected logic of stakeholders, offerings, delivery, resources, partners, costs, and sustainability. Processes help execute the model.

### “The customer is always the user”

False. Buyer, payer, user, beneficiary, sponsor, and affected party may be different. Merging them can hide conflicting needs and power.

### “Products are tangible and services are intangible”

This distinction is too simple for modern organizations. Digital products, physical products with ongoing services, and service platforms combine both. The useful question is which analytical lens reveals lifecycle, experience, capacity, and outcome risks.

### “Functions show how value flows”

Functions show specialization and responsibility. Value usually flows across them. An organization chart cannot replace end-to-end process, journey, capability, or value-stream analysis.

### “The operating model is the technology architecture”

Technology is one dimension. People, processes, information, locations, partners, governance, controls, and performance management are equally relevant.

### “Delivering the requested output creates value”

An output creates only potential. Stakeholder adoption, behavior, operating conditions, side effects, and sustained outcomes determine realized value.

## Advanced synthesis

### Reading an organization at three levels

At the **strategic level**, analysts examine purpose, environment, business models, stakeholder groups, capabilities, portfolios, and major choices. At the **operating level**, they examine value streams, functions, capacity, governance, information, technology, and performance. At the **change level**, they examine a particular need, scope, solution, transition, and outcome.

The levels constrain and inform each other. A local improvement may conflict with strategy, duplicate an enterprise capability, or create costs in another function. Conversely, strategy that cannot be translated into operating choices is only aspiration. The analyst moves between levels and makes the links traceable.

### Boundary analysis

Important problems often sit at boundaries: between customer and organization, product and shared platform, frontstage and backstage, one function and another, internal operation and supplier, or current and future state. Boundaries are where ownership becomes ambiguous and information changes meaning.

Boundary analysis asks what crosses, in what form, under whose responsibility, to what standard, with what delay, and what happens when it fails. It examines contracts, interfaces, data definitions, decision rights, service expectations, escalation, and feedback.

### A literacy checklist for early discovery

Before accepting a change request, an analyst should be able to ask:

- Which business model and stakeholder segment are in scope?
- Who buys, pays, uses, benefits, sponsors, and may be harmed?
- Is the offering best understood as a product, service, or combination?
- Which functions, capabilities, processes, and teams contribute?
- How does the current operating model actually work?
- What outcome represents value, and for whom?
- What costs, constraints, risks, and dependencies shape the situation?
- What evidence supports the causal link from proposed change to outcome?
- Which decisions require authority outside the analysis role?

The analyst does not need every answer immediately. The checklist reveals uncertainty and directs further discovery.

### BA-01-C02-T01 - Business models: deep reference

A business model explains how an organization creates, delivers, and sustains value. It includes customers or beneficiaries, value proposition, channels, relationships, activities, resources, partners, costs, revenue or funding, and governance. Different sectors use different mechanisms. A subscription business, public service, marketplace, manufacturer, and nonprofit may all create value differently. Business-model literacy helps an analyst assess second-order effects. A proposed discount may increase volume but reduce margin. A self-service channel may reduce support cost but create accessibility or trust problems. An AI feature may improve speed while introducing review cost and risk. Analysis should connect a requested change to the model rather than judging the feature in isolation. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C02-T01 in practice

Real-life scenario. A food-delivery platform considers offering free delivery. The analyst examines customer demand, restaurant economics, courier supply, order frequency, and margin. A change that attracts orders may still weaken the business model if the subsidy is unsustainable. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C02-T02 - Customers: deep reference

Customers are people or organizations that buy, use, fund, receive, influence, or are affected by an offering. Buyer, payer, user, beneficiary, decision-maker, and administrator may be different people. Treating them as one group can hide conflicting needs. Customer analysis considers goals, context, behavior, barriers, expectations, trust, accessibility, cost, and desired outcomes. A customer request is valuable evidence but not automatically a complete requirement. The analyst should explore what the customer is trying to achieve and what evidence shows the need. Internal users and employees can also be customers of an internal service. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C02-T02 in practice

Real-life scenario. In an employee expense system, the employee submits the expense, the manager approves it, finance pays it, and the company funds it. Each group is a stakeholder with different needs. Designing only for the submitting employee can create approval and audit problems. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C02-T03 - Products: deep reference

A product is an offering designed to create value for a defined group. It includes purpose, users, capabilities, experience, quality, economics, operating responsibilities, and lifecycle. A product is not just a feature list. Features are means; outcomes and value are the reason for them. Product analysis asks which problem is being addressed, for whom, under what conditions, with what adoption behavior, at what cost, and with what risks. A feature may be useful to one segment and harmful to another. The analyst helps make trade-offs visible and ensures that product decisions remain connected to evidence and measurable outcomes. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C02-T03 in practice

Real-life scenario. A software company asks for twenty new features because competitors have them. Product analysis asks which customer segment needs each feature, what outcome it supports, how often it will be used, and what maintenance cost it creates. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C02-T04 - Services: deep reference

A service is a set of activities and interactions that produces an outcome for a customer or beneficiary. Customers experience the whole service, including waiting, handoffs, instructions, error recovery, communication, and support. A technically correct component can still produce a poor service experience. Service analysis distinguishes front-stage interactions from back-stage work that enables them. It examines channels, policies, people, systems, information, and exceptions. It also considers accessibility and trust. A service should be analyzed end to end because optimizing one channel or team may move failure to another part of the customer journey. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C02-T04 in practice

Real-life scenario. An airline may provide a technically working online refund form, but the service still fails if customers cannot understand eligibility, upload documents, receive updates, or recover from rejection. The analyst studies the full service journey. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C02-T05 - Functions: deep reference

Functions group related responsibilities such as finance, operations, sales, technology, legal, support, or human resources. Functions create specialization and accountability, but outcomes often cross functional boundaries. Functional literacy means understanding a function’s objectives, vocabulary, inputs, outputs, decisions, measures, constraints, and dependencies. The Business Analyst does not need to become the specialist owner of every function. The analyst does need enough understanding to ask relevant questions, identify conflicts, and connect local work to the end-to-end outcome. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C02-T05 in practice

Real-life scenario. A customer refund crosses sales, support, finance, fraud, and technology. Each function may optimize its own queue, but the customer experiences one refund process. Functional analysis reveals the handoffs and conflicting measures. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C02-T06 - Operating models: deep reference

An operating model describes how strategy is delivered through people, processes, technology, information, governance, partners, locations, and decision rights. It explains how work gets done. Two organizations may pursue the same strategy with different operating models. A change can fail when it does not fit the operating model. A centralized organization may require central governance, while a distributed organization may need local flexibility. Operating-model analysis examines ownership, handoffs, escalation, capability gaps, standardization, variation, and the decisions that must be redesigned alongside a system or process. [1]

Real-life scenario. A global company wants one worldwide approval workflow, but local offices have different legal requirements and decision rights. The analyst examines what can be standardized and where local variation is necessary. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C02-T07 - Value creation: deep reference

Value creation is the chain through which resources and activities produce a beneficial outcome. The analyst connects stakeholder need to capability, activity, output, outcome, and measure. This prevents a team from confusing delivery with value. A report is an output; a faster and more accurate decision may be the outcome. Value can be financial, operational, strategic, social, regulatory, customer-related, or risk- related. A good value hypothesis identifies who benefits, what changes, how it will be measured, when it should appear, and what dependencies could prevent it. Benefits should have owners rather than being left as vague promises. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C02-T07 in practice

Real-life scenario. A university launches an online learning platform. The platform is an output. Improved course completion, student access, and learning outcomes are potential outcomes. The analyst defines how value will be measured rather than declaring success at launch. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.
