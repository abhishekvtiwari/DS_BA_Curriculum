## Purpose

Business analysis depends on precise language. Words such as objective, KPI, benefit, risk, dependency, capability, process, requirement, and decision are often used casually, yet each describes a different part of organizational reasoning. When stakeholders use them interchangeably, teams can approve activity without an outcome, report a metric without a purpose, record a wish as a requirement, or treat an unresolved dependency as though it were a managed risk.

This chapter establishes a shared vocabulary and shows how the nine terms connect. The goal is not memorization. The learner should be able to classify statements, detect ambiguity, and preserve traceability from organizational intent through implementation and evaluation.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Distinguish goals, objectives, measures, targets, and initiatives.
- Explain how a KPI indicates progress without becoming the objective itself.
- Separate potential benefits from delivered and realized benefits.
- Describe risk as uncertainty affecting value rather than as a confirmed issue.
- Identify and manage dependencies with explicit ownership and conditions.
- Distinguish capabilities from functions, processes, systems, and teams.
- Describe processes using boundaries, inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and controls.
- Classify and evaluate requirements as representations of needs.
- Separate analysis, recommendation, authorization, and decision records.

## A vocabulary chain

```text
Objectives state measurable results to achieve.
KPIs indicate whether progress or performance is on course.
Benefits describe valued improvements realized by stakeholders.
Risks describe uncertainty that may affect value.
Dependencies identify conditions or relationships needed for success.
Capabilities describe what the organization must be able to do.
Processes organize activities that transform inputs into outputs.
Requirements represent needs and necessary conditions.
Decisions authorize choices among alternatives.
```

The terms form a reasoning system. They should not be treated as nine independent definitions.

## BA-01-C04-T01 - Objective

### Objective as a measurable result

An objective is a specific result an organization, initiative, team, or product seeks to achieve. It translates a broad goal into a condition that can guide choices and evaluation. “Improve customer trust” is a goal. “Reduce verified billing errors from 2.4 percent to below 1 percent within twelve months without increasing unresolved disputes” is an objective.

A sound objective identifies the result, relevant stakeholders or domain, measure, desired direction or target, and time horizon. It may also include safeguards. Not every early objective can contain a defensible numeric target; false precision is worse than an explicitly provisional target.

### Goal, objective, target, and initiative

A **goal** expresses a desired direction or condition, usually at a high level. An **objective** makes the intended result more specific and assessable. A **target** is a desired level for a measure. An **initiative** is organized work undertaken to influence the objective.

Launching a portal is an initiative, not the objective. Portal adoption may be a measure, but the intended objective might be faster access to a service. Confusing activity with result encourages teams to declare success at delivery.

### Objective hierarchy and alignment

Objectives exist at enterprise, portfolio, product, program, team, and operational levels. Lower-level objectives should contribute to higher-level intent without becoming vague copies. Apparent alignment must be tested: reducing handling time may support efficiency but conflict with first-contact resolution or accessibility.

Analysts make contribution assumptions visible and identify conflicts requiring an authorized trade-off.

### Quality checks

An objective should be relevant, understandable, measurable enough for its decision, time-aware, owned, and bounded. The analyst asks whether a baseline exists, whether the organization can influence the result, and whether achieving the target could cause harmful behavior.

## BA-01-C04-T02 - KPI

### Indicator of critical performance

A key performance indicator is a measure selected because it provides important evidence about progress toward an objective or the health of a critical activity. The word “key” matters. A dashboard containing every available number is not a KPI system.

A metric is any defined quantitative measure. An indicator provides information about a condition. A KPI is an indicator judged critical for managing performance. The same measure may be a KPI for one role and supporting detail for another.

### Anatomy of a KPI

A controlled KPI definition includes name, purpose, linked objective, formula, unit, population, data source, exclusions, segmentation, frequency, owner, baseline, target or threshold, direction, and known limitations. Without these attributes, two teams can report the same label using different calculations.

### Leading, lagging, and safeguard indicators

A **leading indicator** changes earlier and may predict an outcome. A **lagging indicator** confirms a result after it occurs. A **safeguard indicator** checks whether improvement in one area creates harm elsewhere.

For identity verification, completion rate may lead access outcomes; confirmed fraud loss may lag; false rejection and accessibility failure can safeguard against harmful optimization. A leading indicator is useful only if evidence supports the connection.

### Targets and behavior

Targets focus attention but can distort behavior. Staff may exclude difficult cases, reclassify failures, delay recording, or optimize a proxy. Analysts examine incentives, data quality, denominators, averages, outliers, and effects on underrepresented groups. KPIs inform judgment; they do not replace it.

## BA-01-C04-T03 - Benefit

### Benefit as valued improvement

A benefit is a measurable improvement considered valuable by one or more stakeholders. Benefits can be financial, operational, strategic, customer-related, employee-related, social, environmental, compliance-related, or risk-reducing.

An output creates potential benefit, not realized benefit. Installing automation is an output. Reduced error and faster service are potential benefits. They become realized only when use and operating conditions produce evidence of improvement.

### Benefit lifecycle

**Identified benefits** are proposed during analysis. **Planned benefits** have defined measures, owners, baselines, targets, dependencies, and timing. **Delivered benefits** are enabled by completed capabilities or changes. **Realized benefits** are evidenced in operation. **Sustained benefits** continue over the intended period.

The states prevent a business case from treating forecasts as facts.

### Benefit map

A benefit map connects changes and capabilities to behavioral or operational outcomes, benefits, and strategic objectives. Each connection is a causal hypothesis. Analysts record disbenefits, such as temporary productivity loss, migration burden, reduced choice, or increased workload for a subgroup.

### Ownership and realization

Delivery teams can provide outputs, but operational leaders often own benefit realization. Ownership includes measurement, adoption, process change, corrective action, and post-implementation review. Without an owner after launch, benefits commonly remain unverified.

## BA-01-C04-T04 - Risk

### Effect of uncertainty

Risk is the effect of uncertainty on objectives or value. It can involve downside, upside, or variation, though organizations often emphasize threats. A confirmed current failure is an issue; uncertainty about whether or how it may occur is risk.

A useful statement describes a cause or condition, uncertain event, and consequence: “Because the migration tool has not been tested at full volume, conversion may exceed the outage window, delaying customer access.” “Data migration risk” is only a label.

### Risk attributes

Common attributes include likelihood, impact, proximity, velocity, detectability, affected objective, owner, response, trigger, and residual exposure. Scales need definitions; “high” is meaningless if participants interpret it differently.

### Responses and ownership

Responses may avoid, reduce, transfer or share, accept, exploit, enhance, or monitor risk. A control changes likelihood, impact, or detection. A contingency describes action if the event occurs. The owner must have authority and capacity to act.

### Risk versus assumption, constraint, and issue

An assumption is provisionally treated as true. A constraint limits choices. A dependency connects success to another condition. An issue already exists. Any can generate risk, but accurate classification prevents vague registers and inappropriate responses.

## BA-01-C04-T05 - Dependency

### Required relationship or condition

A dependency exists when an outcome, activity, decision, component, or schedule relies on something else. Dependencies may be internal or external, technical or organizational, mandatory or discretionary, and within or beyond the initiative’s control.

Examples include approval before procurement, identity data before account creation, a supplier interface before testing, trained staff before launch, or a policy decision before detailed requirements can stabilize.

### Dependency statement

A useful record states the dependent item, prerequisite, needed condition or deliverable, owner on each side, required date, current status, evidence, impact of failure, and escalation route. “Waiting on IT” hides the actual relationship.

### Dependency versus sequence

Work occurring earlier is not necessarily a dependency. A true dependency means one item cannot proceed safely or correctly without another condition. Analysts challenge arbitrary sequencing and identify opportunities for parallel learning.

### Managing external dependencies

The team may not control a regulator, vendor, partner, or enterprise platform. It can still clarify commitments, monitor indicators, create alternatives, adjust scope, negotiate service expectations, or escalate. Lack of control does not remove management responsibility.

## BA-01-C04-T06 - Capability

### What the organization can do

A capability is an ability an organization needs to possess to achieve an outcome. Examples include detect fraud, onboard employees, forecast demand, manage consent, fulfill orders, or recover services.

A capability describes **what** must be possible, not one specific **how**. It may require people, skills, processes, information, technology, facilities, suppliers, governance, and funding.

### Related concepts

A function groups responsibility or expertise. A process describes a flow of activities. A system supports work. A team performs responsibilities. A product or service delivers an offering. A capability can span all of them.

### Capability assessment

Assessment can consider performance, capacity, maturity, consistency, coverage, cost, resilience, compliance, data, technology, skills, and ownership. A maturity score is not automatically valuable; the required level depends on strategy and context.

### Current and target capability

Gap analysis compares current ability with the level required for an objective. The analyst avoids assuming that every gap needs maximum maturity. Investment should reflect value, risk, dependency, and the capability’s strategic importance.

## BA-01-C04-T07 - Process

### Transformation of inputs

A process is a set of related activities that transforms defined inputs into outputs to achieve an objective. A business process often crosses functional boundaries and responds to a trigger, event, or demand.

A controlled description includes trigger, inputs, activities, decisions, roles, systems, business rules, controls, outputs, customers, outcomes, boundaries, exceptions, and measures.

### Process versus procedure and journey

A procedure instructs how to perform work. A process describes the coordinated flow. A customer journey describes the stakeholder’s end-to-end experience. The views overlap but answer different questions.

### Variation and exceptions

Documented “happy paths” rarely represent all work. Analysts examine volume, case types, rework, waiting, handoffs, exceptions, informal workarounds, failure demand, and regional or channel variation. Exceptions often expose the true complexity.

### Local and end-to-end optimization

A function may meet its target while total lead time, customer effort, or risk worsens. Process analysis follows value across boundaries and uses end-to-end measures alongside local controls.

## BA-01-C04-T08 - Requirement

### Usable representation of a need

A requirement is a usable representation of a need. It is not automatically whatever a stakeholder says, nor is it limited to a sentence in a document. Requirements may be expressed through text, models, rules, examples, data definitions, prototypes, or acceptance criteria.

### Requirement levels

**Business requirements** explain why change is initiated and what outcomes define success. **Stakeholder requirements** represent the needs of stakeholder groups. **Solution requirements** describe capabilities and qualities a solution must provide; they include functional and non-functional requirements. **Transition requirements** describe temporary conditions needed to move from current to future state.

The labels help trace purpose and avoid mixing strategic outcomes with design details.

### Quality and value

Verification asks whether a requirement is expressed to an acceptable standard: clear, concise, consistent, feasible, testable, and appropriately detailed. Validation asks whether it supports stakeholder needs, expected benefits, and solution scope. A beautifully written requirement can still be the wrong requirement.

### Requirement versus design

A requirement describes a need or necessary condition. A design describes a representation of how the need may be satisfied. The boundary depends on perspective and level. Analysts make the reasoning explicit rather than arguing that one format is always a requirement or always a design.

### Lifecycle and traceability

Requirements move from discovery through analysis, approval, change, implementation, verification, validation, operation, and retirement. Traceability connects them to sources, objectives, stakeholders, rules, designs, tests, releases, and benefits. The depth should be proportional to change and risk.

## BA-01-C04-T09 - Decision

### Authorized choice

A decision is an authorized choice among alternatives or a determination that guides action. Analysis produces evidence, options, and recommendations; it does not silently substitute for decision authority.

### Decision components

A good decision record identifies the question, owner, date, options considered, criteria, evidence, assumptions, constraints, risks, conflicts, choice, rationale, conditions, actions, and review trigger. The record should be proportionate to consequence and reversibility.

### Decision versus recommendation and approval

A recommendation advises a course of action. A decision selects or rejects it. Approval confirms authorization or acceptance under a governance process. One person may perform several roles, but the logical distinctions protect accountability.

### Decision quality versus outcome quality

A sound decision can produce an unfavorable outcome because uncertainty remains. A weak decision can be lucky. Quality should be judged by the evidence available, reasoning, stakeholder inclusion, ethical treatment, and process used at the time, then improved through learning.

### Reversible and irreversible decisions

Low-cost reversible choices can be made quickly and tested. High-impact, irreversible, safety-critical, or rights-affecting choices require stronger evidence, broader review, and explicit authority. The analyst calibrates documentation and assurance accordingly.

## Integrated example

A bank seeks to “improve small-business lending.” Its **objective** is to reduce eligible applicants’ time to a responsible credit decision while maintaining fair-lending and loss safeguards. **KPIs** include end-to-end decision time, completion, false rejection review, complaint rate, loss performance, and manual rework. Expected **benefits** include faster access, lower effort, and reduced processing cost.

Key **risks** include biased data, fraud, incorrect affordability assessment, and exclusion of applicants with limited digital records. **Dependencies** include verified business data, policy approval, model validation, and trained underwriters. Required **capabilities** include identity verification, affordability assessment, explainable decisioning, case review, and appeals. The lending **process** connects application, evidence, assessment, decision, notification, funding, and monitoring.

**Requirements** represent necessary stakeholder, solution, quality, and transition conditions. Authorized **decisions** determine policy thresholds, rollout scope, and whether evidence supports release. The shared vocabulary prevents the organization from treating “launch an AI model” as the objective.

## Common misconceptions

- An objective is not a task list.
- A KPI is not automatically useful because it is measurable.
- A forecast benefit is not a realized benefit.
- A current issue is not an uncertain risk.
- An earlier activity is not necessarily a dependency.
- A capability is not identical to a software system.
- A process is not merely a department’s procedure.
- A stakeholder request is not automatically a validated requirement.
- A recommendation is not an authorized decision.

### BA-01-C04-T01 - Objective: deep reference

An objective describes a result an organization or initiative intends to achieve. It should guide decisions and be specific enough to evaluate. Objectives are stronger when they identify target direction, timeframe, population, owner, and measurement. “Build a dashboard” describes an activity or output. “Reduce weekly reporting preparation time by 50% while making data-quality issues visible” is an outcome-oriented objective. Objectives should be connected to strategy and should not be so broad that every result appears to support them. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T01 in practice

Real-life scenario. A logistics team sets the objective of reducing the median order-to- delivery time by one day in the next quarter without increasing damage claims. The target and protection measure guide analysis better than “improve logistics.” Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T02 - KPI: deep reference

A Key Performance Indicator is a selected measure that indicates performance against an important objective or outcome. Every KPI is a metric, but not every metric is a KPI. A governed KPI definition includes name, purpose, formula, unit, grain, numerator, denominator, source, owner, frequency, target, acceptable range, exclusions, and interpretation. Definitions must resolve questions such as whether a count is of orders, customers, events, or records. Without a definition, dashboards can create false precision. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T02 in practice

Real-life scenario. A subscription business defines churn as customers who cancel during a month divided by customers active at the start of that month. Without this definition, one team may count cancellations while another counts inactive accounts, producing contradictory dashboards. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T03 - Benefit: deep reference

A benefit is an advantage or improvement resulting from change. It may be financial, operational, strategic, customer-related, employee-related, regulatory, social, or risk- related. Delivery of a feature does not prove that a benefit occurred. Benefits realization needs a baseline, expected change, owner, timeframe, measurement method, dependencies, and review. The analyst may support benefits analysis, but the business or operational owner normally owns the realized outcome. Benefits should include disbenefits and unintended effects where relevant. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T03 in practice

Real-life scenario. A new self-service address-change service may reduce manual work and improve customer speed. The benefits owner must measure adoption, processing time, correction rate, and support contacts rather than assuming the benefit occurred because the service was released. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T04 - Risk: deep reference

A risk is an uncertain event or condition that may affect an objective. A useful risk statement identifies cause, uncertain event, and impact. A risk is different from an issue, which is already occurring. Risk analysis considers likelihood, impact, proximity, velocity, controls, response, owner, and residual risk. A risk register is not a substitute for action. It is useful when it changes a decision, creates a mitigation, or establishes a trigger for escalation. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T04 in practice

Real-life scenario. A company plans to automate loan-document review. A risk is that poor- quality scans may cause incorrect approvals or rejections. The risk response may include confidence thresholds, human review, sampling, monitoring, and an escalation path. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T05 - Dependency: deep reference

A dependency exists when one activity, decision, capability, requirement, team, system, or outcome relies on another. Dependencies may be technical, organizational, financial, legal, data-related, or external. A dependency record identifies both sides, owners, required condition or date, consequence of delay, and coordination method. Dependencies influence sequencing and feasibility. They should be visible early because a local team may appear ready while the end-to-end change is blocked elsewhere. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T05 in practice

Real-life scenario. A dashboard release depends on a finance data definition, an access approval, a warehouse refresh, and a business owner who can validate the numbers. Missing any one dependency can make a technically finished dashboard unusable. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T06 - Capability: deep reference

A capability is an ability that enables an organization or system to perform an activity or achieve an outcome. Capabilities combine people, process, information, technology, governance, and resources. A feature is narrower; a capability is usually more stable. Capability analysis helps leaders see current strength, desired strength, gaps, investment choices, and ownership. A customer-support capability includes knowledge, channels, case handling, escalation, people, systems, and measures—not only a ticketing application. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T06 in practice

Real-life scenario. A hospital wants a patient portal capability. The capability includes identity, scheduling, notifications, support, accessibility, privacy, data integration, and operating ownership. It is larger than the portal screen itself. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T07 - Process: deep reference

A process is a connected set of activities that transforms inputs into outputs or outcomes. It has triggers, participants, decisions, rules, handoffs, exceptions, measures, and controls. Processes often cross functional boundaries. A process model is a representation, not reality. It must be validated with people who perform or experience the work. Analysis considers flow, waiting, rework, variation, failure, ownership, information quality, and customer value. A process can be efficient locally and poor end to end. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T07 in practice

Real-life scenario. A mortgage application process includes submission, document collection, verification, credit decision, offer, acceptance, and completion. Mapping only the employee’s task hides customer waiting, rework, and handoffs between teams. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T08 - Requirement: deep reference

A requirement represents a need, capability, condition, or constraint. It may describe business outcomes, stakeholder needs, solution behavior, quality attributes, data, rules, interfaces, security, privacy, accessibility, or compliance. A quality requirement is clear, necessary, feasible, testable, traceable, prioritized, consistent, and understandable to its audience. Words such as “fast,” “easy,” and “user-friendly” need operational definitions. Requirements should preserve the need without forcing an unnecessary design decision. [1]

### Worked example - BA-01-C04-T08 in practice

Real-life scenario. “The system shall display the customer’s consent status, consent date, and source after successful authentication” is more testable than “make consent information easy to see.” The requirement defines behavior without prescribing the visual design. Analyst lesson. The analyst should identify the evidence, affected stakeholders, outcome, assumptions, constraints, and decision owner before recommending a change.

### BA-01-C04-T09 - Decision: deep reference

A decision is a commitment to choose an option, direction, priority, rule, or action. A decision has an owner, evidence, criteria, alternatives, date, rationale, and consequences. Decision logs should distinguish decisions from discussions, recommendations, approvals, and actions. Recording a decision prevents repeated debate and exposes unresolved assumptions. A decision can later be changed, but the change should be explicit and traceable. [1]

Real-life scenario. A team chooses between building and buying a document-management capability. The decision record includes options, cost, security findings, time constraints, integration risk, owner, date, and rationale so the choice can be understood later. Analyst lesson. ## Advanced vocabulary system

### Connecting objectives, KPIs, and benefits

An objective states the desired direction or result, a KPI provides selected evidence about performance, and a benefit is an advantage realized by an affected stakeholder. They are related but not interchangeable. "Reduce avoidable order cancellations" is an objective. Cancellation rate may be a KPI. Lower service cost and improved customer confidence may be benefits. A team can improve the KPI without realizing the intended benefit if it changes classification rules, discourages reporting, or shifts cancellation into returns. The analyst therefore records the definition, source, owner, baseline, target, frequency, exclusions, and likely behavioral effects of each measure [1].

A useful chain begins with the stakeholder and condition to improve, then connects the objective to evidence and expected benefit. It also names safeguards. If faster handling is the objective, quality, employee load, accessibility, and repeat contact may be safeguards. This prevents a narrow target from becoming the whole definition of success. Benefits normally appear after adoption and operational use, while an output such as a deployed dashboard appears at delivery. Reporting an output as a realized benefit is a category error.

When several KPIs describe one objective, distinguish leading from lagging evidence. Training completion may precede better service but does not prove it. Customer retention may reveal value later but provides slow feedback. A balanced set helps learning, yet adding measures indefinitely creates noise. Select the smallest set that can guide a decision, expose material harm, and demonstrate the intended outcome.

### Connecting risk, dependency, and capability

A risk describes uncertainty that may affect an objective. A dependency describes reliance: one element needs another condition, decision, service, team, or outcome. A capability describes the enduring ability to perform an activity or achieve an outcome. Confusing them weakens action. "The identity provider" is not itself a risk; reliance on its availability is a dependency, and an outage during enrollment may be a risk event. Reliable identity verification is a capability involving technology, people, policy, data, controls, and operating ownership.

These concepts support different controls. Risk analysis records likelihood, impact, exposure, response, owner, triggers, and residual risk. Dependency management records the provider, required condition, due date, interface, failure consequence, and fallback. Capability assessment examines current maturity, demand, gaps, investment, and ownership. One real situation can require all three records, but copying the same sentence into each conceals the distinct decision.

Dependencies form networks. A milestone may depend on a data definition; the definition may depend on a policy decision; the decision may depend on legal interpretation. The analyst traces the chain far enough to expose critical paths and single points of failure. A dependency without an owner and required date is merely an observation. A risk without a response decision is merely a warning.

Capability language helps teams avoid equating a purchased tool with organizational ability. Buying workflow software does not create case-management capability unless roles, information, rules, integration, skills, support, governance, and measures work together. Conversely, a capability gap does not automatically justify new technology. Process simplification, training, clearer authority, or removal of unnecessary demand may be the stronger intervention.

### Connecting processes and requirements

A process is coordinated work that transforms inputs into outcomes. A requirement is a usable representation of a need, capability, condition, or constraint. Process analysis reveals participants, events, decisions, rules, handoffs, information, exceptions, measures, and controls. Requirements specify what must be true for an approved change, but they should remain traceable to the process evidence and intended outcome [2].

A process map is a model, not reality. It can omit informal work, emotional effort, inaccessible steps, policy exceptions, or failure recovery. Validate it with people who perform and experience the work. Likewise, a requirement can be grammatically precise yet wrong because it formalizes an inaccurate map. Quality requires both internal clarity and external validity.

Requirements exist at different levels. A business requirement may state an outcome or capability; a stakeholder requirement expresses what an affected group needs; a solution requirement describes behavior or quality; a transition requirement supports movement from current to future operation. Mixing levels in one list creates false conflict. The analyst identifies the level, source, owner, rationale, priority, acceptance evidence, and trace links.

The distinction between a rule and a requirement also matters. A business rule may exist independently of the change, such as an eligibility policy. A requirement may state that a service must evaluate that rule and record the decision. If the rule changes, traceability identifies affected requirements, tests, communications, and controls. Repeating the policy text in many requirements makes controlled change harder.

### Decision as the integrating concept

A decision commits authorized people to an option, priority, rule, direction, or action. Analysis supports decisions but does not silently take authority from accountable owners. A recommendation is the analyst's evidence-based proposal. An approval is the authorized acceptance of a defined item. Consensus is broad agreement. These states should not be recorded as though they were identical.

A durable decision record identifies the question, options considered, evaluation criteria, evidence, assumptions, constraints, risks, consultation, decision owner, date, chosen option, rationale, conditions, and review trigger. The purpose is not bureaucracy. It prevents recurring debate, explains trade-offs to people who were absent, and permits revision when evidence changes.

Decision quality and outcome quality are different. A sound decision made with reliable evidence can have a poor outcome because uncertainty materializes. A careless decision can have a good outcome by luck. Review the reasoning available at the time as well as the eventual result. This distinction supports learning without hindsight bias.

Vocabulary becomes useful when it improves a real conversation. When a stakeholder says "Our requirement is a new portal," the analyst can ask which objective it supports, which problem it addresses, which capability is missing, which process changes, which dependencies and risks matter, which benefits should result, which KPIs will provide evidence, and who owns the decision. The nine terms form a reasoning system rather than a glossary to memorize.


### Vocabulary conflict review

+Terminology conflicts often surface when different functions use one word for different objects. Sales may call a qualified lead a customer, finance may reserve customer for an invoiced account, and service may use it for any person receiving support. The analyst does not select a definition by preference. The analyst records each usage, identifies the decision affected, names the authoritative owner for that context, and tests the consequences of adopting one definition. Where several meanings must coexist, qualify the term and maintain an explicit mapping.

+The same discipline applies when two words are treated as synonyms. An objective is not a benefit, a dependency is not a risk, and a capability is not a product feature. A vocabulary review should locate every material term in measures, requirements, process models, decision records, and communications. Conflicts are resolved through accountable decisions and then propagated through trace links. A glossary becomes governed only when changes have owners, rationale, effective dates, and affected-artifact review.

+In multilingual or regulated environments, translation adds another layer. Literal equivalents may not preserve operational or legal meaning. Retain the controlled definition, involve qualified domain reviewers, record jurisdiction, and test translated language with intended users. Clear vocabulary is therefore part of data quality, requirements quality, and decision quality rather than editorial polish.
+

A final control is contextual consistency. The chosen definition should remain stable across the artifact unless a change is explicitly signposted. Review headings, tables, diagrams, formulas, and acceptance statements together; a term corrected in prose but left inconsistent in a KPI formula still creates operational ambiguity. Record unresolved disputes as decisions needed, not as silently selected meanings.

Vocabulary control ultimately protects shared understanding, measurement integrity, accountability, and trustworthy organizational decisions.
