## Purpose

Stakeholder analysis is the disciplined work of determining who has a relevant relationship to a situation or proposed change, what matters to them, how they can affect outcomes, how outcomes may affect them, and where decision authority resides. It prevents an initiative from being shaped only by the people who are easiest to reach, most senior, most vocal, or already committed to a preferred solution.

A stakeholder is not merely someone invited to a meeting. Stakeholders may provide knowledge, experience consequences, control resources, set policy, operate a process, use a service, support a system, represent an affected population, approve a decision, assure compliance, or create conditions that enable or block change. Some stakeholders have several of these relationships at once. Others are important precisely because they have little formal power but may experience substantial impact.

This chapter establishes stakeholder analysis before the later chapters teach interviews, workshops, observation, research, conflict management, and negotiation. Those elicitation activities are only reliable when the analyst understands whose evidence is needed, why their perspective matters, what authority they hold, and what risks arise if they are excluded or misclassified.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Define stakeholder analysis as a continuing evidence-based activity rather than a one-time list.
- Identify internal, external, direct, indirect, represented, and overlooked stakeholders systematically.
- Distinguish stakeholder interest from preference, position, influence, power, authority, and impact.
- Assess formal and informal influence without confusing seniority with actual ability to affect outcomes.
- Analyze positive, negative, intended, unintended, direct, indirect, and uneven impacts.
- Use a power-interest map as a provisional engagement aid rather than a political ranking or permission to ignore people.
- Create evidence-based personas without stereotypes, invented details, or replacement of real stakeholder participation.
- Clarify decision rights, consultation duties, escalation paths, and the difference between advice and authorization.
- Maintain a stakeholder analysis that is traceable, proportionate, inclusive, and responsive to change.

## Stakeholder analysis as a system

Stakeholder analysis connects seven controlled questions:

```text
Identification: Who has a relevant relationship to the situation or change?
Interest: What matters to them, and why?
Influence: How can they shape evidence, decisions, delivery, adoption, or outcomes?
Impact: How may the current situation or proposed change affect them?
Power-interest: What provisional engagement attention does the combination suggest?
Personas: What evidence-based patterns help a team understand important stakeholder or user groups?
Decision rights: Who may decide, approve, advise, perform, assure, veto, or escalate?
```

These questions are connected but not interchangeable. A person may have high interest and severe exposure to harm while holding little influence. A regulator may have substantial formal power but little day-to-day involvement. A subject-matter expert may strongly influence a recommendation without owning the final decision. A persona may summarize patterns among users but cannot approve requirements or speak for every individual represented by the pattern.

Stakeholder analysis is therefore a model of relationships, not a scorecard of personal worth. Its purpose is to improve discovery, participation, governance, communication, and decisions. It should never be used to justify manipulation, concealment, retaliation, token consultation, or exclusion of people whose rights or welfare may be affected.

## BA-02-C01-T01 - Identification

### Plain-language explanation

Stakeholder identification asks who must be considered for the analysis to be credible and the change to be responsible. It begins with known participants but deliberately searches beyond them. The analyst looks for people and groups who know something important, do the work, receive or provide value, own decisions, control resources, enforce constraints, support operations, experience consequences, or represent interests that might otherwise remain unseen.

Identification is not complete when a sponsor provides a list. A sponsor sees the initiative from one position. Process owners may know formal responsibilities; frontline staff may know actual workarounds; customers may know the lived experience; service teams may know recurring failures; security and legal specialists may know non-negotiable duties; suppliers may know contractual dependencies; and communities may experience indirect effects that internal participants do not observe.

### Formal definition

Stakeholder identification is the iterative process of discovering, describing, grouping, and validating the individuals, groups, organizations, roles, or represented interests that have a material relationship to a need, decision, change, solution, operation, or outcome.

The word **material** is important. It means the relationship could affect the analysis or be affected by its consequences enough to matter to the decision. This prevents the list from becoming unlimited while also preventing convenience from defining relevance.

### Units of identification

An analyst can identify stakeholders at different levels:

- **Named individual:** appropriate when authority, expertise, or accountability belongs to a specific person.
- **Role:** useful when the relationship is stable even if the role-holder changes, such as service owner or privacy officer.
- **Team or function:** suitable when a group shares responsibilities, such as customer support or finance operations.
- **Organization:** useful for suppliers, regulators, partners, unions, professional bodies, or public agencies.
- **Population or segment:** necessary for customers, residents, patients, students, employees, or other affected groups.
- **Represented interest:** used when affected people cannot participate directly and a legitimate representative, advocate, guardian, association, or specialist contributes their perspective.

These units should not be mixed carelessly. “Operations” may hide several roles with conflicting needs. A named executive may not represent every member of a department. A customer segment may contain people with very different accessibility, language, channel, or support needs. The chosen unit must match the purpose and level of the decision.

### A systematic discovery frame

Stakeholders can be sought by examining the change from several directions.

**Value and experience:** Who receives value? Who experiences delay, cost, risk, benefit, effort, confusion, exclusion, or harm? Who chooses whether to use the product or service?

**Work and operations:** Who performs the current work? Who supervises it? Who handles exceptions, complaints, failures, reconciliations, support, maintenance, or recovery? Who receives outputs or supplies inputs?

**Authority and governance:** Who sponsors the work? Who owns the policy, process, product, service, budget, data, risk, or technology? Who approves scope, funding, design, deployment, access, acceptance, or change?

**Knowledge and evidence:** Who holds domain expertise, operational memory, research findings, analytics, records, legal interpretation, or technical knowledge? Who knows rare but serious cases?

**Dependencies and interfaces:** Which upstream and downstream teams, suppliers, partners, platforms, or public bodies can affect or be affected by the change?

**Rights and obligations:** Whose rights, entitlements, safety, privacy, employment, access, or legal position may change? Which regulators, auditors, representatives, or assurance specialists protect those interests?

**Lifecycle:** Who is relevant during discovery, authorization, design, delivery, transition, operation, support, measurement, retirement, or incident response? Stakeholders can change across stages.

### Boundary-first identification

Before listing stakeholders, define the analytical boundary: the decision being supported, the current situation, the proposed change if one exists, the organizational and geographic scope, the time horizon, and important exclusions. Without a boundary, identification can become either arbitrary or endless.

For example, “replace the customer platform” is too broad. A clearer boundary might be: “decide whether to replace the case-intake component used by regional support teams during the next budget year, including effects on customers, agents, reporting, privacy, and downstream case handling.” This boundary suggests stakeholder categories and makes omissions easier to detect.

The boundary is provisional. Discovering an affected group or dependency may reveal that the original boundary was too narrow. Expanding it requires an explicit decision, not silent scope drift.

### Direct, indirect, and overlooked stakeholders

**Direct stakeholders** interact with the situation or change. They may operate a process, use a service, approve a decision, or deliver the work.

**Indirect stakeholders** experience consequences or influence conditions without direct interaction. A family member may assist a service user. A downstream reporting team may depend on data generated upstream. A community may experience environmental or access effects.

**Overlooked stakeholders** are repeatedly missed because they are less visible, less powerful, less available, outside formal structures, or represented by incomplete data. Examples include night-shift staff, temporary workers, non-digital users, people using assistive technology, rural customers, rare-case specialists, small suppliers, former users who abandoned a service, and teams that handle failures rather than normal cases.

The absence of a voice from meetings is not evidence that the stakeholder does not exist or does not care. Access barriers, fear of consequences, confidentiality, language, time, disability, employment status, or lack of institutional legitimacy may prevent participation.

### Stakeholder discovery register

A useful identification record can contain:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder ID | Stable reference that does not depend on a display name |
| Individual, role, group, or interest | Defines the unit being analyzed |
| Relationship to scope | Explains why inclusion is material |
| Lifecycle relevance | States when the stakeholder matters |
| Knowledge or evidence | Identifies what can be learned from them |
| Potential impact | Records how they may be affected |
| Authority or influence | Gives an initial, testable description |
| Representation route | Explains how the perspective will be included |
| Evidence source | Shows why the entry exists |
| Confidence and review date | Makes uncertainty and change visible |

This is a conceptual record, not a requirement to use a particular tool. A spreadsheet, controlled table, modeling repository, or governance system may be appropriate depending on sensitivity, scale, and change frequency.

### Conceptual example

A hospital proposes digital self-check-in. An initial list contains the sponsor, product manager, software team, and reception manager. Systematic identification adds patients who use different languages, people with visual or motor impairments, caregivers, emergency arrivals, reception staff on different shifts, clinical teams receiving identity data, information governance, safeguarding, technical support, facilities staff responsible for kiosk placement, infection-control specialists, the identity-system owner, and teams handling check-in failures.

Not every stakeholder needs the same engagement. Yet omitting them from analysis could hide safety, accessibility, privacy, workflow, and recovery requirements. Identification creates the field of inquiry; later analysis decides what participation is proportionate.

### Quality checks

- Every entry has a stated relationship to the defined scope.
- Internal and external, direct and indirect, current and future stakeholders were considered.
- Normal work, exceptions, failures, support, transition, and retirement were examined.
- Affected people with low organizational power were deliberately sought.
- Roles are separated when their needs, authority, or impacts differ materially.
- Entries are supported by evidence or marked as hypotheses requiring validation.
- The list has an owner, review trigger, and last-reviewed date.

### Common failure modes

**Sponsor-only identification:** treating the sponsor’s list as complete. This reproduces the sponsor’s visibility limits.

**Organization-chart identification:** assuming formal reporting lines reveal customers, suppliers, informal experts, operational dependencies, or affected communities.

**Meeting-list identification:** confusing attendance with relevance and excluding people unable or unauthorized to attend.

**Category inflation:** listing broad labels such as “users” or “IT” that conceal meaningful differences.

**Infinite inclusion:** adding everyone remotely connected without a materiality test, making the analysis unusable.

**Static identification:** failing to revisit the list after scope, evidence, leadership, regulation, or delivery conditions change.

### Advanced note: stakeholders as a network

A flat list cannot show every important relationship. Information, trust, authority, dependency, conflict, and influence often travel through networks. A formally junior coordinator may connect several departments and strongly affect adoption. A professional association may influence many independent practitioners. A supplier may control a technical dependency while the contracting organization retains decision authority.

Network thinking asks who communicates with whom, whose interpretation is trusted, where information is filtered, which relationships are strained, and where a single stakeholder creates a dependency. The goal is not covert political surveillance. It is to understand how the organizational system actually behaves and to design ethical, transparent participation.

## BA-02-C01-T02 - Interest

### Plain-language explanation

Interest describes why a stakeholder cares about the situation or change and what outcomes, concerns, duties, needs, incentives, or values are relevant to them. Interest is not the same as enthusiasm. A stakeholder may oppose a change and still have high interest. Another may support its goal but have little time for day-to-day involvement. A regulator may be interested in compliance rather than the product’s commercial success.

Analysts should not reduce interest to “supporter” or “resistor.” Those labels describe an observed or assumed position, not the underlying reasons. A person who resists automation may be protecting customer safety, professional standards, employment security, reliable exception handling, personal status, or an inefficient habit. These possibilities require different evidence and responses.

### Formal definition

Stakeholder interest is the nature and degree of a stakeholder’s concern, involvement, obligation, dependency, benefit, exposure, or motivation in relation to the defined scope and its possible outcomes.

Interest includes both **what matters** and **how much it matters**. It should be described in context and at a point in time. Interest may change when consequences become clearer, a decision approaches, costs move between teams, or a proposed solution changes.

### Sources of interest

Stakeholder interest can arise from:

- **Outcomes:** revenue, access, safety, service quality, customer success, public value, or strategic progress.
- **Work:** workload, role clarity, autonomy, skills, schedule, tools, performance measures, or job security.
- **Rights and welfare:** privacy, fairness, accessibility, dignity, health, legal entitlement, or freedom from harm.
- **Resources:** budget, staffing, capacity, data, technology, facilities, or supplier commitments.
- **Accountability:** professional duty, regulatory obligation, audit exposure, risk ownership, or public responsibility.
- **Dependencies:** reliance on an input, output, decision, interface, capability, or another team’s performance.
- **Identity and values:** professional standards, organizational mission, community priorities, reputation, or trust.
- **Learning and uncertainty:** desire to test an assumption, understand consequences, or preserve options.

A stakeholder may hold several interests that conflict. A manager may want faster processing and strong quality controls. A customer may want convenience and privacy. An employee may support organizational goals but fear an unfair performance measure. Good analysis preserves these tensions rather than forcing a single simplified motive.

### Position, preference, need, and interest

These concepts should be separated:

- A **position** is a stated stance, such as “we must keep the current approval.”
- A **preference** is a favored option, such as “use email rather than a portal.”
- A **need** is a condition required to address a problem or achieve an outcome.
- An **interest** is the underlying concern, value, duty, or exposure that makes the matter relevant.

Suppose finance says, “Every refund must retain two approvals.” The position favors the current control. The interest may be preventing fraud, maintaining segregation of duties, satisfying an audit finding, or limiting personal accountability. If the interest is understood, alternatives may become possible: risk-based thresholds, independent monitoring, or automated control evidence. The analyst does not assume the current position is irrational, nor assume it is the only way to protect the interest.

### Evidence for interest

Interest can be explored through stakeholder statements, observed behavior, role responsibilities, policies, incentives, measures, complaints, prior decisions, service data, contracts, legal duties, and outcome exposure. No single source is perfect.

Self-reported interest matters because stakeholders can explain their own concerns. It may still be incomplete. People may not know downstream effects, may frame a concern strategically, or may feel unsafe expressing it. Behavioral evidence can reveal attention and effort but may be distorted by workload or access barriers. Formal responsibilities describe expected interest but not necessarily actual engagement.

A responsible record distinguishes:

```text
Reported interest: what the stakeholder says matters.
Observed interest: what behavior or evidence suggests receives attention.
Normative interest: what should matter because of a duty, right, or accountability.
Inferred interest: the analyst's provisional interpretation.
```

Inferences must be visible and validated. An analyst should never present a private speculation about motivation as fact.

### Interest profile

Rather than a single high-medium-low score, an interest profile can record:

- relevant outcomes and concerns;
- current position and preferred options;
- underlying needs, duties, or exposures;
- perceived benefits and risks;
- questions the stakeholder needs answered;
- evidence supporting the profile;
- confidence and disagreements;
- likely change triggers;
- appropriate validation route.

Scores can help sort a large register, but narrative evidence preserves meaning. “High interest” alone does not tell a team whether the stakeholder needs co-design, assurance evidence, early warning, accessible communication, or a decision request.

### Conceptual example

In a university redesigning course registration, students may care about fair access to required classes, timetable fit, clear prerequisites, mobile usability, and rapid confirmation. Academic departments may care about capacity, curriculum sequence, instructor workload, and academic rules. Finance may care about fee status. Disability services may care about equitable access and accommodation. Technical operations may care about peak load and recovery.

All have high interest in parts of the service, but their interests differ. Treating “the university” as one stakeholder would hide the tensions the analysis must make visible.

### Quality checks

- Interests are tied to the defined scope and desired outcomes.
- Positions and preferred solutions are not mistaken for underlying interests.
- Positive, negative, neutral, uncertain, and conflicting interests are allowed.
- Formal duties and rights are considered alongside personal or organizational motivation.
- Inferences are labeled and supported by a plan for validation.
- Changes in interest can be recorded over time.
- Sensitive motive claims have appropriate access controls.

### Common failure modes

**Support-resistance simplification:** categorizing people by whether they agree with the proposed solution rather than understanding what matters to them.

**Mind reading:** assigning motives without evidence, often in ways that protect the favored solution.

**Declared-interest bias:** believing only what powerful participants say while ignoring incentives, behavior, and affected groups.

**Single-interest assumption:** treating a stakeholder as if one objective explains every decision.

**Interest as entitlement:** assuming high interest automatically grants decision authority.

### Advanced note: interests, incentives, and institutions

Interest is shaped by more than personal preference. Measures, funding models, reporting lines, professional standards, contracts, cultural norms, and institutional history can reward certain behaviors. A department may rationally resist a change that transfers work to it without resources, even when the change benefits the organization overall.

The analyst should expose this system without accusing individuals. When incentives conflict with desired outcomes, communication alone may not resolve the problem. The change may require redesigned measures, resources, governance, or allocation of benefits and costs.

## BA-02-C01-T03 - Influence

### Plain-language explanation

Influence is a stakeholder’s ability to shape what happens. It may affect which evidence is heard, how a problem is framed, which options are considered, what decisions are made, whether resources are available, how delivery proceeds, whether people adopt the change, or how outcomes are judged.

Influence is broader than formal authority. A board may approve investment, while frontline experts shape feasibility, customers shape adoption, an operations team controls implementation capacity, and an informal opinion leader shapes trust. Conversely, a senior title does not guarantee influence over a specialized regulatory, technical, or community decision.

### Formal definition

Stakeholder influence is the demonstrated or plausible capacity of a stakeholder to affect information, interpretation, choices, resources, behavior, delivery, legitimacy, adoption, or outcomes within the defined context.

Influence should be described by mechanism. “High influence” is weak unless the analyst can explain how the stakeholder can exercise it.

### Sources and mechanisms of influence

Influence may arise from:

- **Formal authority:** permission to approve, reject, direct, fund, regulate, or set policy.
- **Resource control:** control of budget, staff, capacity, data, facilities, systems, or supplier access.
- **Expertise:** trusted domain, legal, technical, operational, research, or professional knowledge.
- **Operational position:** ability to implement, interpret, bypass, delay, or sustain a change.
- **Information access:** control over records, metrics, communication channels, or evidence.
- **Relationships and networks:** trust, reputation, alliances, representation, or access to decision-makers.
- **Legitimacy:** recognized right to speak from lived experience, elected role, professional mandate, or community representation.
- **Adoption choice:** ability to use, refuse, abandon, recommend, or work around a service or process.
- **Veto or assurance:** ability to stop progression because a legal, safety, security, financial, or quality condition is unmet.
- **Public voice:** capacity to shape reputation, media attention, political response, or wider confidence.

Different sources may conflict. A sponsor may have funding authority while an architecture board controls technical approval. Users may lack formal authority but determine adoption. A regulator may rarely participate yet possess decisive enforcement power.

### Formal and informal influence

**Formal influence** is documented in law, policy, governance, contracts, role descriptions, delegations, or approval processes. It is usually easier to trace but may still be unclear or outdated.

**Informal influence** arises through trust, expertise, history, relationships, control of practical knowledge, or the ability to mobilize others. It can explain why a formally approved decision fails in practice. Informal influence is not inherently improper; experienced coordinators and respected specialists often help organizations function. It becomes risky when hidden influence overrides transparent authority, excludes affected groups, or prevents accountable review.

Analysts should recognize informal influence without creating a secret political dossier. Record only what is relevant, evidence-based, respectful, and necessary for engagement or governance.

### Influence across a decision pathway

A stakeholder can influence different stages:

```text
Agenda influence: determines which issue receives attention.
Framing influence: shapes how the problem and evidence are understood.
Option influence: expands, restricts, or designs possible responses.
Authorization influence: contributes to or owns approval.
Delivery influence: enables, changes, delays, or blocks implementation.
Adoption influence: affects whether people use and trust the result.
Evaluation influence: shapes measures and interpretation of outcomes.
```

This prevents one overall rating from hiding where influence operates. A user advocate may strongly affect framing and evaluation while having no budget authority. A technical platform owner may have limited interest in the business outcome but decisive delivery influence.

### Assessing influence with evidence

Useful evidence includes governance documents, decision records, delegations, budgets, process ownership, access permissions, dependency maps, prior change history, meeting observations, communication patterns, adoption data, and validated stakeholder testimony.

The analyst can ask:

- Which decisions can this stakeholder make or materially shape?
- Which resources, information, dependencies, or permissions do they control?
- Whose views do decision-makers trust?
- Can they enable, delay, block, reinterpret, or reverse the change?
- Can they affect adoption, legitimacy, safety, compliance, or public trust?
- Has similar influence been demonstrated before?
- Is the influence current, issue-specific, and bounded?

Influence ratings should state confidence. A plausible ability to block a change may be untested. A previously influential leader may have changed role. A union, regulator, or customer group may respond differently depending on the issue.

### Conceptual example

A bank plans a new fraud-screening rule. The fraud director sponsors the change, compliance interprets obligations, model-risk specialists control approval conditions, data engineering controls feature availability, contact-center staff manage customer consequences, and customers decide whether the added friction is tolerable. A consumer advocate may shape fairness concerns and public legitimacy.

No single hierarchy describes all influence. The analyst must map which stakeholder can shape which part of the outcome.

### Quality checks

- Every influence rating names at least one mechanism.
- Formal authority and informal influence are separated.
- Influence is assessed across framing, authorization, delivery, adoption, and evaluation.
- Evidence, confidence, time sensitivity, and context are recorded.
- Low-power stakeholders are not treated as low-importance when impact is high.
- Sensitive relationship information is limited to legitimate analytical need.
- Influence is reassessed when roles, scope, evidence, or conditions change.

### Common failure modes

**Seniority equals influence:** assuming job level predicts every type of influence.

**Visibility equals influence:** overrating frequent meeting participants and underrating operational or external actors.

**Authority-influence confusion:** treating the person who persuades others as the person authorized to decide.

**Manipulation framing:** using influence analysis to bypass transparent governance or pressure stakeholders covertly.

**Permanent scoring:** failing to recognize that influence changes by issue and lifecycle stage.

### Advanced note: dependence and countervailing influence

Influence often arises from dependency. If an initiative depends on scarce expertise, a platform, a supplier, a legal approval, or user adoption, the dependency gives the relevant stakeholder influence. Dependencies can be mutual: a supplier controls a component but depends on contract renewal; a central team controls standards but depends on local implementation.

Influence analysis should also examine countervailing structures such as appeals, independent assurance, worker representation, public consultation, segregation of duties, or multi-party approval. These structures prevent concentrated influence from becoming unaccountable power.

## BA-02-C01-T04 - Impact

### Plain-language explanation

Impact describes how a stakeholder may be affected by the current situation, the transition, the proposed change, the delivered solution, or its longer-term outcomes. Impact can be beneficial, harmful, mixed, uncertain, or different from what was intended.

Impact is not the same as interest. A group may initially show little interest because it does not know the change is coming, while the eventual impact on its workload, rights, access, or safety is substantial. Ethical analysis gives affected stakeholders attention even when they lack influence or organized representation.

### Formal definition

Stakeholder impact is the material change in outcomes, experience, rights, responsibilities, workload, capability, cost, risk, behavior, access, or welfare that a stakeholder may experience because of a situation, decision, transition, solution, or resulting system effects.

Impact analysis includes both anticipated consequences and evidence collected after change. Before delivery, impacts are hypotheses with varying confidence. After delivery, they should be tested against actual outcomes.

### Dimensions of impact

Impact can be examined through several dimensions:

- **Direction:** beneficial, harmful, mixed, neutral, or unknown.
- **Magnitude:** minor inconvenience through severe or irreversible consequence.
- **Likelihood:** rare, possible, likely, or already occurring.
- **Duration:** temporary, recurring, long-term, or permanent.
- **Timing:** immediate, delayed, cumulative, or triggered by an exception.
- **Distribution:** broadly shared or concentrated on a subgroup.
- **Directness:** experienced directly or through downstream system effects.
- **Reversibility:** easily corrected, costly to reverse, or irreversible.
- **Control:** within the stakeholder’s control, shared, or imposed.
- **Evidence strength:** observed, measured, reported, modeled, assumed, or unknown.

A single high-medium-low impact score can support sorting, but it should not replace the description. A rare safety impact and a frequent minor inconvenience may both receive “high” under different schemes. Decision-makers need to understand why.

### Current-state, transition, and future-state impact

**Current-state impact** records what stakeholders already experience. This avoids presenting the status quo as neutral. Existing delays, exclusions, risks, manual work, or unfair outcomes matter when comparing change options.

**Transition impact** includes training, migration, dual operation, uncertainty, temporary workload, changed responsibilities, data conversion, communication, and support needs. A desirable future state can still fail because transition costs are ignored.

**Future-state impact** includes ongoing outcomes after adoption: new capabilities, changed access, workload redistribution, new controls, different performance measures, support burdens, role changes, or unintended behaviors.

An honest analysis compares alternatives against the current state and includes the cost and risk of getting from one state to another.

### Uneven and cumulative impact

Average impact can conceal inequity. A process that saves most customers two minutes but prevents a small group from accessing an essential service is not adequately described by the average. Segmentation should examine meaningful differences in disability, language, digital access, geography, working pattern, case complexity, legal status, or other context relevant to the service.

Cumulative impact occurs when several individually modest changes combine. Repeated authentication, multiple data requests, small workload transfers, or successive policy changes can create significant burden. Stakeholder analysis should consider the wider journey and recent change history where relevant.

### Impact and risk

Impact analysis describes consequences for stakeholders. Risk analysis considers uncertainty affecting objectives and value. They overlap but are not identical. A known increase in staff workload is an impact; uncertainty about whether capacity can absorb it creates risk. A privacy breach is a possible harmful event; additional identity checks may be a certain impact even when they reduce that risk.

The analyst should avoid using “risk” to make a known cost sound optional or using an impact estimate as if it were proven. State evidence and uncertainty clearly.

### Conceptual example

A public service replaces paper applications with a digital-first process. Many applicants gain faster confirmation and fewer postal delays. People without reliable internet, with limited digital skills, or requiring assisted support may face greater effort or exclusion. Caseworkers may receive cleaner data but also more support calls. Fraud teams may gain validation controls, while privacy teams face new data-retention questions. Local advice organizations may absorb work if official support is insufficient.

The correct conclusion is not simply “positive impact.” The analysis must show distribution, transition, support needs, risks, and the evidence required to validate expected benefits.

### Impact profile

A controlled impact entry can record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder or segment | Who experiences the consequence? |
| Current-state effect | What happens today? |
| Proposed change | What is expected to differ? |
| Impact description | What outcome, experience, right, work, cost, or risk changes? |
| Direction and magnitude | Beneficial, harmful, mixed, and how material? |
| Timing and duration | When and for how long? |
| Distribution | Which subgroups experience it differently? |
| Evidence and confidence | What supports the claim? |
| Mitigation or enablement | What reduces harm or enables benefit? |
| Owner and measure | Who responds and how will actual impact be checked? |

### Quality checks

- Current-state harm and cost are included, not just change impacts.
- Transition and future-state impacts are separated.
- Benefits and harms are both considered.
- Distribution and accessibility are examined rather than relying only on averages.
- Low-influence stakeholders with high impact receive appropriate attention.
- Claims identify evidence, confidence, owner, and validation measure.
- Irreversible, rights-related, safety, privacy, and livelihood impacts receive proportionate scrutiny.

### Common failure modes

**Benefit-only analysis:** documenting intended gains while treating costs and harms as resistance.

**Average-user analysis:** hiding groups with different access, needs, or consequences.

**Future-state fixation:** ignoring migration, learning, temporary workload, and service continuity.

**Impact-interest confusion:** assuming a quiet stakeholder will not be materially affected.

**False precision:** assigning exact impact numbers without a defensible method or evidence.

### Advanced note: impact, power, and procedural fairness

Stakeholders who bear consequences should have a proportionate opportunity to inform the analysis, even if they do not own the decision. This is a principle of procedural fairness: participation does not guarantee the preferred outcome, but relevant perspectives, evidence, and objections should be heard and addressed transparently.

High impact does not automatically create unlimited authority. Safety experts, elected bodies, service owners, courts, regulators, and affected people may hold different legitimate roles. Good governance clarifies how evidence from affected groups enters the decision and how unresolved concerns are recorded, mitigated, or escalated.

## BA-02-C01-T05 - Power-interest

### Plain-language explanation

A power-interest map places stakeholders according to two questions: how much ability they have to affect the work, and how much concern or involvement they have in it. The map helps a team think about engagement attention. It does not determine whose interests are morally important, who has legal rights, or who may be ignored.

The familiar four-quadrant model is useful because it is simple. That simplicity is also its main limitation. Power and interest are contextual, uncertain, dynamic, and sometimes multidimensional. A map should begin discussion, not end analysis.

### Formal definition

A power-interest map is a provisional stakeholder-classification technique that combines an assessed level of power or influence with an assessed level of interest to inform proportionate engagement planning.

The axes must be defined for the specific initiative. **Power** may mean formal decision authority, resource control, delivery influence, adoption influence, or another stated mechanism. **Interest** may mean concern, involvement, exposure, motivation, or obligation. If participants use different meanings, the placements are unreliable.

### The four conventional categories

```text
High power, high interest: manage closely through timely, substantive involvement.
High power, lower interest: keep satisfied and engage when their authority or concerns are relevant.
Lower power, high interest: consult or involve so knowledge and affected perspectives shape the work.
Lower power, lower interest: keep appropriately informed and monitor for change.
```

The labels are engagement heuristics, not commands. “Manage closely” does not mean manipulate. “Keep satisfied” does not mean hide bad news. “Keep informed” does not mean broadcast irrelevant messages. “Lower power” never means lower human value, fewer rights, or permission to exclude.

### A disciplined mapping method

1. **Define scope and purpose.** State the decision or change and why the map is needed.
2. **Define both axes.** Specify what power and interest mean in this context.
3. **Use identified evidence.** Draw on roles, authority, dependencies, observed involvement, impact, and validated stakeholder input.
4. **Place provisionally.** Use ranges or confidence markers when evidence is weak.
5. **Discuss exceptions.** Identify stakeholders whose impact, rights, expertise, or veto role makes the quadrant misleading.
6. **Derive engagement needs.** Decide the information, participation, accessibility, timing, and feedback appropriate to the relationship.
7. **Validate.** Review placements and assumptions with relevant participants without exposing sensitive judgments unnecessarily.
8. **Revisit.** Update after scope changes, decisions, leadership changes, conflict, new evidence, or movement between lifecycle stages.

### Scoring and calibration

For large stakeholder sets, a defined scale can improve consistency. A team might score power and interest from one to five. Each point needs observable anchors. For example:

```text
Power 1: can offer perspective but has no identified mechanism to alter the initiative.
Power 3: controls a meaningful dependency or can materially shape a recommendation.
Power 5: holds formal approval, veto, funding, regulatory, or equivalent outcome authority.

Interest 1: little current involvement or exposure within the defined scope.
Interest 3: recurring concern, dependency, or involvement in a material part of the work.
Interest 5: substantial outcome exposure, duty, active involvement, or sustained concern.
```

These are examples, not universal scales. The team must adapt anchors to context. A numeric average should not erase disagreement. If operations rates a stakeholder’s influence as five and the sponsor rates it as two, the disagreement is evidence worth investigating.

### Adding impact without corrupting the map

Power-interest mapping can marginalize people with low power and high impact. The analyst should maintain impact as a separate dimension. Options include color, symbol size, an adjacent impact table, or an explicit high-impact exception list.

Do not simply replace interest with impact without renaming the axis. Interest describes concern or involvement; impact describes consequences. They often correlate but can differ sharply.

### From quadrant to engagement design

The map becomes useful only when translated into an engagement approach. For each stakeholder or group, specify:

- the purpose of engagement;
- the questions or decisions they can inform;
- the information they need;
- the participation level: inform, consult, involve, collaborate, assure, or decide;
- the timing and frequency;
- accessibility, language, confidentiality, and channel needs;
- who owns the relationship;
- how feedback will be acknowledged and traced;
- what change triggers a review.

This is still theory planning, not the detailed interview or workshop procedure taught in later chapters.

### Conceptual example

A company changes expense approval. The chief financial officer has high formal power and high interest in control outcomes. The payroll team may have moderate formal power but high operational influence and high interest. Most employees have low formal power but high interest and broad impact. Internal audit may have high assurance influence but lower day-to-day interest. A software vendor controls a technical dependency but owns no internal policy decision.

The map suggests attention, but the engagement plan must also reflect impact, evidence, and decision rights. Employees cannot be ignored because they lack approval power, and the vendor cannot decide policy because it controls a platform.

### Quality checks

- Scope, time, and both axes are explicitly defined.
- Ratings name evidence and confidence.
- Impact, rights, expertise, and decision authority are not collapsed into the two axes.
- Quadrant labels are translated into ethical, two-way engagement.
- People are not described with insulting or politically risky labels.
- The map has restricted access if it contains sensitive relationship judgments.
- Review triggers and ownership are recorded.

### Common failure modes

**Quadrant determinism:** treating placement as an objective fact that automatically dictates behavior.

**Ignore-the-low-low error:** excluding a stakeholder who later becomes affected, interested, or influential.

**Power worship:** prioritizing senior stakeholders while neglecting users, workers, communities, or specialists who hold material evidence.

**Static mapping:** failing to update after decisions and impacts become visible.

**Covert strategy:** using the map to manipulate people rather than plan transparent participation.

**Score theater:** debating whether a rating is 3.5 or 4 without evidence or a decision use.

### Advanced note: multidimensional and dynamic maps

Complex initiatives may require separate maps for authorization, delivery, adoption, risk, and public legitimacy. Trying to compress all forms of power into one axis can hide essential distinctions. A stakeholder may move between maps or lifecycle stages.

Dynamic analysis records direction of change: rising interest, declining authority, emerging opposition, increasing impact, or a new dependency. The map then becomes a time-sensitive hypothesis about the stakeholder system rather than a permanent portrait of people.

## BA-02-C01-T06 - Personas

### Plain-language explanation

A persona is an evidence-based representation of a meaningful pattern among users or stakeholders. It helps a team remember goals, behaviors, contexts, constraints, and needs that might otherwise be flattened into the word “user.” A persona is not a fictional biography invented to make a presentation attractive.

Personas are useful when many individuals share relevant patterns and the team needs a compact way to reason about them. They are dangerous when based on stereotypes, unsupported demographics, a few memorable participants, or internal assumptions. Personas complement stakeholder identification; they do not replace direct research, inclusive participation, segmentation data, or named decision roles.

### Formal definition

A persona is a research-grounded, purpose-specific synthesis of recurring characteristics, behaviors, goals, needs, barriers, and contexts within a relevant stakeholder or user group, used to support shared analysis and design decisions.

The definition contains several controls:

- **Research-grounded:** claims trace to evidence.
- **Purpose-specific:** only characteristics relevant to the decision are included.
- **Synthesis:** a persona represents patterns, not one actual participant.
- **Recurring:** the pattern appears across sufficient evidence, not a single anecdote.
- **Decision use:** the persona exists to improve analysis, not decorate an artifact.

### Personas, roles, segments, and profiles

A **role** describes responsibility or relationship, such as caseworker, approver, or customer.

A **segment** groups people using defined criteria, often demographic, behavioral, operational, or value-based. Segments may be quantitatively measured.

A **profile** is a factual summary of a group or participant type and may remain close to observed attributes.

A **persona** turns validated patterns into a coherent representation that teams can use to reason about situations and decisions.

These forms can coexist. A customer segment based on channel behavior may contain multiple personas because people use the same channel for different goals or under different constraints. One persona may cross demographic categories. The analyst chooses the form that best supports the question.

### Evidence appropriate for personas

Evidence can include interviews, observation, contextual research, support contacts, complaints, service analytics, accessibility research, surveys, operational records, prior studies, and validated subject-matter input. Later BA-M02 chapters explain these methods; this chapter establishes what evidence a persona must preserve.

Quantitative data can show frequency and distribution. Qualitative evidence can explain context, goals, workarounds, language, and meaning. Strong personas often combine both. A behavior observed in three participants should not be described as common across a population without appropriate evidence.

### Persona components

A controlled persona may include:

- stable persona ID and descriptive label;
- purpose and decisions it supports;
- relevant group or population;
- goals and desired outcomes;
- behaviors and current approaches;
- context, environment, and triggering situations;
- needs and barriers;
- accessibility, assistance, or channel considerations;
- relevant knowledge, confidence, or capability;
- evidence sources and sample limitations;
- confidence, last-reviewed date, and owner;
- statements explicitly excluded because they are unsupported or irrelevant.

Names, photographs, age, family, hobbies, or quotes should be included only when evidence-based, relevant, lawful, and safe. Decorative details can create false realism and invite stereotyping.

### Proto-personas and evidence status

Sometimes a team begins with hypotheses before research. These should be labeled **proto-personas**, assumption personas, or provisional profiles. They are useful for making assumptions visible and defining research questions. They are not evidence of user needs.

A clear status model is:

```text
HYPOTHESIS: based mainly on internal assumptions; research required.
EMERGING: supported by limited evidence; important uncertainty remains.
VALIDATED FOR PURPOSE: sufficient evidence for the stated decision and scope.
STALE: context or evidence has changed; do not rely on it until reviewed.
RETIRED: no longer represents a relevant pattern or decision need.
```

“Validated” is bounded. A persona validated for redesigning appointment reminders may not be valid for determining clinical consent or payment behavior.

### Avoiding stereotypes and exclusion

Personas become harmful when they treat demographic identity as destiny, use one participant as a universal representative, omit people with access needs, or describe stakeholders in demeaning language. Characteristics should be connected to evidence and decision relevance.

For example, “older users dislike technology” is a stereotype. A defensible finding might be: “Participants who rarely use online identity services required additional recovery support, regardless of age.” The second statement identifies a behavior and service need that can be investigated and addressed.

Inclusive persona work asks who is missing from the research, which needs are rare but critical, whether combined characteristics create different barriers, and whether a persona causes the team to ignore variation within the group. Sometimes an accessibility profile, edge-case analysis, or separate impact segment is more appropriate than a persona.

### Privacy and confidentiality

Persona source material may contain personal, sensitive, or confidential information. Synthesis does not automatically make it safe. Rare combinations of role, location, condition, or quotation may allow re-identification.

Use data minimization, authorized access, informed research practices, appropriate retention, secure storage, and careful aggregation. Do not place raw participant details in a widely shared persona. Avoid exact quotes when they are identifying or unnecessary. Record evidence links in a controlled location rather than exposing sensitive source data in the published representation.

### Conceptual example

A benefits service discovers a recurring pattern among applicants who can start online but cannot reliably complete identity verification without help. The useful persona is not “Mary, age 67, likes gardening.” It is an evidence-based profile such as “Assisted verifier,” characterized by the goal of completing a claim independently where possible, intermittent access to required documents, low confidence in recovery steps, concern about losing progress, and reliance on a trusted helper or service agent.

The persona helps the team examine save-and-return behavior, consent when helpers are involved, accessible guidance, alternative verification routes, and support handoffs. Evidence sources, variation, and limitations remain attached.

### Quality checks

- The persona has a stated analytical purpose and decision use.
- Every material claim traces to research or is labeled as a hypothesis.
- Behaviors, goals, needs, and contexts receive more weight than decoration.
- Variation, exceptions, accessibility, and underserved groups are considered.
- The persona does not replace direct stakeholder participation.
- Privacy, consent, re-identification, retention, and access are controlled.
- Status, owner, evidence date, and review trigger are visible.
- Teams test whether the persona changes or improves an actual decision.

### Common failure modes

**Fiction presented as research:** inventing plausible details and calling the result evidence-based.

**Average persona:** combining incompatible traits into a person who represents nobody.

**Demographic stereotype:** assuming age, gender, occupation, disability, or culture determines behavior without evidence.

**Persona substitution:** refusing further research because a persona already exists.

**False permanence:** reusing a persona after services, policies, or user behavior have changed.

**Privacy leakage:** including enough detail or quotation to identify a participant.

### Advanced note: personas as boundary objects

A well-controlled persona can act as a boundary object: different disciplines use the same representation to discuss a stakeholder pattern while retaining their specialized concerns. Designers consider interactions, analysts consider needs and rules, operations considers support, security considers risk, and decision-makers consider outcomes.

The persona succeeds only if those interpretations remain connected to evidence. When the narrative becomes more memorable than the research, the boundary object turns into mythology. Evidence repositories, confidence statements, and periodic review preserve the connection.

## BA-02-C01-T07 - Decision rights

### Plain-language explanation

Decision rights state who is authorized to make which decision, within what limits, after receiving what input, and with what accountability. Stakeholder analysis is incomplete when it records interest and influence but leaves authority ambiguous.

A stakeholder can be deeply affected, highly knowledgeable, or very influential without owning the final decision. Conversely, a decision owner may be required to consult specialists, users, employee representatives, regulators, or assurance bodies before acting. Clarifying decision rights prevents recommendations from being mistaken for approvals and prevents authority from becoming detached from evidence and responsibility.

### Formal definition

Decision rights are the explicit allocation of authority, accountability, participation, assurance, escalation, and communication responsibilities for a defined class of decisions within stated boundaries.

Decision rights should answer:

```text
What decision is being made?
Who prepares and recommends?
Who must contribute evidence or expertise?
Who must be consulted?
Who performs assurance or verifies conditions?
Who has final authority to approve, reject, or defer?
What limits apply to that authority?
Who implements and communicates the decision?
Where is disagreement or out-of-bound risk escalated?
How is the decision and its rationale recorded?
```

### Authority, accountability, responsibility, and influence

**Authority** is permission to make or approve a decision.

**Accountability** is answerability for the decision or outcome within governance arrangements.

**Responsibility** is an obligation to perform work or provide an input.

**Influence** is the capacity to shape the decision or its implementation.

These can belong to different stakeholders. A business analyst may be responsible for analysis and recommendation. A security specialist may have authority to approve a security exception. A service owner may be accountable for the service. A finance body may authorize funding. A delivery team may decide implementation details within agreed boundaries.

### Decision granularity

“The sponsor decides” is rarely sufficient because initiatives contain many decisions:

- problem definition and desired outcomes;
- scope and priority;
- funding and resource allocation;
- policy and business rules;
- data collection, access, retention, and use;
- architecture and technical design;
- security, safety, legal, accessibility, and risk acceptance;
- product or service behavior;
- operational readiness and deployment;
- acceptance of deliverables;
- response to incidents or failed outcomes.

The same person should not automatically own every decision. Specialist and statutory authority cannot be assumed by a general sponsor. Segregation of duties may deliberately divide recommendation, approval, execution, and assurance.

### Decision-rights record

A practical conceptual record includes:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Decision ID and statement | The exact choice or class of choices |
| Trigger and deadline | When the decision is required |
| Decision owner | Role with final authority within scope |
| Recommender | Role preparing the advised option |
| Contributors | Stakeholders providing evidence or expertise |
| Required consultees | People or bodies whose input must be sought |
| Assurance or veto roles | Authorities that verify conditions or can stop progression |
| Delegation boundary | Financial, legal, technical, geographic, or risk limits |
| Escalation route | Authority for decisions outside the boundary or unresolved conflict |
| Evidence and criteria | Basis on which options will be judged |
| Record and communication | Where rationale, approval, conditions, and recipients are documented |

Matrices such as RACI, RASCI, RAPID, or organization-specific governance models can help, but labels must be defined. A RACI “Accountable” role does not always equal the legal or financial decision authority. Never assume a framework’s letter has the same meaning across organizations.

### Consultation is not approval

Consultation means seeking and considering input before a decision. It does not promise that every preference will be adopted. Ethical consultation explains what is open to influence, what is already constrained, who decides, how input will be used, and how participants will learn the outcome.

False consultation occurs when the decision is already fixed but participation is presented as influential, or when affected people are asked for input that is never examined. This damages trust and produces weak evidence. If engagement is for information, validation, or implementation planning rather than option choice, say so.

### Delegation and escalation

Decision authority often has boundaries. A product manager may prioritize within an approved budget but not change statutory policy. A team may release low-risk changes but require review for personal-data use. An operations manager may approve temporary staffing but not a permanent organizational redesign.

Delegation should specify thresholds and conditions. Escalation is appropriate when:

- the decision exceeds delegated cost, scope, risk, or policy limits;
- required authorities disagree;
- critical evidence is absent or contested;
- a legal, ethical, safety, security, or rights concern cannot be resolved locally;
- the accountable owner is unavailable and delay has material consequences;
- a proposed action conflicts with approved objectives or governance.

Escalation is not a substitute for analysis. The analyst should present the decision, evidence, options, unresolved conflict, consequences of delay, and requested authority.

### Decision traceability

A decision record should preserve:

- the question decided;
- date, owner, and authority basis;
- options considered;
- criteria and material evidence;
- assumptions and uncertainty;
- consulted stakeholders and unresolved objections;
- decision and rationale;
- conditions, actions, and review triggers;
- links to affected requirements, plans, risks, and outcomes.

Traceability supports learning and accountability. It prevents later participants from treating an old decision as timeless or repeating debates without understanding the earlier context. It also allows a decision to be revisited when assumptions fail.

### Conceptual example

An organization considers using automated eligibility checks. The policy owner decides the interpretation of eligibility rules within law. Legal counsel advises on statutory constraints but may not own service design. The data owner decides authorized access under governance. Security approves controls within delegated standards. The service owner is accountable for end-to-end delivery. A product manager prioritizes implementation within approved boundaries. The analyst synthesizes evidence and traces requirements but does not silently approve policy, privacy risk, or deployment.

Affected applicants and caseworkers contribute indispensable evidence. Their participation does not replace formal authority, but formal authority is not credible if it ignores material evidence about access, fairness, exceptions, or operational reality.

### Quality checks

- Each material decision is stated clearly and at useful granularity.
- One final decision owner is identifiable for each decision within scope.
- Authority has a documented basis and explicit limits.
- Recommendation, consultation, assurance, approval, execution, and communication are distinguished.
- Statutory, specialist, or segregation-of-duties controls are preserved.
- Affected stakeholders have a defined route to contribute evidence.
- Escalation routes and time-critical contingencies are known.
- Decisions, rationale, objections, conditions, and review triggers are traceable.

### Common failure modes

**Consensus as authority:** assuming agreement in a meeting creates formal approval.

**Sponsor owns everything:** assigning decisions beyond the sponsor’s legal, technical, financial, or risk authority.

**Analyst overreach:** presenting analysis or recommendation as an authorized decision.

**Matrix without meaning:** filling a responsibility chart whose labels and decision scope are undefined.

**Committee diffusion:** involving many approvers so no one is clearly accountable.

**Silent veto:** allowing an undocumented stakeholder to block work without a transparent authority basis.

**Escalation avoidance:** repeatedly delaying because the correct decision owner has not been engaged.

### Advanced note: decision rights as organizational architecture

Decision rights shape organizational behavior. Centralized authority can improve consistency and control but may slow learning and ignore local context. Delegated authority can accelerate response but may create variation or unmanaged risk. Effective governance places decisions near relevant knowledge while preserving accountability and escalation for consequences beyond local boundaries.

Stakeholder analysis helps reveal whether decision rights match the real system. If formal owners lack information, capacity, or access, informal actors may make de facto decisions. If teams are accountable for outcomes but cannot control necessary resources, governance is misaligned. These findings may require organizational change, not merely a better stakeholder list.

## Integrated stakeholder-analysis model

The seven topics can be applied as a repeating reasoning cycle:

1. Define the situation, decision, change boundary, and time horizon.
2. Identify stakeholders systematically across value, work, authority, knowledge, dependency, rights, and lifecycle.
3. Describe interests using evidence and separate them from positions and preferred solutions.
4. Analyze influence by mechanism and stage rather than title alone.
5. Assess current-state, transition, and future-state impacts, including uneven and cumulative effects.
6. Use power-interest mapping provisionally to focus engagement while applying impact, rights, and expertise exceptions.
7. Create personas only where evidence-based patterns improve decisions; retain direct participation and source traceability.
8. Clarify decision rights, required consultation, assurance, delegation limits, and escalation.
9. Design proportionate engagement and evidence collection for later elicitation work.
10. Review the analysis when scope, people, evidence, decisions, or context changes.

The output is not one perfect diagram. It is a controlled set of claims that explains who matters to the analysis, why they matter, how their relationship differs, and how their evidence and authority enter decisions.

## Integrated conceptual example

A regional transport authority is considering account-based ticketing. The initial business concern is slow boarding and the cost of maintaining older ticket equipment. The proposed change would allow passengers to tap a bank card or digital token and have fares calculated later.

Identification finds passengers, occasional visitors, people without bank cards, disabled travelers, concession-card holders, drivers, station staff, inspectors, customer support, finance, fraud operations, privacy and security teams, transport operators, payment providers, local government, accessibility advocates, unions, regulators, and teams that reconcile revenue.

Interest analysis shows different concerns: passengers want reliable and understandable fares; finance wants accurate settlement; drivers want fewer disputes; privacy specialists care about proportional data use; unbanked travelers need equitable access; operators need predictable revenue; support teams need traceable corrections.

Influence analysis shows that formal approval sits with the authority, payment providers control dependencies, operators influence implementation, staff influence adoption and exception handling, and passenger trust determines actual use. Accessibility and regulatory bodies may possess assurance or enforcement influence even if they are not daily participants.

Impact analysis separates faster boarding and easier travel from risks such as exclusion, mistaken charges, difficult refunds, surveillance concerns, new support burdens, and transition problems. Average passenger benefit cannot erase severe impact on people unable to use the default channel.

A power-interest map helps plan attention, but an impact overlay elevates groups with low formal power and high consequence. Evidence-based personas may describe patterns such as frequent multimodal commuters, occasional visitors unfamiliar with fare rules, assisted travelers, and cash-dependent passengers. These patterns require research and must not become stereotypes.

Decision rights distinguish policy approval, fare rules, privacy authorization, technical assurance, contract decisions, operational readiness, and go-live authority. The analyst prepares evidence and connects stakeholder needs to decisions but does not claim specialist approvals.

This integrated analysis does not decide the solution. It creates the conditions for responsible elicitation in interviews, workshops, observation, and research—the subjects of the following chapters.

## Common misconceptions

### “A stakeholder is anyone who receives project emails”

False. Communication recipients may include administrators with little material relationship, while important affected groups may receive no communication. Stakeholder status comes from relationship to the scope, not mailing-list membership.

### “The sponsor can identify every stakeholder”

False. Sponsors provide an essential starting view but may not see operational exceptions, external dependencies, affected populations, informal networks, or specialist assurance needs.

### “High interest means strong support”

False. Interest measures concern, involvement, obligation, or exposure. Strong opposition can reflect high interest.

### “Power is the same as job seniority”

False. Authority, expertise, dependency, adoption, legitimacy, networks, and public voice create different kinds of influence.

### “Low-power stakeholders can be informed later”

Unsafe. People with little organizational power may experience the greatest impact or hold essential operational evidence. Participation should reflect materiality, rights, evidence, and impact as well as power.

### “A persona is a creative fictional customer”

False. A controlled persona synthesizes research-supported patterns for a stated decision purpose. Fiction presented as research creates false confidence.

### “Consultation means stakeholders decide”

False. Consultation provides input. Decision rights specify authority. Responsible engagement makes the distinction clear without using it to dismiss evidence.

### “A RACI chart automatically clarifies governance”

False. A matrix helps only when the decision, roles, labels, authority basis, boundaries, and escalation routes are explicit.

### “Stakeholder analysis is completed at kickoff”

False. Stakeholders, interests, influence, impacts, personas, and decision rights can all change. Analysis must be reviewed throughout the lifecycle.

## Limitations and ethical boundaries

Stakeholder analysis is necessarily incomplete. People and organizations are complex, evidence is uneven, context changes, and classifications simplify reality. A useful model supports decisions while remaining open to correction.

Power and influence analysis can be sensitive. Poorly governed artifacts may damage relationships, expose confidential information, or be used to manipulate stakeholders. Access should be proportionate. Descriptions should remain factual, respectful, relevant, and evidence-based. Sensitive personal speculation should not be recorded.

Participation also has limits. Time, cost, privacy, safety, accessibility, representation, and research ethics affect who can be engaged and how. An analyst should document whose perspective is absent, why, what representation or evidence is used instead, and how the limitation affects confidence.

Stakeholder analysis cannot resolve every conflict. It can make interests, impacts, evidence, influence, and authority visible. Legitimate decision-makers must still make choices and accept accountability for trade-offs.

## Advanced synthesis

Stakeholder analysis can be understood as assurance for the human and institutional architecture of change. Requirements, process models, data definitions, and solution designs are only as credible as the participation and authority system that produced them. If the wrong people were consulted, affected groups were hidden, influence was mistaken for authority, or decision ownership was unclear, technically precise artifacts may still encode the wrong problem.

The analysis should preserve three forms of legitimacy:

**Epistemic legitimacy** asks whether the work drew on relevant knowledge and evidence. Frontline staff, users, specialists, records, and operational data may all contribute.

**Procedural legitimacy** asks whether affected and responsible stakeholders had a fair, transparent, and proportionate route to participate, challenge, assure, or decide.

**Authority legitimacy** asks whether decisions were made by roles with valid authority inside stated boundaries and with required approvals.

These forms reinforce one another but are not substitutes. A powerful executive cannot replace user evidence. Broad consultation cannot replace legal authorization. Specialist advice cannot replace accountable service ownership. A good analyst designs the stakeholder system so that knowledge, participation, and authority connect visibly.

Stakeholder analysis is also an uncertainty model. Identification may be incomplete; interests may be inferred; influence may change; impact may be estimated; personas may be emerging; authority may be disputed. Instead of hiding uncertainty, the analyst records confidence and directs elicitation toward the most consequential unknowns.

The strongest output is therefore not the most elaborate map. It is a maintainable explanation that helps a team answer: Are material perspectives represented? Do we know what is evidence and what is assumption? Are high-impact groups protected from being ignored? Do engagement choices fit the relationship? Can everyone tell who advises, assures, decides, and escalates? Will we notice when the model becomes stale?

## Reference-style evidence reinforcement

Stakeholder analysis is iterative because interest, influence, impact, and decision authority change across a change lifecycle. IIBA treats stakeholders, needs, value, context, and change as connected concepts rather than a static contact list [1]. UK government service guidance similarly requires teams to understand users and affected groups through evidence rather than assumption [2]. A reviewer should therefore expect a dated map, stated evidence, uncertain classifications, and a plan for refreshing it [3].

| Review question | Evidence expected | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Who is affected? | Process, service, data, and impact analysis | Only senior sponsors are listed |
| Who decides? | Decision-right record and escalation path | Attendance is confused with authority |
| Who may be missing? | Inclusion review and outreach evidence | Personas replace contact with real groups |
