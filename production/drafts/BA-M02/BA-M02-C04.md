## Purpose

Business analysis requires evidence about what people do, what rules and records say, what a wider population reports, and how work changes in its real setting. Interviews and workshops reveal valuable accounts, language, and group reasoning, but reported practice may differ from observable practice. Observation and research methods help the analyst examine that difference without assuming that one source is automatically true.

This chapter treats observation, job shadowing, document analysis, surveys, contextual inquiry, and evidence as one disciplined research system. Each method produces a different view. Observation reveals visible behavior and context. Job shadowing follows a role and its work over time. Document analysis exposes formal rules and recorded organizational memory. Surveys gather standardized responses across a defined sample. Contextual inquiry combines observation with questions in the participant's environment. Evidence practice connects all sources to claims, confidence, decisions, privacy controls, and limitations.

Research is not passive collection. The analyst's question affects what is noticed, the method affects what can be known, access determines whose reality is visible, and interpretation creates risk. Good research therefore makes purpose, selection, method, provenance, uncertainty, and ethical boundaries explicit.

This chapter covers Phase 1 theory only. It does not include fieldwork exercises, assignments, practical procedures, labs, case studies, assessment questions, capstone work, or release artifacts.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Select an observation approach appropriate to the analytical question, setting, risk, and required depth.
- Explain how job shadowing reveals role-level workflows, interruptions, handoffs, tacit knowledge, and work variation.
- Analyze documents for purpose, authority, scope, currency, internal consistency, and relationship to actual practice.
- Evaluate whether a survey is suitable and reason about constructs, questions, sampling, response quality, analysis, and inference.
- Distinguish contextual inquiry from silent observation, interviews, and usability testing.
- Build a traceable evidence chain from source through observation and interpretation to finding and decision.
- Protect consent, privacy, confidentiality, accessibility, safety, and dignity throughout research.
- State limitations and uncertainty without overstating what the available evidence supports.

## The multi-method evidence chain

The six controlled topics form a connected model:

```text
Observation records behavior, sequence, artifacts, conditions, and environment.
Job shadowing follows a role across connected work and time.
Document analysis examines formal and informal records of rules, intent, and history.
Surveys collect standardized self-report data from a defined sample.
Contextual inquiry combines situated observation with purposeful clarification.
Evidence controls determine what claims those sources can responsibly support.
```

No method is a universal truth detector. A policy can be authoritative about what should happen but inaccurate about current practice. An observed session can be accurate but atypical. A survey percentage can be precise but biased by sampling or question interpretation. A participant can explain motives that are invisible to the observer but may not remember every action. Strong analysis uses the right method for the claim and combines sources when their limitations differ.

## BA-02-C04-T01 - Observation

### Plain-language explanation

Observation is the deliberate study of activity as it occurs. The analyst watches people, processes, systems, artifacts, environments, interactions, and exceptions to understand actual work. Observation helps reveal actions people omit from descriptions because they are routine, difficult to explain, unofficial, or taken for granted.

### Formal definition

Observation is a planned research technique in which the analyst records visible or otherwise authorized phenomena in a defined setting, using an explicit focus and level of participation, then separates the record of what occurred from interpretation of its meaning.

### What observation can and cannot show

Observation can reveal sequence, duration, movement, tool switching, waiting, interruption, collaboration, handoffs, physical constraints, workarounds, error recovery, information use, environmental conditions, and differences between prescribed and enacted process. It is useful when people cannot easily recall detailed actions or when documentation describes an idealized process.

Observation cannot directly reveal intention, belief, knowledge, emotion, or cause. An analyst may see a worker reopen a record three times but cannot know whether the cause is uncertainty, poor navigation, a control requirement, slow loading, or interruption without further evidence. Observable behavior and analyst interpretation must remain distinct.

Observation also does not establish prevalence from a few sessions. Seeing an exception once proves that it can occur in the observed circumstances, not how frequently it occurs across all work. Frequency claims need an appropriate sample, operational data, systematic structured observation, or other measurement.

### Observation modes

Observation varies along several dimensions:

- **Direct or indirect:** watching activity as it occurs, or examining authorized recordings, logs, traces, or artifacts later.
- **Naturalistic or arranged:** observing ordinary activity in its usual setting, or asking participants to complete selected work under arranged conditions.
- **Participant or non-participant:** joining parts of the activity, or remaining outside it.
- **Structured or exploratory:** recording defined behaviors against a protocol, or observing broadly to discover relevant patterns.
- **Continuous or sampled:** following the whole period, or observing selected intervals or events.
- **Overt or covert:** participants know about the observation, or do not. Business-analysis research should normally be overt; covert observation carries substantial legal, ethical, employment, and trust risks and requires explicit specialist governance.

The method should match the question. Exploratory observation may identify unknown steps during discovery. Structured observation may later count defined handoff failures. Combining the results without acknowledging that the protocols differ would be misleading.

### Planning observation

A defensible observation plan states:

1. The decision or analytical question.
2. What activity, role, environment, event, or artifact will be observed.
3. Participant and setting selection, including relevant variation.
4. Observation mode and the researcher's role.
5. What will and will not be recorded.
6. Consent, privacy, confidentiality, safety, and access controls.
7. Note-taking or recording method and identifiers.
8. How researcher influence and observer bias will be considered.
9. How observations will be analyzed, validated, retained, and deleted.
10. Stop conditions for distress, emergencies, sensitive information, or unsafe activity.

Selection should capture conditions likely to change work: experienced and new staff, different shifts, sites, channels, case types, workload levels, devices, accessibility needs, and normal versus exception handling. Convenience observation of one cooperative high performer should not be described as “the current process.”

### Recording what happens

Notes should distinguish at least four layers:

```text
Observation: what was seen or heard, with time and context.
Participant explanation: what the participant said it meant.
Analyst interpretation: a possible meaning or relationship.
Follow-up need: evidence required to clarify or test the interpretation.
```

Useful records include session and participant codes, timestamps, process step, action, tool or artifact, trigger, input, output, interruption, handoff, exception, quote, environmental condition, and follow-up question. One atomic observation per note helps later analysis. Loaded language such as “user became confused” should be replaced with the behavior: “participant returned to the previous screen twice, paused for 18 seconds, and asked which address was required.”

Photos, video, audio, screen capture, and system traces can add detail but greatly increase privacy and security exposure. Obtain the required consent and organizational authorization. Exclude unrelated people, customer identifiers, passwords, medical information, confidential screens, and commercially sensitive material. Confirm consent when the recording scope changes.

### Reactivity and observer bias

People may change behavior because they know they are observed. This is reactivity, not participant dishonesty. The observer also changes the environment through questions, movement, dress, status, equipment, or association with management. Allowing time for adjustment, minimizing interruption, observing multiple occasions, and documenting researcher role can reduce or expose the effect but not remove it.

Observers selectively notice what fits their expectations. Use a protocol, record negative cases, compare notes between researchers, preserve raw records, and conduct reflective debriefs. A second observer adds perspective but may also increase participant pressure. Method quality requires balancing analytical value against intrusion.

### Conceptual example

Managers say invoice approval requires four minutes. Observation shows that the approval click is brief, but staff spend substantial time finding missing purchase-order information, contacting requesters, switching between systems, and rechecking status after interruption. The observation does not prove why every invoice is delayed. It changes the process model from a single approval task to a larger evidence-and-coordination flow that can be tested with system data and participant clarification.

### Quality checks and failure modes

Observation is credible when its purpose and setting are defined, consent and boundaries are clear, records distinguish behavior from inference, relevant variation is sampled, unexpected activity remains visible, and claims do not exceed the observed scope.

Failures include arriving without a research question, treating observation as employee surveillance, recording identifiers unnecessarily, asking continuous questions that transform the activity, generalizing from one session, correcting the participant during observation, ignoring inaccessible or off-system work, and presenting inferred motives as facts.

### Advanced note: the observed unit

Analysts often assume the unit is a person completing a task. The more useful unit may be an event, case, handoff, interruption, decision, document, or interaction between human and system. Defining the observed unit changes what can be compared and prevents unrelated behaviors from being counted together.

## BA-02-C04-T02 - Job Shadowing

### Plain-language explanation

Job shadowing is sustained observation of a person or role through connected work. Instead of examining one isolated task, the analyst follows how work arrives, competes for attention, moves between tools and people, and changes across the day or another meaningful period.

### Formal definition

Job shadowing is an overt, planned form of role-centered observation in which a researcher accompanies a participant across a defined work period to understand responsibilities, decisions, interactions, artifacts, interruptions, variation, and tacit practices in context.

### Why role-level continuity matters

Process diagrams often isolate a named activity. Real work crosses boundaries. A claims handler may answer calls, monitor queues, seek specialist advice, manage urgent cases, correct data, mentor colleagues, and resume interrupted work. Shadowing reveals the portfolio of work and the coordination cost between tasks.

It is particularly useful for:

- tacit knowledge that experts no longer verbalize;
- interruptions and recovery effort;
- informal communication and escalation;
- handoffs between roles, teams, and systems;
- workload peaks and competing priorities;
- emotional, cognitive, physical, and environmental demands;
- exception work that procedures treat as rare;
- hidden artifacts such as personal checklists or temporary notes.

Shadowing is not a performance inspection. If participants believe results will be used to judge them, they may hide workarounds, avoid difficult cases, or experience harm. Sponsors, researchers, and participants need a clear statement of purpose, access, reporting, and limits.

### Scope and sampling

“Shadow an agent for a day” is an activity, not a design. Define which questions require continuity, what period captures relevant variation, and how the selected person and day relate to the wider role. Month-end, incident periods, training days, seasonal peaks, remote work, and night shifts may produce different evidence.

Select across relevant experience, location, employment arrangement, shift, caseload, channel, and accessibility need. Do not recruit only manager-recommended model employees. A participant may withdraw or pause the researcher when sensitive work appears; the plan must allow this without penalty.

### Researcher conduct

The researcher should minimize disruption, follow site safety and security rules, and clarify when questions may be asked. Three approaches can be combined:

- observe silently during time-sensitive or concentration-heavy work;
- ask brief clarification at natural pauses;
- reserve reflective questions for scheduled debriefs.

The researcher must not operate systems, advise customers, approve work, view records without authorization, or become an unrecorded extra worker merely to remain helpful. If participation is part of the approved method, the effect on activity must be documented.

Shadowing may expose third parties who did not consent, including customers, patients, suppliers, students, or colleagues. Mask screens, omit calls, use simulated cases, or leave the setting where required. Participant consent alone does not authorize access to other people's data.

### Capturing a workday without flattening it

A useful shadowing record can combine:

- a timeline of activities and interruptions;
- cases or events followed across stages;
- systems and artifacts used;
- people contacted and purpose of contact;
- decision points and information required;
- waiting, rework, switching, and recovery;
- observed workaround with its conditions;
- participant explanation and analyst follow-up;
- workload and environmental context.

Duration alone is an incomplete measure. A two-minute interruption can cause ten minutes of recovery. An “idle” interval may be required waiting, monitoring, or readiness. Shadowing notes should avoid productivity judgments that are not supported by the research purpose.

### Validation and synthesis

At the end or soon afterward, play back selected sequences: “I recorded that you checked three sources before escalating. Is that sequence accurate, and what determines whether each source is needed?” Participant explanation improves meaning but does not erase what was observed. If the account differs, preserve the distinction and seek corroboration.

Compare shadows across contexts, not only people. Repeated behavior may reflect a shared rule, system constraint, training history, or local adaptation. Variation can be more valuable than a common pattern because it exposes conditions the future process must handle.

### Conceptual example

An analyst shadows warehouse coordinators. The formal role appears to allocate work from a queue. Shadowing shows that coordinators also translate ambiguous customer notes, check vehicle capacity through messages, negotiate priorities with sales, and keep temporary lists during system outages. These findings reveal dependencies and resilience work, but the analyst validates them across shifts and records before changing the official role model.

### Quality checks and failure modes

Job shadowing is strong when the period and participant selection match the question, conduct is non-evaluative, connected activities and interruptions are captured, third-party data is protected, and participant meaning is checked without rewriting observation.

Failures include shadowing only a showcase day, following one person and generalizing to the role, collecting performance evidence without disclosure, distracting staff during safety-critical work, ignoring emotional labor, treating waiting as waste, and allowing the researcher to become part of the process without recording that change.

### Advanced note: role, person, and system

Observed behavior belongs neither solely to the individual nor solely to the process. It emerges from role expectations, competence, tools, incentives, relationships, workload, and local conditions. Mature analysis avoids blaming a person for adaptations created by the system while still preserving individual variation that affects requirements.

## BA-02-C04-T03 - Document Analysis

### Plain-language explanation

Document analysis is the systematic examination of written, visual, or recorded artifacts to understand rules, decisions, processes, data, vocabulary, responsibilities, history, and existing requirements. Documents include more than formal policies: forms, templates, training material, reports, contracts, tickets, emails, logs, diagrams, screen content, and prior research may all contain relevant evidence.

### Formal definition

Document analysis is a traceable research method that selects, authenticates, interprets, compares, and extracts evidence from organizational artifacts while evaluating their purpose, authorship, authority, scope, currency, audience, completeness, and relationship to other sources.

### Documents are situated artifacts

A document was created by someone, for an audience, at a time, under constraints, for a purpose. That context affects what it can support. A policy may be authoritative but high level. Training material may explain intended practice but be outdated. A system log may record events precisely but omit human reasoning. A complaint may reveal harm without representing all customers. A template may show required fields but not how people obtain the information.

Do not ask only “What does this document say?” Ask:

- Who created, approved, owns, and uses it?
- What purpose and audience shaped it?
- What authority does it have?
- Which business area, geography, product, channel, or period does it cover?
- Is it current, superseded, draft, or unofficial?
- Which terms, assumptions, exclusions, and dependencies appear?
- What is missing or inconsistent?
- How does observed practice compare?

### Build a document inventory

A document inventory supports control. Fields can include identifier, title, type, version, status, owner, author, approver, creation and review dates, scope, source location, access classification, retention rule, relevance, extracted claims, conflicts, and follow-up owner.

Selection should follow the analytical question. Search both formal repositories and operational artifacts, with authorization. Absence from the official repository may itself reveal weak governance, but it does not grant permission to copy personal notes or messages. Minimize collection and preserve access restrictions.

### Authority, authenticity, and currency

Authority answers whether the artifact can prescribe or approve something. Authenticity asks whether the artifact is what it claims to be and has not been altered outside control. Currency asks whether it applies now. These are separate. An authentic historical policy may no longer be current; a current team checklist may guide practice but lack formal authority.

Check metadata, version history, approval records, links, signatures where relevant, source system, and owner confirmation. A filename containing “FINAL” is not governance evidence. If multiple versions exist, record their relationship rather than selecting the strongest-looking one silently.

### Extracting and comparing information

Create an extraction structure connected to the research questions. It may capture actors, triggers, inputs, outputs, rules, decisions, responsibilities, data elements, exceptions, controls, performance measures, risks, and open questions. Record page, section, paragraph, field, or record reference so another reviewer can locate the source.

Comparison can reveal:

- conflicting definitions;
- policy-practice gaps;
- duplicated or missing responsibilities;
- inconsistent thresholds;
- obsolete system or role names;
- circular references;
- undocumented exceptions;
- controls without an owner;
- requirements without rationale;
- data collected without an evident purpose.

Contradiction is a finding, not an inconvenience. Preserve each source, authority, scope, and date. A formal policy and an observed workaround may both accurately describe different layers of the organization.

### Text is not the entire document

Layout, tables, footnotes, diagrams, headings, form controls, and visual emphasis carry meaning. Automated extraction can scramble columns, omit images, lose comments, or treat hidden text as visible. When layout affects interpretation, inspect the rendered artifact and validate critical fields manually. Optical character recognition introduces recognition error and should not be treated as the original record.

### Conceptual example

A company investigates refund delays. The policy says refunds above a threshold require finance approval. The procedure uses an older threshold, the form routes every refund to finance, and training slides instruct staff to email a manager first. Version records show the policy changed but dependent artifacts were not updated. The analyst does not choose one document as the whole truth; the finding identifies authority, configuration, instructions, and synchronization failure.

### Quality checks and failure modes

Document analysis is credible when selection is purposeful, provenance is recorded, authority and currency are checked, extraction is traceable, layout is preserved where material, contradictions remain visible, and sensitive content is controlled.

Failures include trusting filenames, quoting without context, analyzing only formal policy, confusing a template with actual use, copying excessive confidential material, overlooking attachments or footnotes, allowing searchability to determine importance, and treating absence of documentation as proof that no practice exists.

### Advanced note: documentary silence

What a document omits may reflect assumed knowledge, political sensitivity, an excluded user, or a genuine non-requirement. Omission is a question, not evidence of cause. Analysts should compare silence across artifacts and lived practice before interpreting it.

## BA-02-C04-T04 - Surveys

### Plain-language explanation

A survey gathers standardized self-reported answers from a defined group. It can help estimate the distribution of experiences, behaviors, attitudes, knowledge, or characteristics when the question, sample, administration, and analysis support that inference. Surveys provide breadth but usually less contextual depth than interviews or observation.

### Formal definition

A survey is a structured data-collection method in which sampled participants respond to consistently administered questions designed to measure defined constructs for a stated population and analytical purpose.

### Decide whether a survey is suitable

Use a survey when standardized comparison matters, the target construct can be reported meaningfully, and the intended population and sampling route can be defined. A survey is weak when the team does not yet understand the vocabulary, possible answers, process, or relevant population. Exploratory interviews, observation, and document analysis may be needed first.

A survey cannot directly observe behavior, prove causation from a simple association, or repair an inaccessible sampling frame. It should not be used merely because software can distribute a questionnaire quickly.

### Construct, question, and variable

A **construct** is what the research intends to understand, such as trust, effort, satisfaction, confidence, or frequency of a behavior. A **question** is what the participant sees. A **variable** is the recorded representation used in analysis. These layers can diverge.

For example, “How easy was the service?” may capture remembered effort, interface usability, outcome success, mood, or expectations. The analyst must define the construct, decide whether one item is sufficient, and test how participants interpret the wording. Cognitive interviewing and pilot testing can reveal response error and differences between groups.

### Question design

Questions should be relevant, comprehensible, answerable from the participant's knowledge, and neutral enough for the purpose. Avoid:

- double-barreled questions that ask two things;
- leading or loaded wording;
- undefined terms and acronyms;
- vague reference periods such as “regularly”;
- overlapping or incomplete response options;
- assumptions that an event occurred;
- demanding exact recall beyond reasonable memory;
- agreement scales that encourage acquiescence when a direct question is clearer;
- sensitive questions without justification and protection.

Response options must match the question and include legitimate states such as not applicable, do not know, prefer not to answer, or another answer where appropriate. These options have different meanings and should not be combined automatically as missing data.

Question order creates context. Earlier questions can prime later answers. Randomization may reduce order effects for some options but can destroy logical or chronological order. Branching reduces burden when tested carefully; broken routing can exclude valid participants or expose irrelevant sensitive questions.

### Sampling and coverage

The target population is the group about which the analysis wants to make claims. The sampling frame is the accessible list or route used to reach them. The achieved sample is the people who respond. Differences among these groups create coverage, selection, and nonresponse bias.

A large response count does not guarantee representativeness. An employee survey sent through a voluntary link may overrepresent people with strong opinions or available time. A customer email survey excludes people without a usable email address and may underrepresent those who abandoned the service. Report recruitment, invitations, response process, exclusions, and known differences.

Probability sampling can support statistical inference when selection probabilities and design are handled correctly. Convenience and purposive samples can still be useful for exploration or targeted feedback, but percentages should not be generalized beyond what the design supports. Sample size affects precision, not every form of bias.

### Administration, accessibility, and ethics

Administration mode changes who responds and how. Online, phone, paper, in-person, and assisted modes have different accessibility, privacy, cost, and interviewer effects. Provide clear purpose, voluntary-participation information, estimated burden, data use, retention, support, and contact routes. Avoid collecting identifiers unless necessary.

Test keyboard access, screen-reader behavior, contrast, language, mobile display, error messages, save-and-return, and alternative formats. Incentives should compensate time without becoming coercive. Employee or service-dependent populations may need additional safeguards because refusal may not feel free.

### Analysis and reporting

Predefine important measures and exclusions where possible. Inspect missingness, routing, duplicate responses, straight-lining, impossible combinations, completion time, and open-text risk. Cleaning rules must be documented rather than chosen to improve the result.

Report counts as well as percentages, the question wording, base population for each item, field dates, mode, sampling method, response information, weighting, uncertainty, and known limitations. Subgroup comparisons require adequate support and privacy protection. Small cells may enable re-identification and create unstable estimates.

Correlation in survey responses does not establish causation. Satisfaction among people who completed a service does not describe people who abandoned it. “Eighty percent agree” is incomplete without who was invited, who answered, what they were asked, and how uncertainty was handled.

### Conceptual example

Observation suggests staff frequently re-enter customer information. Interviews reveal different meanings of “duplicate entry.” The analyst uses these findings to define a survey item around a recent reference period and specific systems, pilots it with different roles, and distributes it using a role-based sampling frame. Results estimate self-reported frequency among respondents, while system logs are used separately to test actual event frequency.

### Quality checks and failure modes

A survey is credible when construct and population are defined, questions have been evaluated, sampling and administration are transparent, accessibility and privacy are controlled, analysis rules are documented, and conclusions respect uncertainty.

Failures include writing questions before defining the decision, using customer jargon, equating nonresponse with “no,” reporting percentages without bases, overgeneralizing convenience samples, changing questions mid-field without version control, suppressing unfavorable open text, and collecting demographic data without a justified analytical need.

### Advanced note: measurement invariance

Two groups may interpret the same item differently because of language, culture, role, expectations, or experience. A numerical comparison is not meaningful merely because everyone saw identical wording. Question evaluation, translation practice, subgroup analysis, and qualitative follow-up help test comparability.

## BA-02-C04-T05 - Contextual Inquiry

### Plain-language explanation

Contextual inquiry combines observation with questions while a participant performs real or realistic activity in their normal environment. The researcher learns not only what happens but asks about choices, artifacts, meanings, and exceptions at moments when they are visible.

### Formal definition

Contextual inquiry is a situated, participant-centered research method in which the researcher observes activity in context and uses appropriately timed questions, demonstrations, and interpretation checks to develop a shared understanding of work, environment, tools, goals, and constraints.

### Distinguish related methods

Silent observation prioritizes minimal interruption but may leave meaning unclear. An interview prioritizes the participant's account but may lose physical, temporal, and artifact context. Job shadowing follows a role across connected work, often for longer periods. Usability testing evaluates how representative participants use a defined product or prototype against research tasks. Contextual inquiry centers on understanding ongoing activity in its setting through observation and clarification.

The methods can overlap, but labeling matters because it communicates how evidence was produced. Asking a participant to explain every step changes the activity more than occasional clarification. The analyst should document that trade-off rather than call the result purely naturalistic.

### Principles of situated inquiry

Four ideas support the method:

- **Context:** study activity where relevant tools, interruptions, relationships, and constraints are present.
- **Partnership:** treat the participant as the expert in their activity and the researcher as an investigator, while avoiding pressure to perform or teach perfectly.
- **Interpretation:** share tentative understanding and invite correction during or after activity.
- **Focus:** maintain a research purpose while remaining open to evidence that challenges the original frame.

Partnership does not mean the participant must analyze the whole system or design the solution. The researcher remains responsible for cross-source synthesis and for distinguishing participant explanation from organizational authority.

### Planning and access

Define the activity, participant variation, environment, researcher role, question timing, recording method, safety plan, accessibility, and information boundaries. Context can include homes, workplaces, schools, public locations, vehicles, or remote digital environments. Each introduces different risks.

Home and field visits require researcher and participant safety protocols. Workplaces may contain proprietary information and third-party data. Remote screen sharing can reveal notifications, passwords, personal tabs, or family members. Agree pause and stop signals, exclude unsafe tasks, and provide a route to withdraw material where policy permits.

### Observe, inquire, and verify

A contextual inquiry can move through this cycle:

1. Establish purpose, roles, consent, and boundaries.
2. Ask for a brief orientation to the activity and environment.
3. Observe normal work with minimal interruption.
4. Ask short questions at safe, natural breakpoints.
5. Request demonstrations of artifacts or exceptions only when authorized.
6. State tentative interpretations and invite correction.
7. Capture follow-up questions that require another source.
8. Debrief, confirm use of records, and explain what happens next.

Questions should connect to what is present: “I noticed you copied the identifier into a note before changing screens. What does the note help you do?” This is stronger than “Would an integrated dashboard help?” because it explores function before proposing a solution.

Avoid repeated “why” questions that make the participant defend behavior. Ask about triggers, conditions, examples, alternatives, consequences, and how they learned the practice. If an unsafe or unlawful act appears, do not promise confidentiality beyond actual obligations; follow the agreed escalation boundary.

### Artifacts and environment

Contextual inquiry makes background resources visible: notes, labels, browser tabs, wall charts, calculators, messages, spatial arrangements, accessibility aids, noise, lighting, connectivity, and social support. These are not clutter around the process; they may be part of how the process succeeds.

Do not photograph or copy an artifact just because it is useful. Establish ownership, contents, third-party rights, and retention. A participant's personal workaround may contain customer data or reveal a control weakness. Record the necessary analytical fact with the least sensitive detail.

### Conceptual example

During contextual inquiry with a maintenance planner, the analyst sees printed diagrams marked by hand. The planner explains that the official system shows current assets but not safe access routes during shutdowns. The analyst checks the interpretation, records the dependency and risk, and seeks engineering and safety evidence. The finding is not “digitize the diagram”; it is that planning requires current asset data plus situational access knowledge under controlled conditions.

### Quality checks and failure modes

Contextual inquiry is strong when activity occurs in relevant context, interruptions are proportionate, participant expertise is respected, interpretations are checked, environmental artifacts are considered, and consent remains active.

Failures include turning the visit into a solution interview, asking participants to perform risky or rare work for demonstration, ignoring bystanders, collecting personal artifacts, assuming explanation proves policy, and treating the researcher-participant partnership as permission to exceed professional boundaries.

### Advanced note: context is layered

Context is not only physical location. It includes time, workload, organizational rules, social relationships, history, device configuration, language, incentives, and consequences. A remote worker at home may operate within a tightly controlled organizational context; an office demonstration may still be artificial. Analysts should state which contextual layers were and were not observed.

## BA-02-C04-T06 - Evidence

### Plain-language explanation

Evidence is information used to support or challenge a claim for a defined purpose. Research produces sources and records; analysis turns them into findings. Evidence quality depends on the connection between the claim and the source, not on whether the source looks formal or contains a number.

### Formal definition

Evidence in business analysis is traceable information whose provenance, relevance, credibility, limitations, and relationship to a stated claim have been evaluated sufficiently for the decision or analytical use it is intended to support.

### Source, record, observation, finding, and decision

These layers should remain visible:

```text
Source: where information originated.
Record: the captured representation, such as a note, file, response, or event.
Observation: a relevant fact identified in one or more records.
Interpretation: a proposed meaning or relationship.
Finding: a supported synthesis that answers a research question.
Recommendation: analytical advice based on findings, constraints, and objectives.
Decision: an authorized commitment informed by evidence and judgment.
```

A source is not automatically evidence for every claim. A procedure supports a claim about documented instructions; it may not support a claim about current behavior. A survey response supports what was reported under that question; it may not prove what occurred. Traceability prevents these substitutions.

### Evidence properties

Evaluate evidence using properties appropriate to the claim:

- **Relevance:** does it address the actual question, population, time, and context?
- **Provenance:** can its origin, method, custody, and transformation be traced?
- **Credibility:** is the source and method suitable and competently applied?
- **Authority:** can the source prescribe, approve, or attest to the matter claimed?
- **Currency:** does it apply to the relevant period and version?
- **Specificity:** is it detailed enough to distinguish alternatives?
- **Consistency:** does it agree with other evidence, and are differences explainable?
- **Coverage:** which people, cases, conditions, or process parts are included or missing?
- **Independence:** do apparently separate sources depend on the same original account?
- **Uncertainty:** what error, ambiguity, variability, or missingness remains?

Authority is not the same as accuracy about all domains. A policy owner can authorize policy but may not observe daily practice. Independence matters because ten reports repeating one undocumented estimate are not ten independent confirmations.

### Triangulation

Triangulation compares sources, methods, researchers, or theories whose strengths and weaknesses differ. Agreement can increase confidence when sources are genuinely independent and relevant. Disagreement can reveal context, time change, definition differences, incentives, measurement error, or a real policy-practice gap.

Do not force convergence. Build a contradiction register containing the claim, sources, scope, definitions, authority, dates, impact, evidence needed, owner, and status. Some disagreements should remain explicit until a decision can proceed conditionally or further research is completed.

### Claim-evidence matrix

A claim-evidence matrix can include:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Claim ID and wording | Defines exactly what is asserted |
| Decision use | States why the claim matters |
| Supporting sources | Links relevant records and methods |
| Challenging sources | Preserves negative or conflicting evidence |
| Population and context | Prevents inappropriate generalization |
| Confidence and rationale | Explains current strength without false precision |
| Limitations | States missing, biased, or uncertain elements |
| Owner and review date | Maintains accountability and currency |

Confidence labels need definitions. “High” should not mean the analyst feels convinced. It can mean, for example, that multiple relevant sources using appropriate methods converge, material contradictions are resolved, and the claim is sufficiently current for its stated use. High confidence for a discovery hypothesis may still be inadequate for legal or safety approval.

### Evidence hierarchy and fitness for purpose

There is no universal ranking that makes one method strongest for every claim. System logs may be strong for recorded event timing but weak for unrecorded work. Observation may reveal behavior but cover few cases. Surveys may estimate self-reported distribution but lack depth. Documents may define authority but lag practice. Expert judgment may interpret complex constraints but carry institutional bias.

Fitness for purpose also depends on decision risk. A reversible pilot may proceed with bounded uncertainty and monitoring. An irreversible, expensive, discriminatory, safety-critical, or rights-affecting decision requires stronger assurance, specialist review, and more explicit governance.

### Analysis discipline

Preserve raw records separately from coded or summarized material. Document transformations, inclusion rules, excluded cases, coding changes, calculations, and versions. Protect negative cases and minority evidence. Peer review can test whether the finding follows from the records, but agreement between analysts does not repair poor sampling or invalid measurement.

Use calibrated language:

```text
Observed in three selected sessions...
Reported by respondents to this voluntary survey...
Required by the current approved policy...
Supported across observation, records, and interviews...
Not yet established because...
```

Avoid universal claims such as “users always,” causal language without design support, or numerical precision beyond the method. Evidence strength and business urgency are separate: an urgent decision may still rest on uncertain evidence and should say so.

### Privacy, security, and retention

Traceability does not require copying every sensitive detail into every artifact. Use coded references, controlled repositories, role-based access, minimization, retention limits, and approved deletion. Aggregation and anonymization may reduce risk, but rare roles, quotations, locations, or event combinations can re-identify people.

Research records may be subject to legal holds, access requests, regulatory duties, contractual constraints, or records policy. Analysts should involve privacy, legal, security, HR, safeguarding, or research specialists where required and avoid inventing their own retention rules.

### Conceptual example

A team claims that manual review causes most application delay. Interviews describe frustration with review, observation shows waiting around missing documents, survey respondents report unclear instructions, and event data shows long intervals before documents arrive. The approved process also requires review only after completeness. The evidence supports a different finding: document completeness and status visibility contribute materially to delay, while manual review remains one later component. The original claim is revised rather than defended.

### Quality checks and failure modes

Evidence practice is credible when claims are precise, sources are traceable, method fitness and independence are assessed, contradictions and negative cases remain visible, confidence is justified for the intended use, and sensitive records are controlled.

Failures include citation dumping, equating formality with truth, counting repeated sources as independent, hiding inconvenient observations, using survey precision to mask selection bias, changing a claim after seeing evidence without version history, and treating stakeholder approval as empirical validation.

### Advanced note: evidence changes decisions through models

Evidence rarely determines a decision directly. It changes a model of the situation: expected benefits, harms, constraints, probabilities, dependencies, and uncertainty. Values, authority, resources, and risk tolerance also affect the decision. Keeping this separation visible prevents the phrase “data-driven” from hiding judgment and accountability.

## Integrated research model

A defensible observation-and-research cycle can be summarized as:

1. Define the decision, analytical question, claim, population, context, and risk.
2. Review existing evidence and identify the gap.
3. Select methods whose strengths match the gap and whose limitations differ.
4. Define participants, settings, documents, sampling, units, and variation.
5. Establish consent, access, privacy, safety, accessibility, recording, retention, and escalation controls.
6. Pilot protocols, extraction forms, discussion guides, or survey questions.
7. Collect records while separating observation, participant explanation, and analyst interpretation.
8. Preserve provenance, method changes, negative cases, contradictions, and missingness.
9. Analyze against the research question and compare evidence across methods.
10. Validate critical interpretations with participants, records, specialists, or owners as appropriate.
11. State findings, confidence, limitations, and decision fitness precisely.
12. Update linked process models, stakeholder analysis, requirements, risks, and research gaps without erasing history.

## Integrated conceptual example

A bank wants to understand why small-business account applications are abandoned. Document analysis finds that policy, public guidance, staff instructions, and form content use different definitions of beneficial ownership. Survey responses suggest applicants find the evidence request unclear, but the voluntary sample excludes many abandoners.

Contextual inquiry shows applicants switching between the form, company records, messages from advisers, and handwritten notes. Observation of support staff reveals repeated explanation and a workaround that is not in training. Job shadowing exposes interruptions and escalation delays across a full support shift. Event data identifies where recorded abandonment occurs but cannot explain private work completed outside the system.

The evidence matrix distinguishes prescribed rules, reported experience, observed behavior, system events, and analyst interpretations. It preserves a contradiction about which policy version applies and asks the authorized owner to resolve it. The team can now support a traceable problem finding and research plan. It cannot yet claim that a redesigned form will solve every abandonment cause.

## Common misconceptions

### “Observation shows the truth”

False. It shows selected visible activity in a setting influenced by access and the observer. Meaning and prevalence require further evidence.

### “Job shadowing is employee monitoring”

False. Proper research has a defined analytical purpose, consent, boundaries, and non-evaluative reporting. Surveillance or performance management is a materially different activity.

### “The approved document describes current practice”

False. It may be authoritative about intended practice. Observation, system evidence, and current users are needed to test enactment.

### “A large survey is representative”

False. Size improves sampling precision under suitable design but does not remove coverage, selection, nonresponse, measurement, or processing bias.

### “Contextual inquiry means interviewing at the workplace”

False. The activity, environment, artifacts, and in-the-moment interpretation are central, not merely the location.

### “Triangulation means sources must agree”

False. Structured disagreement may reveal the most important condition, definition, or governance gap.

### “More evidence always improves a decision”

False. Irrelevant, dependent, outdated, or excessive data can obscure the question and increase privacy risk. Evidence must be fit for purpose.

## Limitations, ethics, and professional boundaries

Research access is unequal. People who are easiest to observe, whose work is digitally recorded, who have time to answer surveys, or whose documents are searchable can become overrepresented. Deliberately identify absent groups, inaccessible settings, off-system work, third parties, and people who declined participation.

Consent is ongoing and specific. Agreement to be observed does not automatically cover photographs, screen recording, quotation, additional observers, or reuse in a different study. Employment, service dependency, education, health, immigration, or supplier relationships can make participation feel compulsory. Provide realistic choices and alternative routes where possible.

Do not collect data because it might become useful. Minimize identifiers, sensitive content, unrelated records, and duration of retention. Separate research participation from operational decision-making where feasible. Protect staff workarounds from punitive exposure while never promising secrecy beyond safety, legal, regulatory, or organizational duties.

Accessibility affects both participation and evidence. Excluding disabled people, assistive technologies, low-bandwidth environments, non-dominant languages, or supported use can produce a falsely simple process model. Design methods and tools so people can participate without excessive burden or disclosure.

Researchers must stay within competence. Safety-critical sites, vulnerable participants, medical or legal information, covert methods, safeguarding disclosures, and regulated data require specialist governance. Business analysis authority does not permit trespass, deception, unauthorized system access, clinical interpretation, or investigation of misconduct.

## Advanced synthesis

Observation and research do not simply collect fragments of reality. They construct a disciplined view through questions, selection, instruments, access, recording, analysis, and governance. Transparency about that construction strengthens rather than weakens evidence.

Mature practice maintains three forms of validity:

- **Descriptive validity:** the record accurately represents what was observed, reported, or documented.
- **Interpretive validity:** the meaning attributed to the record is supported and appropriately checked.
- **Inferential validity:** the claim does not extend beyond the people, contexts, measures, and methods that can support it.

It also maintains decision proportionality. A low-risk, reversible experiment may need enough evidence to learn safely. A high-consequence commitment needs deeper corroboration, explicit uncertainty, affected-stakeholder involvement, and formal assurance. The objective is not perfect certainty. It is an honest, traceable basis for responsible action.
