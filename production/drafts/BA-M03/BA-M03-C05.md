## Chapter overview

Requirements change because knowledge, context, law, risk, technology, and stakeholder priorities change. Control does not mean freezing learning; it means knowing which information is authoritative, what changed, why it changed, who decided, and what else is affected. This chapter explains baselines, versions, traceability, impact analysis, change requests, and scope control as one lifecycle system.

This chapter is Phase 1 theory only. It does not include configuration exercises, change-board simulations, or assessment items.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- explain what a baseline authorizes and what it does not freeze forever;
- distinguish artifact version, requirement version, status, variant, and effective date;
- design trace relationships that answer defined governance questions;
- assess direct, indirect, downstream, transition, and retirement impacts;
- structure a change request from trigger through decision and implementation;
- maintain scope boundaries without suppressing legitimate discovery.

## BA-03-C05-T01 - Baselines

### Concept

A baseline is an approved snapshot of a defined set of requirements or related information used as a reference for further work and controlled change. It establishes what was authorized at a point in time; it does not declare the information permanently correct.

### Contents and authority

Define the included items, versions, scope, approval authority, date, applicable release or context, unresolved exceptions, and repository location. A baseline may include requirements, models, rules, acceptance criteria, interface definitions, and trace links. Its granularity should fit the governance need; baselining every minor draft can create noise, while baselining only a large document can hide changed clauses.

Baseline approval should confirm suitability for the next decision, not merely attendance at a meeting. Preserve dissent, assumptions, and conditions. After approval, proposed changes are compared with the baseline and processed through the agreed route.

### Failure modes

A baseline is a named, approved configuration of requirements and related information used as a reference for a defined purpose. It may cover a release, contract, increment, interface, policy version, or migration wave. Its contents need stable identity: a filename alone is insufficient when linked rules, models, criteria, and definitions change independently. The record names included versions, approval scope, effective point, authority, accepted conditions, and retrieval location.

Baselines should be proportionate. Freezing a discovery hypothesis as if it were contractual discourages learning; allowing a supplier to build against a continuously changing page creates commercial and verification risk. Different baselines can support different decision points while controlled elaboration continues. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 places requirements information within lifecycle practices rather than treating approval as permanent correctness [1].

### Worked example - defining the reference state

A claims program approves business scope while interface rules remain under joint design. Its baseline includes the outcome, scope, rules, data authorities, quality thresholds, and an open interface decision with an owner and date. A later interface baseline references that business state. When a threshold changes, reviewers can determine which implementations and tests used each version instead of asking which emailed document was "final."

Common failures include silent replacement, unclear included versions, approval by people without authority, treating a shared file as a baseline, and continuing implementation against superseded information.

## BA-03-C05-T02 - Versions

### Concept

Versioning distinguishes meaningful states of an information item over time. It provides identity and history so users can determine what changed and which representation applies.

### Dimensions

Artifact version refers to a document, model, or package. Requirement version refers to an individual controlled item. Status indicates lifecycle state such as draft, reviewed, approved, superseded, implemented, or retired. Variant distinguishes simultaneously valid forms for products or jurisdictions. Effective date states when a rule or requirement applies. These dimensions should not be collapsed into one filename.

Record version identifier, author or changer, timestamp, change summary, rationale, source or request, approval, affected baseline, and effective date. Preserve history and prevent identifiers from being reused for different meanings. Minor editorial corrections and semantic changes may use different governance, but the rule must be explicit.

### Retrieval

Versioning answers more than "which file is newest?" An item has content history, workflow state, applicability, and sometimes effective time. Revision 7 may be the latest draft while revision 5 remains the approved production baseline. A future-dated rule can be approved but not effective. These dimensions should be explicit rather than compressed into one decimal label.

Use immutable history and a single authoritative current view. Corrections create new versions; they do not erase earlier decisions. Retrieved copies should show identity, state, and date and link to their source. Variants need applicability such as jurisdiction, product, tenant, or release. A version scheme communicates ordering; the change record communicates meaning.

### Failure analysis - newest is not applicable

Operations deploys `PolicyRequirements_v12_FINAL2`, although its threshold is approved for the next tax year. The content is newer but not effective. Controlled records separate approval and effective dates, associate cases with governing policy versions, and prevent future requirements from appearing as current instructions.

Users need to find the current applicable version and understand superseded ones without accidentally using them. Access, retention, audit, and confidentiality requirements apply to requirement history as they do to other controlled records.

## BA-03-C05-T03 - Traceability

### Concept

Traceability is the ability to follow meaningful relationships among needs, requirements, designs, delivery items, tests, decisions, risks, rules, and outcomes. Upward traces justify an item; downward traces show realization and verification; horizontal traces reveal peers, dependencies, and conflicts.

### Purpose-led design

Define the questions traceability must answer: Which stakeholder outcome supports this feature? Which requirements are affected by a changed rule? Which approved requirements lack verification? Which release realizes this requirement? Each question determines link types, granularity, direction, attributes, and maintenance responsibility.

A trace matrix is one view, not traceability itself. Repositories and linked models may be more maintainable. Define link semantics such as derives from, satisfies, depends on, verifies, conflicts with, supersedes, and applies to. A generic "related to" link rarely supports reliable analysis.

### Quality and cost

Traceability is a purposeful network of typed relationships: derives from, satisfies, refines, implements, verifies, conflicts with, depends on, and supersedes. Adjacent identifiers can look complete while hiding meaning. Direction should permit forward questions—how will this outcome be realized and verified?—and backward questions—why does this behavior exist and under whose authority?

Trace to the degree needed for decisions, assurance, change, and learning. High-risk rules, obligations, shared interfaces, and safety controls warrant stronger links than exploratory notes. Excessive linkage becomes stale. Automate referential integrity and coverage where possible while preserving semantic review; a valid link to the wrong source remains a defect.

### Worked example - exposing unjustified scope

A review finds twelve dashboard requirements with tests but no approved outcome or user decision. Three support operations, two are required reports, and seven were copied from an old product. The team links five and removes seven from committed scope. Traceability has controlled scope rather than merely documented it.

Check orphan requirements, unsupported outcomes, missing verification, invalid links, duplicate relationships, and stale versions. Traceability has maintenance cost; apply depth according to complexity, regulatory exposure, change rate, and consequence of error. Automating links without semantic review can scale bad relationships.

## BA-03-C05-T04 - Impact Analysis

### Concept

Impact analysis evaluates the probable consequences of a proposed or observed change before a decision and again as implementation knowledge develops. It examines more than the edited requirement.

### Coverage

Assess need and value, stakeholders, processes, rules, data, functions, quality attributes, interfaces, architecture, operations, suppliers, controls, documentation, training, migration, tests, monitoring, cost, schedule, risk, accessibility, privacy, security, compliance, and decommissioning. Follow trace links in both directions and inspect unlinked areas where the trace model may be incomplete.

Identify direct, indirect, cumulative, and distributional effects. State assumptions, estimate ranges, confidence, unknowns, reversibility, consequence of delay, and the baseline comparison. A small wording change can have large semantic impact; a large document diff can be editorial.

### Outcome

Impact analysis starts from a proposed change and follows meaning across outcomes, stakeholders, processes, rules, data, interfaces, qualities, designs, tests, migration, operations, training, contracts, suppliers, metrics, and documents. Direct links are only a starting point. Identify both the cost of change and the consequence of no change.

Compare options. A rule might be implemented centrally, duplicated in channels, or handled temporarily through a manual control. Each option changes timing, consistency, recovery, and retirement. Record uncertainty and confidence rather than one invented estimate. The authority needs affected items, consequences, alternatives, recommendation, and residual risk.

### Worked example - one definition changes five products

"Active customer" changes from any transaction in twelve months to an eligible transaction in six. Impacts reach segmentation, benefits, dashboards, campaigns, model features, and retention reports. A field-name search misses derived logic. Lineage, rule ownership, trace links, and review produce a staged transition with parallel measures and reconciliation.

Impact analysis should support options and a decision, not merely list affected files. It may recommend accept, reject, defer, split, investigate, pilot, or redesign, with conditions and owners.

## BA-03-C05-T05 - Change Requests

### Concept

A change request is a controlled proposal to add, remove, or modify a baselined requirement, scope element, design constraint, rule, or related commitment. It creates a decision route and audit trail; it should not be used to punish discovery.

### Information model

Record identifier, requester, date, trigger, current baseline, proposed change, rationale, affected outcome, urgency, alternatives, impact analysis, dependencies, risks, recommendation, decision authority, decision, conditions, implementation owner, target version, communication, and closure evidence.

Use proportionate routes. A safety-critical rule change may require specialist review; a low-impact clarification may follow delegated authority. Emergency change can shorten advance review but still needs retrospective evidence and reconciliation. "Approved" is not the final state: the decision must propagate to requirements, plans, tests, communications, and operations.

### Governance

A change request is a decision package, not permission to edit. It identifies outcome, source, rationale, urgency, affected baseline items, alternatives, impacts, costs, risks, desired effective date, and acceptance evidence. It can be approved, rejected, deferred, split, or returned. The decision links to resulting versions and implementation state.

Authority must match consequence. A product owner may adjust local priority, while a policy owner controls eligibility and a security authority accepts specified risk. Emergency paths accelerate work but still capture identity, authorization, validation, communication, and retrospective completion.

### Worked example - urgent correction without lost control

A retry defect calculates fees twice. An emergency change disables retries behind a controlled flag, identifies affected transactions, and begins reconciliation. The authority records the temporary impact. A permanent idempotency correction follows review and tests. Closure occurs after the temporary control is retired and remediation verified.

Separate submitter, analyst, recommender, and decision owner when conflicts of interest or risk warrant it. Rejection should include rationale and reconsideration conditions. Track duplicate and linked requests to avoid fragmented decisions.

## BA-03-C05-T06 - Scope Control

### Concept

Scope control maintains an explicit boundary around intended outcomes, included capabilities, affected populations, deliverables, and exclusions while providing a governed path for learning and change. It is not the prevention of all additions.

### Scope model

Define product, project, process, organizational, data, geographic, channel, release, and time boundaries as relevant. Link included scope to outcomes and assumptions. Exclusions should state rationale and consequence, especially when they affect accessibility, support, migration, security, or downstream operations.

Scope creep is uncontrolled expansion, not every new requirement. Scope cut is uncontrolled or poorly analyzed removal. Gold plating adds unapproved functionality. Scope discovery reveals necessary information previously unknown. These conditions require different responses.

### Control practices

Scope control protects an approved outcome and boundary while allowing justified learning. The scope model includes outcomes, capabilities, exclusions, interfaces, constraints, assumptions, and acceptance. A new idea becomes uncontrolled when it enters commitments without analysis and authority. "Out of scope" should not suppress evidence that the boundary is unsafe or unable to meet its outcome.

Intake, triage, impact analysis, decision thresholds, contingency, and visible status provide control. Requirements may be clarified without changing intent, elaborated within a boundary, or changed in a way that affects commitment. Define which class needs which authority. Gold plating is a scope defect even when technically elegant.

### Worked example - clarification versus expansion

A baseline allows evidence in supported digital formats. Specifying approved size limits and errors is elaboration. Adding live video creates privacy, storage, accessibility, support, and supplier consequences and is a scope change. The video proposal receives outcome and impact review instead of entering as a "small upload enhancement."

Use baselines, decision rights, prioritization, impact analysis, change routes, release boundaries, and traceability. Monitor cumulative small changes because individually acceptable requests can collectively defeat time, budget, architecture, or value. Avoid using a fixed scope statement to silence evidence of harm or mandatory obligations; escalate conflicts to the appropriate authority.

## Integrated lifecycle model

Approve a defined baseline and identify its versions. Maintain purpose-led trace relationships. When a trigger arises, capture a change request and analyze impacts across the network. Decide through authorized governance, update the baseline and applicable versions, communicate the outcome, verify implementation, and preserve history. Scope control monitors whether cumulative decisions remain aligned to the need and constraints.

### End-to-end control pattern

Start by defining the authoritative repository and identity rules. Each governed item records type, statement, source, rationale, owner, state, applicability, priority, verification method, relationships, and history as appropriate. Documents become reviewable views over that information. If files remain authoritative, they need equivalent discipline through controlled paths, metadata, stable anchors, and reviewed changes.

Before a baseline decision, check that included items are valid for their level, material conflicts are resolved or explicitly accepted, and required reviewers examined the exact version. Create a manifest or immutable reference. Delivery teams link designs, backlog items, changes, tests, and operational controls to governed requirements. Coverage reports reveal missing realization or verification, but reviewers still determine whether links are meaningful.

When evidence changes, open a record and analyze the network. Do not edit every downstream artifact first and seek approval afterward. Decide whether the proposal clarifies wording, corrects an error, elaborates within intent, or changes intent; identify applicability and transition; then update authoritative sources and propagate controlled views. Superseded versions remain retrievable for cases, audits, or products governed by them.

Retirement is part of control. An item can be withdrawn when no active element depends on it, retention obligations are met, and consumers have migrated. Mark it retired rather than deleting history. Redirect references where a successor exists and communicate the effective point. NASA systems-engineering guidance connects requirements and configuration management to preserving integrity across lifecycle change [3].

### Worked example - a controlled policy transition

A benefits threshold changes on 1 April. The policy owner approves the new rule in January, but existing cases remain under the old version until annual reassessment. The impact record identifies decision services, letters, agent guidance, reports, tests, and appeals. Both rules coexist with explicit applicability. Deployment occurs in March behind an effective-date control; production evidence confirms selection of the correct version for boundary dates. After the final old-policy case completes and retention obligations are met, the rule is retired from active use but preserved in history.

The example shows why one "current requirements" document is insufficient. Current for new applications differs from applicable to an existing case. The trace network connects each decision to the rule version, while the publication view explains the transition without exposing internal identifiers. Reviewers can reconstruct intent and evidence without permitting learners or operators to select an obsolete rule accidentally.

### Control metrics and cautions

Useful measures include orphan requirements, missing verification links, unresolved review comments, change lead time, baseline volatility, failed references, reopened decisions, and implementation found outside authorized scope. Interpret them in context. Zero changes may indicate stability or a culture that bypasses control. Many links may indicate assurance or indiscriminate administration. A metric should lead to inquiry, not substitute for it.

The final measure is decision reliability. A reviewer should identify the current applicable item, reconstruct why it exists, see who approved it, locate realization and evidence, understand changes, and distinguish active from superseded information. More versions and links do not automatically improve this capability. Accurate identity, meaningful relationships, proportionate governance, and disciplined retirement do.

The following control questions connect the six practices without treating them as interchangeable:

| Control object | Primary question | Evidence of control |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | What exact state was authorized for this purpose? | Immutable manifest, decision, conditions, and approvers |
| Version | Which revision is applicable to this context and date? | History, state, applicability, effective dates, and source link |
| Trace | Why does this item exist and how is it realized? | Typed, navigable relationships with semantic review |
| Impact | What changes directly or indirectly if this proposal is accepted? | Affected network, alternatives, estimates, uncertainty, and residual risk |
| Change request | Who decided what, on which evidence, and with what result? | Decision package linked to resulting versions and implementation |
| Scope | Do accumulated commitments still support the authorized outcome? | Boundary, exclusions, intake status, variance, and review triggers |

These controls should fit the delivery environment. A small internal improvement may use a versioned brief, linked backlog, and recorded approval. A regulated multi-supplier program may require formal specifications, signed baselines, configuration audits, and contractual change boards. Proportionate does not mean informal by default; it means the strength of control follows consequence, longevity, distribution, and cost of misunderstanding.

IIBA's requirements lifecycle perspective links tracing, maintaining, prioritizing, assessing changes, and approving requirements [2]. The practices reinforce one another: impact analysis depends on meaningful traceability; controlled change depends on known baselines; version applicability depends on scope and effective dates. Weakness in one control propagates. A precise change request cannot protect a baseline whose contents are unknown, and an extensive matrix cannot support impact analysis when its link meanings are undefined.

Finally, access and confidentiality form part of lifecycle control. Trace links may connect public requirements to sensitive threats, personal evidence, legal advice, or supplier information. The authoritative system should provide the minimum necessary access while retaining decision provenance. Publication views can omit sensitive detail without severing the controlled reference. Redaction must not silently change meaning or conceal a risk from an authorized reviewer.

Tool migration deserves the same discipline as requirement change. Before moving records, define field mappings, relationship semantics, history retention, attachment handling, permissions, and reconciliation evidence. Counts alone do not prove a successful migration: identifiers may be present while links point to the wrong versions or approval history has been flattened. Preserve the legacy repository read-only until authorized reconciliation and retention decisions are complete.

When teams exchange requirements across organizational boundaries, define who owns the authoritative item and how acknowledgements, proposed changes, and accepted versions move between systems. Copying a supplier requirement into an internal tool without a synchronization rule produces two apparent sources of truth. A controlled interface may exchange stable IDs, versions, status, and decisions while each party retains appropriate evidence. The governance agreement is as important as the file format.

Control must remain usable in practice.

## Common misconceptions

- A baseline is not a promise that nothing will change.
- A filename containing "final" is not version control.
- Traceability is not limited to a spreadsheet or one-to-one links.
- Impact analysis is not just an implementation estimate.
- Approval of a change request does not prove the change was propagated.
- Scope control should enable governed learning, not suppress it.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Change decisions can shift cost and risk to people absent from the decision. Analyze affected groups, preserve dissent, protect sensitive records, and ensure emergency routes do not become a method of avoiding scrutiny. Retention and audit needs must be balanced with privacy and access controls. Specialist owners decide legal, security, safety, privacy, architecture, and financial questions within their authority.

## Advanced synthesis

Traceability and change control create organizational memory. Their value is not the number of links or forms completed, but the ability to explain present commitments, reconstruct decisions, anticipate consequences, and retire obligations responsibly. The control system should be as rigorous as the consequence of being wrong and as lightweight as responsible decision-making permits.
