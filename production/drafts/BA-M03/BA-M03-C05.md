## Purpose

Requirements change because knowledge, context, law, risk, technology, and stakeholder priorities change. Control does not mean freezing learning; it means knowing which information is authoritative, what changed, why it changed, who decided, and what else is affected. This chapter explains baselines, versions, traceability, impact analysis, change requests, and scope control as one lifecycle system.

This chapter is Phase 1 theory only. It does not include configuration exercises, change-board simulations, or assessment items.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- explain what a baseline authorizes and what it does not freeze forever;
- distinguish artifact version, requirement version, status, variant, and effective date;
- design trace relationships that answer defined governance questions;
- assess direct, indirect, downstream, transition, and retirement impacts;
- structure a change request from trigger through decision and implementation;
- maintain scope boundaries without suppressing legitimate discovery.

## BA-03-C05-T01 - Baselines

### Concept

A baseline is an approved snapshot of a defined set of requirements or related information used as a reference for further work and controlled change. It establishes what was authorized at a point in time; it does not declare the information permanently correct.

### Contents and authority

Define the included items, versions, scope, approval authority, date, applicable release or context, unresolved exceptions, and repository location. A baseline may include requirements, models, rules, acceptance criteria, interface definitions, and trace links. Its granularity should fit the governance need; baselining every minor draft can create noise, while baselining only a large document can hide changed clauses.

Baseline approval should confirm suitability for the next decision, not merely attendance at a meeting. Preserve dissent, assumptions, and conditions. After approval, proposed changes are compared with the baseline and processed through the agreed route.

### Failure modes

Common failures include silent replacement, unclear included versions, approval by people without authority, treating a shared file as a baseline, and continuing implementation against superseded information.

## BA-03-C05-T02 - Versions

### Concept

Versioning distinguishes meaningful states of an information item over time. It provides identity and history so users can determine what changed and which representation applies.

### Dimensions

Artifact version refers to a document, model, or package. Requirement version refers to an individual controlled item. Status indicates lifecycle state such as draft, reviewed, approved, superseded, implemented, or retired. Variant distinguishes simultaneously valid forms for products or jurisdictions. Effective date states when a rule or requirement applies. These dimensions should not be collapsed into one filename.

Record version identifier, author or changer, timestamp, change summary, rationale, source or request, approval, affected baseline, and effective date. Preserve history and prevent identifiers from being reused for different meanings. Minor editorial corrections and semantic changes may use different governance, but the rule must be explicit.

### Retrieval

Users need to find the current applicable version and understand superseded ones without accidentally using them. Access, retention, audit, and confidentiality requirements apply to requirement history as they do to other controlled records.

## BA-03-C05-T03 - Traceability

### Concept

Traceability is the ability to follow meaningful relationships among needs, requirements, designs, delivery items, tests, decisions, risks, rules, and outcomes. Upward traces justify an item; downward traces show realization and verification; horizontal traces reveal peers, dependencies, and conflicts.

### Purpose-led design

Define the questions traceability must answer: Which stakeholder outcome supports this feature? Which requirements are affected by a changed rule? Which approved requirements lack verification? Which release realizes this requirement? Each question determines link types, granularity, direction, attributes, and maintenance responsibility.

A trace matrix is one view, not traceability itself. Repositories and linked models may be more maintainable. Define link semantics such as derives from, satisfies, depends on, verifies, conflicts with, supersedes, and applies to. A generic "related to" link rarely supports reliable analysis.

### Quality and cost

Check orphan requirements, unsupported outcomes, missing verification, invalid links, duplicate relationships, and stale versions. Traceability has maintenance cost; apply depth according to complexity, regulatory exposure, change rate, and consequence of error. Automating links without semantic review can scale bad relationships.

## BA-03-C05-T04 - Impact Analysis

### Concept

Impact analysis evaluates the probable consequences of a proposed or observed change before a decision and again as implementation knowledge develops. It examines more than the edited requirement.

### Coverage

Assess need and value, stakeholders, processes, rules, data, functions, quality attributes, interfaces, architecture, operations, suppliers, controls, documentation, training, migration, tests, monitoring, cost, schedule, risk, accessibility, privacy, security, compliance, and decommissioning. Follow trace links in both directions and inspect unlinked areas where the trace model may be incomplete.

Identify direct, indirect, cumulative, and distributional effects. State assumptions, estimate ranges, confidence, unknowns, reversibility, consequence of delay, and the baseline comparison. A small wording change can have large semantic impact; a large document diff can be editorial.

### Outcome

Impact analysis should support options and a decision, not merely list affected files. It may recommend accept, reject, defer, split, investigate, pilot, or redesign, with conditions and owners.

## BA-03-C05-T05 - Change Requests

### Concept

A change request is a controlled proposal to add, remove, or modify a baselined requirement, scope element, design constraint, rule, or related commitment. It creates a decision route and audit trail; it should not be used to punish discovery.

### Information model

Record identifier, requester, date, trigger, current baseline, proposed change, rationale, affected outcome, urgency, alternatives, impact analysis, dependencies, risks, recommendation, decision authority, decision, conditions, implementation owner, target version, communication, and closure evidence.

Use proportionate routes. A safety-critical rule change may require specialist review; a low-impact clarification may follow delegated authority. Emergency change can shorten advance review but still needs retrospective evidence and reconciliation. "Approved" is not the final state: the decision must propagate to requirements, plans, tests, communications, and operations.

### Governance

Separate submitter, analyst, recommender, and decision owner when conflicts of interest or risk warrant it. Rejection should include rationale and reconsideration conditions. Track duplicate and linked requests to avoid fragmented decisions.

## BA-03-C05-T06 - Scope Control

### Concept

Scope control maintains an explicit boundary around intended outcomes, included capabilities, affected populations, deliverables, and exclusions while providing a governed path for learning and change. It is not the prevention of all additions.

### Scope model

Define product, project, process, organizational, data, geographic, channel, release, and time boundaries as relevant. Link included scope to outcomes and assumptions. Exclusions should state rationale and consequence, especially when they affect accessibility, support, migration, security, or downstream operations.

Scope creep is uncontrolled expansion, not every new requirement. Scope cut is uncontrolled or poorly analyzed removal. Gold plating adds unapproved functionality. Scope discovery reveals necessary information previously unknown. These conditions require different responses.

### Control practices

Use baselines, decision rights, prioritization, impact analysis, change routes, release boundaries, and traceability. Monitor cumulative small changes because individually acceptable requests can collectively defeat time, budget, architecture, or value. Avoid using a fixed scope statement to silence evidence of harm or mandatory obligations; escalate conflicts to the appropriate authority.

## Integrated lifecycle model

Approve a defined baseline and identify its versions. Maintain purpose-led trace relationships. When a trigger arises, capture a change request and analyze impacts across the network. Decide through authorized governance, update the baseline and applicable versions, communicate the outcome, verify implementation, and preserve history. Scope control monitors whether cumulative decisions remain aligned to the need and constraints.

## Common misconceptions

- A baseline is not a promise that nothing will change.
- A filename containing "final" is not version control.
- Traceability is not limited to a spreadsheet or one-to-one links.
- Impact analysis is not just an implementation estimate.
- Approval of a change request does not prove the change was propagated.
- Scope control should enable governed learning, not suppress it.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Change decisions can shift cost and risk to people absent from the decision. Analyze affected groups, preserve dissent, protect sensitive records, and ensure emergency routes do not become a method of avoiding scrutiny. Retention and audit needs must be balanced with privacy and access controls. Specialist owners decide legal, security, safety, privacy, architecture, and financial questions within their authority.

## Advanced synthesis

Traceability and change control create organizational memory. Their value is not the number of links or forms completed, but the ability to explain present commitments, reconstruct decisions, anticipate consequences, and retire obligations responsibly. The control system should be as rigorous as the consequence of being wrong and as lightweight as responsible decision-making permits.
