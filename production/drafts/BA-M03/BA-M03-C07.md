## Purpose

Requirements documentation turns evidence about a business need into a controlled body of information that people can understand, review, approve, build from, test against, and maintain. The document itself is not the objective. The objective is shared understanding and defensible decision-making across the life of a change.

This chapter establishes the foundations needed before creating a Business Requirements Document (BRD), Product Requirements Document (PRD), or Functional Requirements Document (FRD). It explains why documentation exists, how the principal document types differ, who participates, what counts as evidence, how scope and assumptions are controlled, how templates should be used, and how versions become approved baselines.

The terms BRD, PRD, and FRD are common professional labels, but organizations do not use them uniformly. A team may combine them, rename them, represent them as linked records in a requirements-management tool, or replace parts of them with product backlogs and models. Therefore, this module treats each name as a useful document responsibility-not as a universal file format. What matters is that the required information has an accountable home, traceable sources, an understood audience, and controlled change.

After completing this theory chapter, the learner should be able to:

- Distinguish BRD, PRD, FRD, and common supporting artifacts by decision level and audience.

- Separate evidence from interpretation, assumptions, preferences, and proposed solutions.
- Define scope boundaries and manage assumptions and constraints without hiding uncertainty.
- Choose and tailor a template without allowing the template to drive the analysis.
- Explain stable identifiers, versions, revision histories, baselines, and controlled change.
- Describe a review and approval system that preserves accountability and traceability.

## A conceptual model for controlled documentation

Requirements documentation can be understood as a chain of accountable transformations:

```text
Observed context and evidence
        downward
Needs, problems, opportunities, and constraints
        downward
Business outcomes and business requirements
        downward
Product choices and user-facing capabilities
        downward
Functional behavior and quality expectations
        downward
Design, implementation, tests, release, and measured outcomes
```

Each downward step adds detail. It must not quietly change the intent of the step above it. Each upward link makes it possible to ask why a lower-level requirement exists. This is the core logic of traceability.

A controlled document set also has a lifecycle:

```text
discover -> draft -> review -> revise -> approve -> baseline -> change -> retire
```

The lifecycle is not necessarily linear. New evidence can cause revision, review can expose missing analysis, and a proposed change can be rejected. "Controlled" does not mean frozen. It means that the current state, authority, rationale, and history can be determined reliably.

## BA-03-C07-T01 - Purpose and value of requirements documentation

### Plain-language explanation

Requirements documentation helps a group remember what it learned and agree on what a change must accomplish. It gives people a durable reference that is less fragile than meetings, chat messages, personal notes, or memory. Good documentation lets a stakeholder verify that the business need is represented, lets a delivery team understand what must be built, lets a tester determine what to check, and lets a future maintainer learn why a decision was made.

Documentation does not create truth merely because something is written down. A polished document can still be incomplete, biased, contradictory, or unsupported. Its value comes from the quality of the evidence and analysis behind it, the clarity of its statements, the participation of the right people, and the discipline used to review and maintain it.

### Formal concept

Requirements documentation is the controlled representation of needs, requirements, constraints, rationale, relationships, decisions, and approval state for a defined change or product scope. It may be stored in one document, several linked documents, or a structured repository. Its quality is judged by whether authorized participants can use it to make consistent decisions and verify intended outcomes.

The current ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 standard describes requirements engineering across the lifecycle and addresses the processes, information items, contents, and formatting guidance associated with requirements. This reinforces an important distinction: documentation is one output of requirements engineering, not the whole discipline.

### Sources of value

Requirements documentation creates value in several connected ways:

1. **Alignment.** It gives business, product, technical, operational, security, legal, and testing participants a shared reference.
2. **Decision support.** It records the information needed to approve, reject, sequence, or reshape a proposed change.
3. **Scope control.** It makes boundaries and exclusions visible, reducing accidental expansion and conflicting expectations.
4. **Traceability.** It links a delivered capability or test back to an approved need and links a business objective forward to implementation evidence.
5. **Quality assurance.** It provides criteria for verification-whether the work matches its specification-and validation-whether the result meets the underlying business and user need.
6. **Change analysis.** It enables teams to assess the consequences of changing a requirement, rule, interface, metric, or assumption.
7. **Continuity.** It preserves organizational knowledge when people, suppliers, priorities, or systems change.
8. **Accountability.** It identifies who supplied information, who owns decisions, who reviewed content, and who approved the baseline.
9. **Risk reduction.** It exposes unknowns, dependencies, conflicting needs, compliance obligations, and security considerations before they become expensive failures.

These benefits are not free. Documentation takes time to produce and maintain. Excessive documentation can slow feedback, create stale copies, and give false confidence. The right amount is the minimum controlled information needed for the risk, complexity, longevity, regulatory environment, team distribution, and cost of misunderstanding.

### Conceptual example

Suppose a company wants to reduce customer-support waiting time. A meeting produces the suggestion "install an AI chatbot." If that sentence becomes the requirement, the proposed solution has replaced the actual need. Controlled documentation would preserve the evidence-wait-time data, enquiry categories, failure demand, customer feedback, legal constraints, and service targets-then state the desired outcome and boundaries. A chatbot might later be evaluated as one option. Documentation adds value by keeping the team connected to the problem and by making the eventual decision explainable.

### Boundaries and limitations

Documentation cannot replace stakeholder engagement, observation, analysis, prototyping, experimentation, or professional judgment. It cannot remove uncertainty; it can only make uncertainty visible and govern how decisions are made under it. It also cannot guarantee agreement. Sometimes its most valuable function is to reveal that authorized stakeholders genuinely disagree.

## BA-03-C07-T02 - BRD, PRD, FRD, and supporting-document boundaries

### Why boundaries matter

Document names often create confusion because different organizations use the same label for different content. A BRD may be a short investment case in one company and a comprehensive requirements specification in another. A PRD may be a strategic product narrative, a feature specification, or a collection of linked backlog items. An FRD may describe functional behavior only, or it may include interfaces, data rules, and selected non-functional requirements.

The solution is not to argue that one template is universally correct. The solution is to define the information responsibility of each artifact within the local governance system.

### Business Requirements Document (BRD)

The BRD explains the business change that is needed and why it matters. It normally centers on business context, problem or opportunity, objectives, desired outcomes, scope, stakeholders, business requirements, business rules, constraints, risks, benefits, and success measures.

The BRD should answer questions such as:

- What condition or opportunity justifies change?
- Which business outcomes are sought?
- Who is affected and who owns the decisions?
- What capabilities or business conditions are required?
- What is included and excluded?
- How will success be measured?

The BRD should not prematurely become a detailed software design. It may identify known constraints or mandated technology, but it should preserve the distinction between the business need and the chosen implementation.

### Product Requirements Document (PRD)

The PRD explains what a product or product change must achieve for users and the organization. It connects business outcomes to product scope, users, problems, capabilities, feature boundaries, prioritization, acceptance, product metrics, release considerations, and dependencies.

The PRD should answer questions such as:

- Which users and user problems are being served?
- What product outcomes and behaviors are expected?
- Which capabilities are included now, later, or not at all?
- How are priorities and trade-offs decided?
- What evidence will show that the product change is useful?

In this repository, **PRD** means Product Requirements Document. **PDR** is not used as a synonym because PDR can mean Preliminary Design Review or other organization-specific terms.

### Functional Requirements Document (FRD)

The FRD explains the behavior a system, service, or process must provide. It translates approved business and product intent into precise functional requirements, actors, inputs, processing, outputs, data rules, interfaces, states, exceptions, permissions, and links to quality constraints and tests.

The FRD should answer questions such as:

- What must the system do under defined conditions?
- Who or what initiates the behavior?
- What data enters, changes, and leaves the process?
- What rules, calculations, states, and exceptions apply?
- How will the behavior be verified and traced to higher-level intent?

The FRD is not a substitute for architecture or detailed design. A requirement states the necessary behavior or quality; a design describes how the solution will achieve it. Some implementation constraints may legitimately be requirements, but they need an authoritative source and rationale.

### Supporting artifacts

BRDs, PRDs, and FRDs rarely stand alone. Supporting artifacts can include:

- A business case, benefits plan, or decision memo.
- Stakeholder maps and responsibility assignments.
- Current-state and future-state process models.
- Context diagrams, data-flow diagrams, state models, and decision tables.
- Business-rule catalogues and data dictionaries.
- Non-functional requirements or quality-attribute specifications.
- User-research findings, personas, journey maps, and service blueprints.
- Epics, user stories, use cases, and acceptance criteria.
- Interface specifications and API contracts.
- Requirements traceability matrices.
- Test plans, test cases, UAT evidence, release records, and operational procedures.

The boundary test is simple: every required piece of information needs one authoritative home. Other artifacts may reference it but should not create uncontrolled copies. If the same requirement must appear in more than one view, stable IDs and synchronization rules must make the relationship explicit.

### Common structural patterns

Organizations typically use one of four patterns:

1. **Separate documents.** BRD, PRD, and FRD exist independently and are linked. This supports specialized audiences but increases synchronization risk.
2. **Layered specification.** One controlled repository contains business, product, functional, and quality layers. This strengthens traceability but requires good tooling and navigation.
3. **Lean document plus backlog.** A concise vision and scope document links to product-backlog items and models. This supports iterative delivery but can lose rationale if backlog items are treated as isolated tickets.
4. **Regulated document set.** Formal specifications, signatures, baselines, and change records are maintained to satisfy assurance or contractual needs. This increases control but must still permit evidence-based learning.

No pattern is automatically superior. The choice depends on decision risk, organizational policy, audit obligations, supplier boundaries, product lifecycle, and team needs.

## BA-03-C07-T03 - Audiences, owners, reviewers, and decision rights

### Documentation is a social control system

A requirements document coordinates people with different knowledge, incentives, authority, and vocabulary. A document that is clear to a developer may be inaccessible to a business sponsor. A statement that satisfies a product manager may be too vague for a tester. Effective documentation therefore begins with its audience and the decisions that audience must make.

### Core participation roles

- **Author or analyst:** structures evidence, facilitates analysis, drafts content, manages trace links, and makes uncertainty visible. Authorship does not grant unilateral authority over business or product decisions.
- **Business owner or sponsor:** owns the business outcome, investment rationale, and major scope decisions. This role should confirm that the documented change remains valuable and aligned.
- **Product owner or product manager:** owns product direction, product trade-offs, and prioritization within the authorized business context.
- **Subject-matter expert:** contributes domain rules, exceptions, terminology, operational knowledge, and evidence. Expertise informs decisions but may not include approval authority.
- **User or user representative:** provides evidence about real goals, contexts, barriers, and consequences. A proxy should not silently replace direct user evidence when direct research is feasible.
- **Architect, engineer, or developer:** evaluates feasibility, system boundaries, dependencies, technical constraints, and implementation implications.
- **Quality assurer or tester:** evaluates clarity, consistency, observability, testability, acceptance conditions, and failure behavior.
- **Security, privacy, legal, risk, accessibility, and compliance specialists:** review obligations and risks that may not be visible from a feature-focused perspective.
- **Operations and support participants:** assess monitoring, supportability, continuity, recovery, training, workload, and real operating conditions.
- **Approver:** has explicit authority to accept a defined baseline or decision. An approver is accountable for the decision, not merely aware of the document.
- **Change authority:** decides whether a proposed change to an approved baseline is accepted, rejected, deferred, or returned for analysis.

One person may perform several roles in a small team. The responsibilities must still be distinguished. "Alex wrote and approved the document" carries different risk from "Alex drafted the document; Priya owned the outcome; the security lead reviewed access requirements; and the steering group approved the baseline."

### Decision rights

Consultation is not approval, attendance is not agreement, and silence is not consent. A controlled document should define:

- Who decides business scope and funding?
- Who decides product priority?
- Who accepts domain rules?
- Who can approve risk or compliance exceptions?
- Who confirms technical feasibility?
- Who accepts UAT results?
- Who can change an approved baseline?

A responsibility matrix can help, but labels such as Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed are useful only when they correspond to real authority. Assigning every stakeholder as an approver creates delay and obscures accountability. Assigning too few approvers creates legitimacy and risk problems.

### Audience-based writing

The same controlled fact can require different views. An executive may need objectives, value, risk, scope, and decisions. A delivery team may need precise behavior, data, exceptions, and acceptance criteria. A compliance reviewer may need source obligations and evidence. A tester may need observable outcomes and boundary conditions.

Different views should be generated from or linked to the same controlled information. They should not become competing truths. Executive summaries must accurately compress detail; they must not omit a risk or uncertainty merely because it is inconvenient.

### Conceptual example

A finance director states that invoices above a threshold need "manager approval." Operations explains that temporary managers can approve during leave. Security says approval authority must come from the identity system. Audit requires the approver and policy version to be retained. The analyst does not select one statement by preference. The analyst identifies the decision owner, records the evidence and conflict, models the rule and exceptions, and routes the decision to authorized participants.

## BA-03-C07-T04 - Source evidence and elicitation inputs

### Evidence before specification

Every important requirement should be connected to a credible source. A source can be a person with relevant authority or experience, an observed workflow, a policy, a regulation, a contract, system telemetry, user research, a defect record, a process measure, a market condition, or another approved requirement. The source explains why the requirement deserves attention; it does not automatically prove that the first proposed wording or solution is correct.

Evidence is information that can support or challenge a claim. An assumption is a proposition treated as true for planning while confirmation is pending. An opinion is a person's view. A preference is a desired choice. A decision is an authorized selection among alternatives. A requirement is a necessary condition or capability within a defined scope. These categories may interact, but they should not be collapsed.

For example, "customers want a mobile app" may be a stakeholder opinion. Evidence might show that customers fail to complete a task on mobile browsers. The need could be reliable mobile task completion. A native application is one possible response. Preserving these distinctions prevents a suggested feature from masquerading as an established need.

### Common evidence sources

**Interviews and workshops** reveal goals, rules, pain points, exceptions, terminology, and conflicts. They are valuable but affected by recall, status, group dynamics, and framing. Meeting notes should identify participants, date, context, decisions, unresolved questions, and the analyst's interpretation.

**Observation and contextual inquiry** show how work is actually performed. They can expose unofficial workarounds, handoffs, delays, duplicate entry, and differences between policy and practice. Observation must respect privacy, consent, safety, and workplace rules.

**Documents and policies** establish existing obligations, vocabulary, processes, and decisions. They can also be obsolete. Record the document owner, effective date, version, applicability, and whether a newer authority supersedes it.

**Operational and analytical data** can quantify volume, timing, error rates, abandonment, rework, cost, and variation. Data has its own assumptions: definitions, grain, time period, missingness, selection effects, and lineage. A chart is not self-interpreting evidence.

**User research and feedback** illuminate user goals, contexts, accessibility needs, and consequences. GOV.UK's service guidance emphasizes beginning with user needs, grounding them in research rather than assumptions, and linking specific stories back to those needs. Feedback channels are useful but may overrepresent unusually satisfied or dissatisfied users.

**Systems and prototypes** reveal current behavior and allow uncertain ideas to be tested. Existing behavior is evidence of what the system does-not necessarily what it should continue to do.

**External authorities** include laws, regulations, contractual clauses, standards, and formal organizational policies. Their interpretation may require legal, compliance, risk, security, or domain specialists. The analyst records the authoritative source and decision; the analyst should not present personal legal interpretation as settled fact.

### Provenance and confidence

Provenance is the history of where information came from and how it was transformed. For significant statements, documentation should make it possible to determine:

- Source identity or artifact.
- Date and applicable period.
- Collection method and context.
- Responsible owner or authority.
- Relevant limitations or conflicts.
- Analyst interpretation.
- Validation state.
- Linked requirement or decision IDs.

Not all sources have equal authority or reliability. A signed policy may determine a mandatory rule, while a single interview suggests a hypothesis. High authority does not always mean high accuracy: an approved but outdated policy can conflict with current operations. Good analysis considers authority, recency, representativeness, corroboration, and relevance.

A confidence label can communicate maturity-such as confirmed, supported, provisional, disputed, or obsolete-but the label needs defined criteria. Avoid decorative confidence scores that imply mathematical precision without a defensible method.

### Triangulation and conflict

Triangulation compares different sources to determine whether they support the same conclusion. If interviews, observed work, and transaction data all indicate the same failure, confidence increases. If they conflict, the conflict is information. It may reveal different user groups, local practices, seasonal variation, incorrect definitions, or incentives.

The analyst should not average away a conflict or quietly choose the most senior person's account. Record the disagreement, its impact, the evidence required to resolve it, and the decision authority. When immediate resolution is impossible, the requirement remains conditional or open rather than falsely certain.

### Privacy and confidentiality

Evidence collection can expose personal information, trade secrets, credentials, security architecture, employee performance data, and protected business information. Requirements artifacts should contain only the minimum sensitive detail necessary. Use approved storage, access controls, retention rules, and redaction. Examples and learning artifacts in this repository must use synthetic or public information.

## BA-03-C07-T05 - Scope, boundaries, assumptions, and constraints

### Scope as a decision boundary

Scope defines the portion of a problem, organization, product, process, system, population, geography, time period, or release that the work is authorized to address. It prevents participants from assuming that every related concern is included.

A useful scope statement identifies:

- The outcome or change being considered.
- Included users, processes, capabilities, systems, data, locations, and events.
- Explicit exclusions.
- Interfaces with adjacent scope.
- Time or release boundary.
- Ownership and approval authority.
- Conditions that would require scope reconsideration.

"Improve onboarding" is not a controlled scope. "Reduce incomplete self-service onboarding for new domestic retail customers from identity submission through account activation, excluding credit underwriting and branch-assisted onboarding" is more useful because it defines a journey, population, endpoints, and exclusions. It may still require further analysis, but participants can detect scope changes.

### Context and boundary models

Prose alone may hide boundary misunderstandings. Context diagrams identify the subject under consideration and the external actors or systems that exchange information with it. Process maps identify starting and ending events, participants, handoffs, and outputs. Ecosystem maps reveal partners and indirect dependencies. Data-flow and interface views expose exchanges that a business narrative may omit.

Models are selective representations. A boundary drawn on a diagram is a claim that must be validated. If the team cannot agree where the boundary sits, that disagreement should be resolved before detailed requirements multiply.

### Assumptions

An assumption is a condition believed to be true for planning but not yet confirmed. Examples include expected user volume, availability of an integration, continued validity of a policy, stakeholder availability, or data quality.

Each material assumption should have:

- A stable identifier.
- A clear statement.
- A reason it is currently being used.
- An owner who will confirm or reject it.
- Evidence needed for resolution.
- A target date or decision point.
- Impact if false.
- Linked scope, requirements, risks, and decisions.

Assumptions are not harmless footnotes. An untested assumption can invalidate an entire design. The more consequential the assumption, the earlier it should be tested.

### Constraints

A constraint restricts the available solution space or the manner in which change can occur. Constraints may come from law, policy, contract, technology, budget, schedule, compatibility, accessibility, geography, data residency, workforce capacity, or operating conditions.

A valid constraint should have an authoritative source and rationale. Statements such as "we must use Vendor X" are often preferences until evidence identifies the contract, architecture decision, switching cost, or policy that makes the choice mandatory. Challenging an unsupported constraint is not ignoring stakeholder input; it is protecting the integrity of the analysis.

Constraints differ from requirements. A requirement states a needed capability or condition; a constraint limits how that capability can be achieved. A deadline may be a constraint, while the ability to submit an application is a functional need. Some organizations classify constraints as a requirement type. Whatever taxonomy is selected, the practical distinction should remain visible.

### Dependencies and risks

A dependency is something the scope relies on but does not fully control, such as another team's API, a policy decision, data migration, supplier delivery, or regulatory approval. A risk is an uncertain event or condition that could affect objectives. A constraint is already operative; a risk may or may not occur. An assumption is provisionally treated as true. Confusing these categories leads to weak ownership and response planning.

### Preventing scope creep without suppressing learning

Scope control should not stop legitimate discovery. When new evidence reveals an important related need, record it and assess its impact. The authorized owner may include it, defer it, split the work, or reject it. Scope creep occurs when expansion happens without an explicit decision-not whenever scope changes.

## BA-03-C07-T06 - Templates, writing standards, and document navigation

### Template as scaffold, not analysis

A template is a reusable structure that reminds authors which information may be needed. It improves consistency and navigation, especially across teams. It cannot determine whether the information is true, sufficient, relevant, or approved.

Template-driven failure occurs when authors fill every heading with generic text, invent information to avoid blank sections, repeat the same requirement in several places, or assume that a completed form equals completed analysis. A section that does not apply should be marked not applicable with a reason. A required section lacking information should be marked open, assigned, and tracked.

### Tailoring principles

Tailoring changes the structure or depth of an artifact to suit its purpose while preserving required controls. Consider:

- Audience and decisions.
- Delivery method and cadence.
- Complexity and novelty.
- Safety, financial, privacy, security, and regulatory risk.
- Supplier and contractual boundaries.
- Number and distribution of teams.
- Expected product life and maintenance horizon.
- Tool capabilities and accessibility.
- Required review and audit evidence.

A low-risk internal report may need a short controlled brief and linked backlog. A medical, financial, or safety-related system may need formal specifications, traceable approvals, and rigorous configuration management. Tailoring should be documented so reviewers know what was intentionally included, excluded, or represented elsewhere.

### Writing standards

Good requirements writing favors clarity over ceremony. Useful principles include:

- Use defined terms consistently.
- Prefer direct, active constructions where responsibility matters.
- Separate one requirement from another when they can change or be tested independently.
- State necessary conditions and observable outcomes.
- Quantify thresholds where evidence supports them.
- Define units, populations, time windows, states, and boundary conditions.
- Avoid vague terms such as fast, intuitive, robust, seamless, appropriate, and user-friendly unless they are operationally defined.
- Distinguish mandatory requirements from goals, examples, notes, and rationale.
- Avoid embedding several alternatives inside one approved statement.
- State exceptions and failure conditions where they matter.
- Link detailed models or rules rather than duplicating them.

Standards sometimes recommend normative words such as "shall" for mandatory requirements. The exact vocabulary is less important than consistent, governed meaning. If "must," "shall," "should," and "may" are used, the repository should define them.

### Structure and navigation

A usable document allows readers to find context before detail and move between related information. Common navigation elements include a purpose statement, scope, audience, status, table of contents, glossary, stable IDs, linked models, source references, decision log, traceability links, and revision history.

Long documents need layered reading. Executives should be able to understand outcomes, scope, material risk, and decisions without reading every functional rule. Specialists must still reach precise details. Summaries and details must remain consistent.

Accessibility also applies to requirements documentation. Use descriptive headings, meaningful link labels, readable tables, text alternatives for diagrams, sufficient contrast in visual artifacts, and language that does not depend on color alone. Clear documentation benefits readers using assistive technology and readers working under time pressure.

### Documents versus structured tools

A word-processing file is convenient for narrative and formal review. A wiki supports collaboration. A backlog tool supports decomposition and workflow. A requirements-management system supports attributes, relationships, baselines, and traceability. A repository supports version-controlled text and automation.

The tool should serve the information model. Moving from a document to a tool does not automatically improve quality, and using a document does not automatically imply a waterfall delivery method. Teams can maintain controlled requirements iteratively if ownership, state, links, and change are clear.

## BA-03-C07-T07 - Identifiers, versions, and revision history

### Stable identifiers

A stable identifier gives an artifact or requirement a durable identity independent of its title, page, or position. Titles and wording can change; the identity should remain stable while the underlying requirement remains conceptually the same.

Identifiers enable review comments, trace links, test mapping, change requests, audit records, and impact analysis. A simple controlled scheme might distinguish business requirements, product requirements, functional requirements, rules, assumptions, decisions, and risks. The scheme should be unique, predictable, documented, and generated or checked where practical.

Renumbering every item when a new requirement is inserted destroys references. Reusing a retired ID for a different requirement corrupts history. If a requirement changes so fundamentally that it becomes a different obligation, retire or supersede the old item and create a new identity with an explicit relationship.

### Version dimensions

Versioning can refer to several different things:

- **Artifact version:** the state of a document or repository item.
- **Requirement revision:** the evolution of an individual requirement.
- **Baseline version:** an approved collection of mutually consistent items.
- **Product or release version:** the delivered system state.
- **Schema version:** the structure used to represent information.

These must not be confused. Document version 1.4 might describe requirements for product release 3.0 and conform to schema 2.1. Clear metadata prevents misleading comparisons.

### Revision history

A revision history should answer what changed, why it changed, who changed it, when it changed, and under whose authority. It may also identify affected IDs, review evidence, and the superseded baseline.

A revision-history table is only a summary. Version control or a requirements tool may contain detailed differences. Conversely, raw version-control history may not explain the business rationale or approval. Effective governance connects technical history with decision history.

Minor editorial correction and substantive requirement change should not be treated identically. Correcting spelling without changing meaning may follow a lightweight path. Changing an approval threshold, data-retention period, user population, or success metric requires impact analysis and authorized approval.

### Single-current-version discipline

Files named `final`, `final-final`, `latest`, or `new version` force readers to guess which copy is authoritative. A stronger approach maintains one current controlled artifact, records its version and state, and preserves history through version control and approved baselines. Exports can be useful snapshots, but they are not the editable source of truth.

## BA-03-C07-T08 - Review, approval, baseline, and controlled change

### Review

Review is a structured evaluation of an artifact against defined criteria. Different reviews answer different questions:

- **Business review:** Does this represent the need, outcome, scope, value, and constraints?
- **User review:** Is it grounded in real user contexts and inclusive needs?
- **Product review:** Are product choices, priorities, measures, and trade-offs coherent?
- **Technical review:** Is the requirement feasible, internally consistent, and compatible with system boundaries?
- **Quality review:** Is it clear, atomic where needed, testable, traceable, and complete enough for its state?
- **Security/privacy/compliance review:** Are obligations, threats, data use, access, retention, and exceptions addressed?
- **Operational review:** Can the capability be supported, monitored, recovered, and maintained?

Review comments need a controlled disposition: accepted, rejected with rationale, deferred, duplicate, or requiring decision. Resolving a comment means addressing the concern and recording the outcome, not merely marking a discussion closed.

### Approval

Approval is an authorized decision that an artifact is suitable for a stated purpose and state. It does not mean the artifact is perfect, permanently correct, or immune to change. An approval should identify:

- The artifact and exact version.
- The scope and purpose of approval.
- Approver identity and authority.
- Date.
- Conditions, exceptions, or unresolved accepted risks.
- Effective baseline or release association.

Electronic workflows can provide valid approval evidence when identity, authority, content integrity, and history are controlled. A typed name in an uncontrolled copy is weak evidence.

### Baseline

A baseline is an approved reference state placed under change control. It creates a stable point for design, implementation, testing, contracting, comparison, and impact analysis. NASA's systems-engineering guidance treats baselines and configuration control as mechanisms for preserving product and documentation integrity while managing approved evolution.

Baselining does not prohibit learning. It means subsequent changes are evaluated relative to a known state. Different lifecycle points may have different baselines, and an organization should state which items belong to each.

### Controlled change

A sound change process normally includes:

1. Identify the requested change and affected stable IDs.
2. Record the source, rationale, urgency, and desired outcome.
3. Analyze impacts on scope, value, users, process, data, interfaces, tests, security, privacy, operations, cost, schedule, and other requirements.
4. Identify alternatives and consequences of no change.
5. Route the decision to the correct authority.
6. Approve, reject, defer, or request more evidence.
7. Update the authoritative artifacts and trace links.
8. Create a new baseline or revision where required.
9. Communicate the decision to affected participants.
10. Verify that implementation and tests reflect the approved change.

Emergency change can use an accelerated path, but urgency should not erase accountability. The decision, implementation, validation, and retrospective documentation still need to be captured.

### Common failure modes

- Approval is requested before important reviewers can assess the content.
- A stakeholder approves only the summary while contradictory detail remains.
- Comments are resolved in email but not reflected in the source of truth.
- Teams continue working from downloaded copies after the baseline changes.
- A change is implemented in code without updating requirements and tests.
- Every small edit triggers excessive governance, encouraging people to bypass the process.
- Approval is treated as transfer of responsibility from the analyst to the approver.

The aim is proportionate control: enough evidence and authority to protect outcomes, without ceremony that discourages timely learning.

## Common misconceptions

### "A BRD, PRD, and FRD are mandatory on every project"

False. Their information responsibilities are widely useful, but the packaging can vary. A small product team may use a concise product brief, linked stories, decision records, models, and acceptance criteria. A regulated supplier engagement may require formal separate documents. The repository uses the three forms for learning and professional capability, not because every organization must create three files.

### "More pages mean better requirements"

False. Completeness is coverage of necessary information, not document length. Repetition can make a longer document less reliable.

### "The analyst owns all requirements decisions"

False. The analyst owns the quality and transparency of analysis within the assigned role. Business owners, product authorities, specialists, and governance bodies retain their decision rights.

### "Approved means correct forever"

False. Approval applies to a defined version and purpose based on available evidence. Controlled change exists because needs, evidence, laws, risks, and systems evolve.

### "Agile teams do not document requirements"

False. Agile delivery values useful working outcomes and responsiveness; it does not eliminate the need to preserve critical intent, decisions, rules, and acceptance evidence. Agile documentation is often incremental and linked rather than assembled as one large document.

### "A template guarantees consistency"

False. A template can standardize headings while the content remains contradictory. Semantic consistency requires defined terms, stable ownership, traceability, review, and active maintenance.

## Advanced perspective: documentation as an information architecture

At scale, requirements documentation is better understood as an information architecture than as a collection of files. Each item has a type, identity, state, owner, source, rationale, attributes, relationships, and history. Documents become views over this controlled information.

This perspective supports several advanced practices:

- **Bidirectional traceability:** navigating from need to delivery evidence and from implementation back to its justification.
- **Coverage analysis:** identifying approved needs without requirements, requirements without tests, or delivered features without authorized value.
- **Change propagation:** finding related items that may need review when a source, rule, interface, or assumption changes.
- **Automated quality checks:** detecting missing attributes, invalid IDs, broken links, prohibited terms, stale approvals, or conflicting versions.
- **Audience-specific views:** producing executive, product, technical, testing, and audit views without creating independent truths.
- **Lifecycle analytics:** measuring review delay, change volatility, defect escape, traceability completeness, and approval bottlenecks.

Automation should assist governance, not manufacture certainty. A tool can flag that a requirement lacks a source; it cannot determine whether the source is truthful or whether the requirement delivers value. Generative AI can help summarize, classify, compare, or draft, but human reviewers remain responsible for evidence, decisions, confidentiality, and approval.
