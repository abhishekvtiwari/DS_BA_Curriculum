## Chapter overview

A Product Requirements Document connects approved business outcomes to a coherent product change for defined users. It explains product intent, user evidence, capability scope, priorities, acceptance, experience, measurement, release context, and decision authority. It should permit design and engineering judgment while protecting the outcomes and constraints that matter.

PRD is not a universal format. A product team may use a narrative document, linked backlog, prototypes, models, decision records, and analytics plan. The controlled information can be distributed, provided each responsibility has one authoritative home and stable links. In this curriculum PRD always means Product Requirements Document; PDR is not used as a synonym.

The running example is an appointment-service product derived from the BRD in the previous chapter. The product must help people confirm, change, cancel, and recover appointments across digital and assisted channels. The PRD does not repeat the full business justification and does not specify every interface field or system transition.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- state a product vision and value proposition grounded in strategy and evidence;
- represent users, contexts, jobs, needs, and use cases without fictional certainty;
- translate user problems into product outcomes rather than requested features;
- define capability and feature boundaries with explicit exclusions;
- connect priority and roadmap decisions to value, risk, evidence, and dependencies;
- decompose outcomes into epics and stories without losing traceability;
- distinguish acceptance criteria from definition of done and outcome measures;
- integrate experience, accessibility, content, analytics, experiments, releases, risks, and approvals.

## BA-03-C09-T01 - Product Vision, Strategy, and Value Proposition

### Product direction

A product vision describes the future condition the product helps create for defined users and the organization. It is durable enough to guide decisions but specific enough to reject attractive distractions. Product strategy identifies the choices and hypotheses through which the team expects to advance that vision: target contexts, differentiated value, capability focus, constraints, and evidence plan.

The value proposition connects a user problem and desired progress to a credible product benefit and organizational value. It is not a slogan. It names who benefits, in what context, why existing alternatives are inadequate, and what evidence would show improvement. The product is one part of a service and operating model; it should not claim outcomes controlled by policy, staffing, suppliers, or user circumstances alone.

### Trace to business intent

The PRD references the approved business outcome, scope, rules, constraints, and guardrails. Product choices can refine how value is delivered but must not silently redefine the business need. If product discovery contradicts a BRD assumption, the appropriate response is controlled feedback and change, not hiding the evidence inside backlog priority.

Strategy includes explicit non-goals. A team may prioritize successful appointment change over broad personalization, decline to automate eligibility decisions, and retain assisted service. Non-goals prevent the vision from becoming an unlimited promise and protect capacity for the chosen advantage.

### Worked example - vision that guides a trade-off

Weak vision: "Build the best appointment app." Stronger direction: "Enable people using any authorized service channel to understand and change their next appointment without losing continuity, while operations safely reuse released capacity." When a loyalty-rewards idea appears, the team can see that it does not advance continuity or capacity and would introduce fairness concerns. The vision supports a defensible no.

## BA-03-C09-T02 - Users, Personas, and Jobs to Be Done

### Evidence-based user representation

Users include direct operators and people receiving value, while affected non-users may still bear consequences. Segment by relevant needs, behavior, authority, context, access, or risk rather than convenient demographics alone. Research should include successful, failed, assisted, abandoned, and edge journeys. Averages can conceal people who cannot enter the dataset.

A persona is a research synthesis, not a fictional biography. It should state evidence base, scope, behaviors, goals, constraints, and variation. Decorative names and stock photos can create unwarranted confidence. When evidence is weak, use provisional segments or hypotheses and identify the research required. Personas should not replace accessibility needs or individual accommodation.

### Jobs and context

Jobs-to-be-done language focuses on progress a person seeks in a situation. Functional, emotional, and social dimensions may matter, but the statement should avoid embedding the proposed feature. "When my schedule changes, help me move the appointment without losing my place or repeating evidence" preserves more solution freedom than "I need a reschedule button."

Context includes device, connectivity, language, ability, stress, time, assistance, privacy, and consequences. A staff persona with full system access should not stand in for customers. The PRD links user representations to research sources and review dates so a stereotype does not become permanent product truth.

### Worked example - replacing one universal user

Discovery identifies people managing their own appointment, authorized carers, service agents, interpreters, and clinicians creating urgent changes. Their goals and permissions differ. The PRD models these roles and contexts rather than one "user." It includes low-bandwidth recovery and assisted paths because evidence shows failure concentrated there, not because an invented persona preferred them.

## BA-03-C09-T03 - User Problems, Needs, and Use Cases

### Problem framing

A user problem describes the gap between desired progress and current ability in context, supported by evidence and consequence. Separate need from feature, and distinguish frequency from severity. A rare inability to recover a high-consequence appointment may deserve more attention than a frequent cosmetic irritation.

Needs can concern outcome, information, control, assurance, accessibility, privacy, support, or recovery. They should connect to business outcomes without assuming perfect alignment. A user may want unlimited last-minute change while operations needs predictable capacity; the PRD makes the trade-off and decision authority visible.

### Use cases and journeys

Use cases define actor goals and interactions with a product boundary. Journey views show activity across channels, people, and time. Scenarios vary normal, alternate, failure, abuse, accessibility, and recovery conditions. These representations expose requirements that isolated feature requests miss.

Select use cases by value and risk. Include triggers, preconditions, success, minimal guarantees, alternatives, and unresolved policy. Do not turn a use case into a click script before interaction design. Link derived stories, rules, data, and acceptance back to the governed need.

### Failure analysis - treating complaint volume as priority

Users frequently complain about calendar color but rarely report failed cancellation because they call support instead. Journey and contact data show that cancellation failure causes travel, unused capacity, and repeat work. The PRD prioritizes reliable cancellation and recovery before cosmetic choice, while keeping the color request in evidence rather than declaring it invalid.

## BA-03-C09-T04 - Product Scope, Capabilities, and Feature Boundaries

### Scope as product responsibility

Product scope defines which user outcomes, capabilities, journeys, channels, populations, markets, data, integrations, and lifecycle stages the product will address in the decision horizon. It identifies external service and system boundaries. "Appointment management" is too broad unless the included actions, roles, locations, and dependencies are named.

Capabilities express what the product enables, such as view authoritative status, change an eligible booking, recover interrupted work, or notify through an authorized preference. Features are candidate manifestations of capabilities. A feature can be changed without abandoning the capability, which preserves product learning and avoids premature solution commitment.

### Boundaries and slices

Define now, later, and not planned, with rationale. A minimum product is the smallest coherent capability set that produces safe value or credible learning, not the fewest screens. A slice must include necessary data, security, accessibility, error, support, and measurement; postponing them creates a falsely small feature.

Boundary models show responsibilities between the product, users, internal services, suppliers, and manual operations. An exclusion can remain a dependency. Assisted service may sit outside the digital product team's build scope while still being part of the user outcome and release readiness.

### Worked example - coherent first slice

The first appointment slice supports viewing, cancellation, confirmation, and clear recovery for one service type across web and assisted channels. Rescheduling remains assisted because policy and capacity rules differ by center. The product does not label a web-only happy path an MVP; it includes identity recovery, accessibility, notifications, audit, support, and measures needed for safe use.

## BA-03-C09-T05 - Prioritization, Roadmap Context, and Trade-offs

### Product priority

Priority is a decision for a horizon, not an intrinsic requirement attribute. Compare product outcomes and increments using user value, business value, risk, learning, cost of delay, effort, dependencies, reversibility, and strategic fit. Mandatory controls act as gates rather than score components that can be traded away.

Evidence and confidence matter. A high-potential idea with weak evidence may receive research priority rather than delivery commitment. A small enabling capability can precede a visible feature because it reduces uncertainty or unlocks several outcomes. Keep estimates as ranges and test whether modest changes reverse the order.

### Roadmap context

A roadmap communicates intended outcomes, sequencing logic, dependencies, and uncertainty. It is not a disguised fixed project plan. Near-term commitments may be more specific; later horizons remain outcome-oriented and conditional. Dates tied to law, contract, campaign, or operational window identify their authority and consequence.

Trade-off records explain options, criteria, distribution of benefit and harm, decision, owner, and review trigger. Product teams should not hide political or ethical decisions behind one master score. IIBA's prioritization and lifecycle practices connect value, risk, dependencies, and change [1].

### Worked example - learning before automation

Automated rescheduling scores highly on potential value but depends on inconsistent capacity rules. The team prioritizes rule discovery, a unified availability read model, and a manual-assisted pilot. The roadmap shows the automation outcome as conditional on accuracy, fairness, and workload evidence. This is not delay through analysis; it is a sequence designed to prevent scaling an unresolved policy.

## BA-03-C09-T06 - Epics, User Stories, and Requirement Decomposition

### Preserve outcome through decomposition

An epic organizes a large product outcome or capability that requires refinement. A user story is a small invitation to discuss a stakeholder-valued increment. Neither format is a complete requirement system. The PRD retains rationale, research, rules, data, quality, and cross-cutting constraints in linked authoritative artifacts rather than copying them inconsistently into every ticket.

Decompose by journey step, scenario, rule variation, data variation, operation, risk, interface, or quality level. Each child should contribute to its parent and remain traceable to the product outcome. Avoid horizontal slices that produce a database or interface with no usable behavior while being reported as customer value.

### Story quality

The familiar role-capability-benefit sentence can expose intent but does not guarantee it. A useful story names a real role and value, links to evidence, states boundaries, carries examples or acceptance, and identifies dependencies and cross-cutting requirements. "As a system" and "as a user" often hide the trigger and stakeholder.

Splitting should not defer security, accessibility, recovery, data integrity, or support indefinitely. Enabling work can be stated honestly as an enabler and linked to the outcomes it supports. The backlog is a controlled view of product work, not the sole location of product knowledge.

### Worked example - vertical decomposition

An epic to manage appointments splits first into view and cancel for one service, including identity, status, audit, notification, accessibility, and recovery. Rescheduling follows after rule validation. A separate integration enabler provides an availability contract and is linked to both rescheduling and assisted service. The team avoids stories named "build database" and "create UI" that cannot be accepted as user outcomes.

## BA-03-C09-T07 - Acceptance Criteria and Definition of Done

### Two control levels

Acceptance criteria state the conditions under which a product item is acceptable for its intended behavior or outcome. They cover relevant normal, boundary, alternate, failure, authorization, data, quality, and recovery conditions. Criteria are not test scripts and do not prove later benefit realization.

Definition of Done is a shared quality policy applied to increments, such as reviewed code, required tests, security checks, accessibility evidence, documentation, monitoring, and deployment readiness. Acceptance criteria vary by item; the Definition of Done supplies recurring controls. Neither should contain a hidden product requirement that only one team knows.

### Evidence and authority

Define who accepts the item, which evidence is required, and how exceptions are governed. Given-When-Then can clarify event behavior, but decision tables, state models, examples, measures, and inspection may suit other conditions. Cucumber describes Then as an observable outcome rather than an internal implementation detail [2].

Done does not equal released, adopted, or valuable. Release readiness includes dependencies and operations; outcome evaluation occurs after use. A team may technically complete an increment but decide not to release it because a risk condition or external dependency remains unresolved.

### Worked example - acceptance beyond the happy path

Cancellation criteria cover eligible and ineligible states, boundary cutoff, duplicate requests, dependency timeout, notification failure, audit evidence, and accessible confirmation. The Definition of Done adds peer review, automated regression, security and accessibility checks, operational dashboards, and support guidance. The product owner accepts behavior while specialist controls retain their owners.

## BA-03-C09-T08 - User Experience, Accessibility, and Content Requirements

### Experience as requirement information

Experience requirements describe task outcomes, information, interaction constraints, continuity, trust, and support across the journey. They should not freeze visual design prematurely. Research, prototypes, content, and usability evaluation help teams discover what behavior and information users need.

Content is functional. Labels, instructions, errors, confirmations, notifications, help, and policy explanations affect decisions and recovery. Requirements identify content purpose, owner, authority, reading needs, language, personalization, versioning, and channel consistency. Error messages should explain what happened, what remains safe, and what the person can do next without exposing sensitive information.

### Accessibility and inclusion

Accessibility is an end-to-end product property. Reference applicable WCAG 2.2 requirements, then specify product behaviors for keyboard, focus, semantics, reflow, contrast, alternatives, timing, errors, authentication, documents, and assisted service [3]. Automated scans are incomplete; include manual and assistive-technology evaluation and disabled-user evidence.

Inclusive requirements examine device, connectivity, language, literacy, stress, environment, and support. An alternative route must provide equivalent outcome, dignity, cost, and timeliness. Privacy and security controls should be designed with accessibility rather than creating a barrier and calling support the exception.

### Worked example - recoverable form experience

The appointment form saves permitted progress, identifies errors next to fields and in a summary, preserves valid input, announces status to assistive technology, and allows return after interrupted identity verification. Content explains cutoff rules before commitment. An assisted agent sees the same authoritative status. The requirement protects continuity while design remains free to determine layout through evaluation.

## BA-03-C09-T09 - Product Metrics, Analytics, and Experiment Requirements

### Measurement model

Product metrics connect user behavior and service performance to outcomes. Define each measure's decision, population, event, numerator, denominator, segment, source, latency, quality, owner, and response. Avoid vanity measures that increase without demonstrating value. Pair success with guardrails such as errors, exclusion, support demand, complaints, privacy events, or unequal outcomes.

Analytics requirements specify event semantics, properties, identity boundaries, consent or other authority, minimization, retention, access, quality, and versioning. Event names alone are not a measurement plan. Changes to product behavior can break historical comparability; metrics and experiments therefore need lineage and release context.

### Experiments and causal claims

An experiment states hypothesis, population, intervention, comparison, outcome measure, guardrails, duration or stopping logic, assignment method, analysis plan, and decision rule. Ethical, legal, operational, and statistical review should be proportionate to consequence. Do not experiment by withholding mandatory access or exposing users to unacceptable harm.

Results include uncertainty and limitations. A statistically detectable effect may be too small to matter; a useful segment effect may be hidden in aggregate. Novelty, interference, seasonality, attrition, and instrumentation can invalidate inference. NIST guidance on engineering statistics illustrates the importance of planned measurement and uncertainty rather than relying on isolated averages [4].

### Failure analysis - metric improves, outcome worsens

A reminder test increases message clicks, so the team declares success. Completed appointments do not improve, opt-outs rise, and support contacts increase because the link opens an inaccessible login path. The corrected experiment uses completion as outcome, contact burden and opt-out as guardrails, and accessible task completion as a requirement. Click rate becomes diagnostic, not value.

## BA-03-C09-T10 - Releases, Dependencies, Risks, and Approvals

### Release as an outcome decision

A release packages product behavior for a defined population and operating context. Release requirements include scope, audience, data or configuration migration, feature controls, compatibility, communications, training, support, monitoring, continuity, rollback, and success review. Deployment is a technical event; release is an organizational and user-facing decision.

Dependencies can include policy, data, interface, supplier, platform, staffing, content, legal review, accessibility evidence, and operational readiness. Identify owner, commitment, status, lead time, fallback, and consequence. A product should not inherit an optimistic supplier date as fact.

### Risk and approval

Product risk covers desirability, usability, feasibility, viability, security, privacy, accessibility, safety, operations, compliance, and reputation. Define exposure, mitigation, evidence, owner, residual state, and escalation. Reversible pilots, limited populations, feature flags, and rollback can reduce uncertainty but do not excuse inadequate safeguards.

Approvals identify exact scope and version, authority, evidence, conditions, accepted risks, and effective point. Product acceptance, technical readiness, specialist assurance, and business release authority may sit with different people. A concise decision record preserves accountability without turning every backlog item into a committee event.

### Worked example - conditional phased release

The service releases cancellation to one center after contract, accessibility, security, reconciliation, support, and rollback evidence pass. Rescheduling remains disabled pending rule approval. Monitoring covers errors, completion, assisted demand, duplicate state, and unequal failure. Expansion requires thresholds for two weeks and review of complaints. The release plan converts uncertainty into controlled evidence.

## Integrated PRD model

The PRD connects vision to evidence, product scope, usable increments, acceptance, experience, measurement, and release. It should answer why this product change, for whom, which problem and outcome, what capability boundary, what is prioritized now, how acceptable behavior and quality will be judged, what evidence will guide learning, and who can authorize release or change.

| PRD responsibility | Primary question | Controlled relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Vision and strategy | What future and choices guide the product? | Business outcome and product decisions |
| Users and jobs | Who seeks what progress in which context? | Research evidence and affected groups |
| Problems and use cases | What prevents progress and how does interaction vary? | Needs, journeys, scenarios and rules |
| Scope and capabilities | What product responsibility is included now? | Product boundary, dependencies and exclusions |
| Priority and roadmap | Why this sequence under current evidence? | Value, risk, effort, time and learning |
| Decomposition | How does work preserve outcome and traceability? | Epics, stories, enablers and models |
| Acceptance and done | What evidence makes an increment acceptable? | Criteria, shared quality and verification |
| Experience and content | How can people understand, act and recover? | Research, design constraints and accessibility |
| Metrics and experiments | What will change a product decision? | Events, measures, hypotheses and guardrails |
| Release and approval | Is the product safe and ready for this population? | Readiness, authority, monitoring and rollback |

### End-to-end worked example - from BRD to product evidence

The BRD authorizes equitable appointment completion and released-capacity reuse. The PRD narrows the first product outcome to successful viewing and cancellation. Research identifies roles and recovery barriers. Capabilities define authoritative status, eligible cancellation, confirmation, assisted continuity, and measurement. Stories deliver a vertical slice; rules remain linked to their policy source; accessibility and privacy constrain the journey.

Acceptance evidence shows correct behavior and quality. Release evidence shows data, support, monitoring, and rollback readiness. Product metrics later show whether cancellation succeeds, capacity is reused, and burden is distributed fairly. If evidence contradicts the original assumption, the PRD updates product strategy and may trigger a BRD change. Traceability enables learning in both directions.

## Advanced synthesis

### Product requirements as decisions under uncertainty

A PRD is strongest when it distinguishes evidence, hypothesis, choice, commitment, and result. User research provides evidence; a proposed explanation or value mechanism is a hypothesis; scope and priority are choices; an approved release is a commitment; observed behavior is a result. Collapsing these categories creates roadmaps that look certain but cannot learn.

Discovery and delivery interact. A prototype can test comprehension before implementation; a concierge service can test demand and operating work; a limited release can test behavior under safeguards. Each method needs a stated question and decision rule. Producing a prototype or experiment is not success by itself.

### Cross-product and platform considerations

One user outcome may cross several products, channels, and teams. The PRD identifies end-to-end ownership and interface obligations without pretending one team controls everything. Local metrics can conflict with service outcomes: reducing call duration may increase repeat contact, while increasing digital adoption may exclude people who need assistance.

Platform capabilities require consumer evidence and governance. A preference service, identity component, or notification platform should define stable semantics, service levels, privacy, accessibility, compatibility, and adoption support. Avoid building a generic platform from speculative future needs. Trace each capability to real consumers while preserving an extensible boundary.

### Product debt and retirement

Product debt includes confusing behavior, unsupported variants, inconsistent rules, inaccessible journeys, obsolete experiments, stale metrics, and manual controls that were never retired. It is not purely technical. The PRD can make debt visible through impact, users, risk, and planned treatment rather than placing it behind feature demand.

Retirement is a product outcome. Requirements cover communication, alternatives, data export or retention, contract and supplier exit, disabled integrations, archived decisions, and support. A feature may have few active users but high consequence for them. Usage count informs the decision; it does not replace impact analysis.

### Documentation proportionality

Teams should tailor the PRD, not abandon controlled knowledge. A low-risk experiment may need a one-page hypothesis, linked research, safeguards, metrics, and decision. A regulated multi-channel service may need formal models and approvals. The amount of documentation follows consequence, complexity, longevity, distribution, and cost of misunderstanding.

Stable identifiers allow precise review in working files. The learner-facing collated publication removes those internal codes to improve reading. Both forms derive from the same controlled content. Manual rewriting between them would create exactly the divergence that requirements governance is intended to prevent.

### Product discovery evidence map

A discovery evidence map links each important product belief to its source, confidence, consequence if wrong, and next test. Beliefs may concern user prevalence, problem severity, willingness or ability to change, product usability, operational capacity, technical feasibility, commercial viability, or regulatory acceptability. Treating them separately prevents a convincing prototype from being interpreted as proof of demand or a strong survey result from being interpreted as proof of feasibility.

Research methods answer different questions. Interviews can reveal motivations and vocabulary; observation exposes real work and workaround; analytics shows recorded behavior at scale; usability evaluation examines whether people can use a proposed interaction; prototypes test comprehension and flow; experiments estimate causal effect under conditions. No method automatically represents people who were unable to access the service. The UK Service Standard emphasizes understanding users and providing a joined-up, accessible service rather than optimizing one interface in isolation [5].

The PRD records synthesis, not raw personal data. Findings name population and context, include contradictory evidence, and distinguish observation from interpretation. Research repositories require access and retention controls. Quotations can illustrate a pattern but should not become a substitute for prevalence or decision authority.

### Worked example - prototype evidence has a boundary

Ten participants complete a rescheduling prototype and rate it clearly. The result supports comprehension of the tested flow, not national demand, technical feasibility, operational capacity, or performance for people using assistive technology who were absent from the sample. The PRD marks those unanswered questions and adds an accessible evaluation, rules review, integration spike, and limited operational pilot before committing to broad release.

### Decision architecture

Product documents contain several decision types: vision, target users, scope, priority, experience principles, metric definitions, release, and risk acceptance. These decisions can have different owners. A product manager may own product direction within business scope, while a policy owner controls eligibility and a security owner accepts specified exposure. A decision register prevents "product approval" from implying authority over every specialist domain.

Decision records include context, options, evidence, criteria, participants, authority, choice, dissent, consequences, and review trigger. They are especially valuable when a choice cannot be inferred from the current backlog. A later team should know why assisted service remained in scope, why automation stopped at triage, or why a supplier capability was rejected.

Product governance should be lightweight enough to use. Reserve formal approval for decisions with material commitment or consequence; use delegated authority for routine refinement. The controlled record can be concise. The key is that a change to approved intent is visible and assessed, while normal elaboration does not wait for a committee.

### Outcome-oriented roadmap construction

Begin roadmap design with outcomes and evidence gaps, not a list of features and dates. For each horizon, state the user or service condition to improve, the principal hypothesis, the measure and guardrail, major dependency, confidence, and decision expected. Candidate capabilities can be shown without turning them into promises. This structure allows learning to change the solution while retaining accountability for direction.

Commitment language matters. "Now" can mean funded and ready, "next" can mean intended subject to evidence, and "later" can mean option. Define local terms. A fixed external deadline should identify the required outcome and authority; adding arbitrary dates to every idea creates false precision. Communicate changes with rationale so roadmap movement does not look like unexplained instability.

Dependencies are not always reasons to wait. The team can negotiate an interface contract, use a temporary manual service, stub a low-risk dependency, or deliver a thin slice. Each alternative has risk and retirement needs. The roadmap represents the dependency treatment, not only an arrow.

### Worked example - roadmap by outcome

The first horizon targets successful cancellation and released-capacity reuse. The second targets rule-governed rescheduling after policy validation. A third explores proactive capacity recommendations, explicitly excluding automated adverse decisions. When data shows that cancellation is limited by contact preference rather than interface clarity, the first horizon changes to include preference repair. The roadmap remains aligned to the outcome instead of defending a predetermined screen.

### Product requirement quality

Product requirements need clear identity, source, rationale, owner, state, applicability, priority, acceptance, and trace relationships according to risk. The statement should be understandable to business, design, engineering, quality, and operations without prescribing accidental implementation. Examples and models clarify meaning. A prototype alone is insufficient because it may not express data, rule, quality, failure, or support behavior.

Atomicity is purpose-relative. One backlog item may describe a coherent vertical slice containing several observable effects. Individual functional requirements later separate behaviors for traceability and testing. Splitting the PRD into tiny statements too early can destroy the narrative of user value. The product layer should be precise enough for scope and acceptance while allowing functional elaboration.

Quality includes consistency across text, prototypes, diagrams, analytics plans, and backlog views. If a prototype allows cancellation after cutoff while the rule catalogue prohibits it, the visual design is part of the contradiction. Product reviews should compare representations, not examine each artifact in isolation.

### Content and trust

Content requirements begin with what people must understand or decide. Identify authoritative facts, required disclosures, tone constraints, reading needs, localization, timing, channel, ownership, and change process. Avoid specifying exact copy as a permanent requirement when content design needs evidence and iteration, but preserve mandated wording and its source where applicable.

Trust depends on accurate expectation. A confirmation should not claim completion when a request is merely queued. A personalized recommendation should disclose relevant limits without overwhelming the user. Error content should not blame the person or expose security information. Notifications should state whether action is required, identify a safe route back to the service, and avoid unnecessary sensitive content on shared devices.

Localization is more than translation. Dates, numbers, addresses, names, reading order, legal text, cultural context, and available support can vary. Requirements identify authoritative locale data and review. Machine translation can assist but does not remove the need for qualified review where misunderstanding has consequence.

### Worked example - status language changes behavior

The product displays "Appointment changed" immediately after sending a request to a legacy scheduler. Users stop attending the original slot, although some changes later fail. The requirement is corrected to show "Change requested" until authoritative confirmation, preserve the original appointment, display expected timing, and provide recovery. Content, state behavior, notification, and audit now agree.

### Analytics integrity and privacy

Instrumentation should be designed with the product behavior. Each event specifies trigger, actor or anonymous boundary, properties, timestamps, version, duplicate handling, offline behavior, and expected sequence. Validation checks that an event represents the intended business occurrence. A button-click event does not prove task completion; a completion event should reflect authoritative state.

Collect only data needed for approved decisions and safeguards. Access, retention, aggregation, deletion, and supplier processing follow privacy requirements. Small segments can create re-identification risk. Experiment assignment and analysis may use personal or sensitive attributes only under appropriate authority and controls. A desire to "measure everything" is not a product requirement.

Metric governance includes changes. If cancellation eligibility changes, the completion population and comparison baseline may change. Version the definition and annotate releases so trends remain interpretable. Reprocess historical data only when method and authority allow it, and disclose breaks rather than smoothing them away.

### Operating product requirements

The PRD should identify how the capability is supported after release. Operations need health and business measures, alert ownership, exception queues, service commitments, support tools, escalation, known limitations, and recovery. Product teams need user feedback and decision cadence. Suppliers need clear interface and incident obligations. Without this operating model, a technically successful launch can create unmanaged work.

Capacity is a product constraint. An accessible assisted route is not real if staffing cannot meet demand. A human-review safeguard is not effective if queues exceed reviewer capacity. Requirements should forecast ranges, define overload behavior, and specify which outcomes are protected under degraded conditions. Pilots test workload as well as user response.

Support evidence feeds discovery. Categorize contacts and workarounds without assuming every call is a user training problem. A rise in contact after release may reveal unclear content, inaccessible interaction, data inconsistency, or a product rule that conflicts with user need. The product measure links contact cause to journey and release version.

### Worked example - release succeeds technically but not operationally

Digital cancellation launches with low error rates. Support calls rise because carers cannot act for represented users, and centers manually reconcile duplicate legacy statuses. The PRD had omitted delegated authority and reconciliation. A controlled change adds role behavior, authoritative status mapping, queue capacity, and measures. The team delays expansion until the operating evidence passes.

### Product lifecycle and retirement

Product requirements persist through introduction, growth, change, and retirement. Review assumptions, user evidence, rules, metrics, dependencies, and risks at defined triggers. A feature that once created value may become redundant, harmful, noncompliant, or costly. Retention by inertia consumes support and increases complexity.

Retirement requires affected-user analysis, alternatives, communication, data handling, contract and integration changes, archive, support, and evidence. Give users sufficient time and accessible routes. A dormant feature can still carry data or security risk. Trace links reveal downstream consumers and tests that can be safely retired.

Outcome evaluation closes the loop. Compare observed results with hypotheses and guardrails, explain external factors, and decide to scale, adapt, stop, or investigate. Preserve negative results and changed decisions as organizational learning. A product culture that publishes only successful experiments corrupts the evidence available for future prioritization.

### Final PRD review

Review the PRD from several directions. Start with each capability and trace upward to a user problem and business outcome; then start with each approved outcome and trace downward to product scope, acceptance, measurement, and release evidence. Examine whether excluded users, failure paths, non-digital work, or cross-cutting controls disappear between views. Compare prose with prototypes, journey maps, roadmap, metrics, and backlog.

Ask an independent reader to state what the product will do now, what it will not do, who benefits, who may be burdened, which claims remain hypotheses, and what evidence changes the decision. If those answers depend on private meeting history, the PRD is not self-sufficient for its purpose. If the document dictates detailed design without necessity, it has crossed into an FRD or design specification.

Ready for review does not mean self-approved. It means controlled identifiers, sources, boundaries, examples, measures, risks, and decision rights are present enough for others to challenge. Review comments remain linked to the exact version, and owner acceptance follows correction or explicit disposition. This protects the distinction between authoring evidence and independent judgment.

The controlled publication records that distinction clearly.

## Common misconceptions

- A PRD is not a feature wish list or a renamed BRD.
- Personas are not fictional substitutes for research.
- An MVP still includes controls required for safe, accessible operation.
- Backlog tickets do not replace product rationale and cross-cutting requirements.
- Definition of Done is not item-specific acceptance or business value.
- More tracked events do not automatically improve decisions.
- Deployment does not prove release readiness, adoption, or outcome.
- Agile product work still requires controlled decisions and traceability.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Product choices distribute access, attention, friction, and risk. Research samples, default settings, metric definitions, experiment populations, and prioritization can systematically exclude less powerful groups. Include affected perspectives, protect evidence, use guardrails, and preserve challenge. Do not treat engagement as consent to unrelated data use.

Analysts and product managers do not independently approve legal interpretation, security risk, accessibility conformance, or statistical validity unless assigned that authority and competence. Specialists inform decisions while accountable owners retain approval. Generative tools can assist synthesis, but invented research, users, citations, or test evidence are unacceptable.
