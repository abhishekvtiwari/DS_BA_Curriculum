## Chapter overview

Requirements elicitation is disciplined discovery, not passive note-taking. It combines existing evidence, stakeholder knowledge, observation, models, questions, and iterative confirmation to reveal needs, rules, assumptions, exceptions, and constraints. This chapter develops six complementary ways to shape elicited information into analyzable requirements.

The chapter assumes the stakeholder and elicitation foundations from BA-M02. Its concern is how discovery becomes requirements knowledge while preserving sources and uncertainty. It contains Phase 1 theory only.

BA-M02 teaches how to plan and conduct interviews, workshops, observation, document analysis, surveys, contextual inquiry, and validation. This chapter does not repeat those method instructions. It begins after a method has produced evidence and asks a different question: how does an analyst transform evidence into a coherent requirement model without losing provenance, conflict, boundary, or uncertainty? IIBA treats preparing, conducting, and confirming elicitation as connected work rather than a single meeting activity [1].

| Transformation problem | Primary representation | Control question |
|---|---|---|
| The problem space is uncertain | Discovery frame | Which decision, unknown, and evidence gap are we addressing? |
| The scope is too broad to reason about | Decomposition model | Do the parts retain their relationship to the whole outcome? |
| Context and variation are hidden | Scenario | What happens across normal, alternate, failure, and recovery paths? |
| Interaction with a defined system is unclear | Use case | Which external actor seeks which result from which system boundary? |
| A small delivery conversation is needed | User story | What stakeholder value is negotiable, and what supporting detail remains linked? |
| Policy or decision logic is buried in workflow | Business rule or decision model | Who owns the rule, when does it apply, and how are conflicts resolved? |

These representations overlap by design. A scenario can reveal a use case; a use-case step can invoke a business rule; a user story can deliver part of a use case. The analyst does not choose one universal format. The analyst chooses the smallest set of representations that allows the intended audience to detect missing behavior, make decisions, and verify results.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- plan discovery around decisions, stakeholders, evidence, risk, and unknowns;
- decompose broad outcomes without losing end-to-end value;
- use scenarios and use cases to expose normal, alternate, and exception behavior;
- use user stories without treating their sentence pattern as a complete specification;
- express business rules independently from processes and interfaces;
- confirm elicitation results and preserve disagreement, assumptions, and provenance.

## BA-03-C02-T01 - Discovery

### Concept

Discovery is the purposeful investigation of a need and its context before and during requirement development. It seeks enough shared understanding to make the next responsible decision; it is not a promise that uncertainty can be eliminated before delivery.

### Discovery frame

Define the decision to support, scope boundary, stakeholders and affected groups, known evidence, hypotheses, constraints, risks, unknowns, and confirmation route. Combine methods because each has blind spots: interviews reveal perspective, workshops expose interaction, observation reveals actual work, document analysis reveals formal rules, data reveals patterns, and prototypes reveal interpretation.

Discovery outputs may include a problem frame, glossary, process or context model, evidence register, assumptions, stakeholder needs, rules, candidate requirements, conflicts, and open questions. Preserve provenance. A statement from one interview is evidence of that person's view, not automatic proof of an organization-wide need.

### Discovery as a managed learning system

Discovery is governed learning, not a ceremonial project phase. It may occur before funding, during product discovery, while requirements are refined, or after implementation exposes a false assumption. A good frame makes the learning objective explicit: "Determine why eligible applicants abandon identity verification and which intervention is safe to test" is more useful than "Gather portal requirements." The former identifies a population, observed condition, decision, and boundary.

An evidence map prevents meetings from becoming the default method. For each uncertainty, record the current claim, source, confidence, consequence if wrong, and next evidence needed. Operational data can show the distribution of an issue; observation can reveal workarounds; interviews can explain goals and interpretations; documents can establish formal authority; prototypes can expose how people understand a proposed interaction. Triangulation does not require agreement. Conflicting sources may reveal different definitions, time periods, populations, incentives, or parts of a process.

Discovery also needs a decision cadence. Open questions should have owners and dates. Assumptions should identify how and when they will be tested. Findings should be communicated in forms that the decision makers and affected participants can examine. Confirmation does not mean asking everyone to approve one transcript. It means checking that the interpreted information accurately represents the evidence and clearly preserving disagreement. This aligns with IIBA's separation of conducting elicitation, confirming results, communicating information, and managing collaboration [1].

### Worked example - evidence overturns the requested feature

A support director requests a chatbot because customer wait time increased. The discovery frame separates three questions: which contacts are avoidable, where delay occurs, and which customers are harmed. Call data shows that password-reset contacts are frequent but short. Observation shows that address-change agents wait for manual fraud decisions. Complaint analysis shows that customers are most frustrated by missing status information after submitting evidence.

The team does not reject the chatbot outright. It records that the requested feature addresses a different hypothesis from the dominant delay. Candidate requirements instead concern visible case status, evidence completeness, fraud-decision turnaround, and a safe escalation route. The recommendation is traceable to evidence, while the chatbot remains an option for a narrower information need.

### Iteration and stopping

Stop or narrow discovery when decision-critical questions have credible answers, major affected perspectives are represented, high-risk assumptions have a validation route, and remaining uncertainty is accepted by the correct authority. Deadlines do not transform unknowns into facts; record residual uncertainty and the cost of learning later.

## BA-03-C02-T02 - Decomposition

### Concept

Decomposition breaks a broad need, outcome, process, capability, feature, rule set, or requirement into smaller elements that can be understood, owned, prioritized, and verified. It is useful only when the relationships among the parts remain visible.

### Approaches

Decompose by outcome, capability, stakeholder, process stage, event, business rule, information object, geography, channel, risk, or system boundary. Choose the dimension that clarifies the current question. A capability hierarchy may support scope; an event model may expose missing behaviors; a data model may reveal semantic gaps.

Each child should contribute to its parent, and the set of children should cover the parent's intended scope without unjustified overlap. This does not require a mathematically perfect tree. Requirements often have many-to-many relationships, so trace links and models are more honest than forcing every item under one parent.

### Selecting a decomposition axis

The decomposition axis should match the decision. Capability decomposition is useful when leaders need investment boundaries. Process decomposition clarifies stages, handoffs, and control points. Event decomposition exposes what the solution must respond to. Information decomposition clarifies concepts and ownership. Rule decomposition separates decisions and policy. Stakeholder decomposition reveals different outcomes and access. A team may use several views, but it should not mix axes inside one hierarchy without labeling the change.

A parent-child relationship needs a stated meaning. "Part of," "supports," "depends on," and "is a variant of" are different relationships. If a hierarchy uses all of them as unnamed indentation, reviewers will infer incompatible meanings. For example, payment validation may be part of checkout, depend on an external provider, and support the fraud-control outcome. A graph with typed links represents this more honestly than placing the item under three copied headings.

Completeness is evaluated relative to the parent's boundary, not by counting children. When decomposing a customer journey, check triggers, entry and exit conditions, decisions, information, handoffs, exceptions, controls, and affected groups. When decomposing behavior, check normal, alternate, failure, retry, cancellation, timeout, duplicate, and recovery paths. When decomposing data, check definition, source, lifecycle, quality, access, and retention. Different subjects need different completeness questions.

### Worked example - decomposing without losing the outcome

A business requirement states that eligible grant applicants should receive a decision within ten working days. A team initially decomposes by department: intake, finance, legal, and approval. That view clarifies ownership but hides the applicant's waiting time. The analyst adds an event-and-outcome view: application received, completeness established, evidence requested, assessment completed, decision authorized, applicant notified, and correction handled.

Requirements are linked between the views rather than copied. The finance check remains owned by finance, while its relationship to the end-to-end clock is explicit. The model reveals that most delay occurs before completeness is established, not during authorization. Decomposition now supports both accountability and the business outcome.

### Failure modes

Over-decomposition produces fragments with no user value or decision context. Under-decomposition hides incompatible rules and makes verification vague. Other errors include splitting by organizational ownership when the customer journey crosses teams, duplicating shared requirements, and losing non-functional or transition concerns during feature breakdown.

## BA-03-C02-T03 - Scenarios

### Concept

A scenario is a contextual narrative of how an actor, organization, or system moves from an initiating situation toward an outcome. Scenarios help participants reason about time, context, decisions, exceptions, and impact in language that can bridge business and technical perspectives.

### Scenario anatomy

State the purpose, actors, trigger, preconditions, context, main sequence, variations, exceptions, outcome, postconditions, information used, relevant rules, and unresolved questions. Include unsuccessful and recovery paths. A "happy path" alone systematically hides the conditions that often create cost and harm.

Scenarios can be current-state, future-state, misuse, failure, accessibility, support, or operational scenarios. They are analytical devices, not proof that every possible event has been found. Use frequency and impact to select additional paths, and avoid inventing a fictional user whose convenience erases diverse real users.

### Use variation to find requirements

A scenario should be concrete enough to reveal conditions while remaining clear about what is illustrative. Name the actor's goal, environment, trigger, relevant state, and outcome. Then vary one dimension at a time: the user cannot access the normal channel; information arrives late; a dependency is unavailable; authority changes; a request is duplicated; or the user abandons and returns. Each variation tests whether the emerging requirement set is robust.

Current-state scenarios expose work, evidence, delay, and coping behavior without assuming the existing process is correct. Future-state scenarios test how a proposed capability could change outcomes and responsibilities. Misuse and abuse scenarios explore intentional harmful behavior. Recovery scenarios ask how people and systems return to a safe state after failure. Accessibility scenarios examine interaction under different abilities, devices, environments, and assistance needs. These are distinct analytical lenses, not decorative personas.

Scenario selection is risk based. A rare path may deserve attention when its consequence involves safety, rights, financial loss, or irreversible data change. A common path may need less documentation if it is simple and observable. The scenario register should record why a path was selected, which requirements it supports, and which questions remain unresolved.

### Worked example - a normal flow hides a serious exception

A delivery service describes a simple scenario: a customer changes the delivery address before dispatch and receives confirmation. Variation reveals a case in which an attacker changes the address after taking over the account. Another variation involves a customer with no access to the original contact channel. The initial requirement to "allow address changes" is therefore incomplete.

The scenarios produce candidate requirements for re-authentication, notification to established channels, risk-based holds, recovery, evidence, and accessible assisted service. They also reveal a policy decision about the latest permitted change. The narrative is not copied directly into the specification; it provides traceable evidence for behavior, rules, data, and quality constraints.

### From narrative to requirements

Extract candidate functions, rules, data, quality constraints, transition needs, and acceptance conditions. Trace them back to the scenario and confirm them separately. Do not copy narrative sentences into a requirement set without removing ambiguity and establishing ownership.

## BA-03-C02-T04 - Use Cases

### Concept

A use case describes interactions between an actor and a system boundary that achieve a goal. It organizes functional behavior around observable value and explicitly separates the main success flow from alternate and exception flows.

### Essential elements

Record identifier, goal, scope, level, primary actor, supporting actors, trigger, preconditions, minimal guarantees, success guarantees, main flow, extensions, business rules, data, special requirements, and open issues. The system boundary matters: if it shifts, actor responsibilities and requirements may change.

Use cases are strongest for interaction-rich behavior and weaker for broad strategy, data semantics, quality attributes, organizational change, or continuous algorithms. Complement them with other models. A use case should not become a click-by-click screen script unless interface detail is genuinely constrained.

### Boundary and goal discipline

The system boundary is the subject whose behavior is being specified. An actor is outside that boundary and interacts with it to achieve a goal. OMG's UML specification uses this distinction: actors represent users or external systems, while use cases specify required usages of the subject [2]. If the boundary changes from a customer portal to an end-to-end service, an internal verification component may become part of the subject rather than an external actor. Unstated boundary changes create contradictory responsibilities.

Use-case level matters. A summary use case such as "Manage a claim" may organize scope but is too broad for detailed interaction. A user-goal use case such as "Submit supporting evidence" can describe a meaningful result in one session or coherent episode. A lower-level subfunction such as "Validate file type" may support several use cases but usually does not express stakeholder value by itself. Labeling the level prevents reviewers from comparing unlike artifacts.

Preconditions describe what must already be true; they should not hide behavior the subject is responsible for establishing. A success guarantee states the condition after successful completion. A minimal guarantee states what remains true even when the goal fails, such as preserving an audit record or avoiding duplicate payment. Extensions identify the step and condition at which behavior diverges. They should explain whether the flow resumes, terminates, compensates, or transfers responsibility.

### Worked example - use case with recovery

In "Submit expense claim," the employee selects eligible expenses, provides evidence, and confirms submission. The main flow records the claim and returns an identifier. Extensions cover missing evidence, duplicate expense detection, an unavailable finance service, withdrawal before approval, and a user who loses connectivity after confirmation.

The last extension matters: if the user retries without knowing whether submission succeeded, the system must prevent accidental duplicates and provide a way to retrieve the result. That behavior might not emerge from a screen mock-up. The use case exposes the interaction and recovery responsibility, while separate data, performance, and security requirements provide the conditions it cannot express well.

### Quality

Flows should use observable actor intentions and system responsibilities, not hidden implementation. Extensions must state where they branch and how the use case ends or resumes. Confirm authorization, invalid input, dependency failure, cancellation, timeout, repeat submission, and recovery where relevant.

## BA-03-C02-T05 - User Stories

### Concept

A user story is a concise placeholder for a conversation about a small increment of stakeholder value. A common form identifies a role, capability, and benefit, but the sentence is not a complete requirement and is not valuable merely because it follows the template.

### Good use

A story should connect to an outcome, identify the real role, state a meaningful goal, carry acceptance criteria, expose dependencies and quality constraints, and be small enough to discuss and deliver responsibly. The "so that" clause explains value and should not restate the feature. Technical or enabling work may require a different expression rather than a fictional user.

Stories can be split by workflow step, rule variation, data variation, operation, scenario, risk, interface, or quality level. Splitting should preserve a coherent vertical slice where possible. Do not split in a way that delivers an unusable interface, bypasses security, or postpones accessibility indefinitely.

### Conversation, evidence, and acceptance

The card or backlog line is an index into a conversation. The conversation should establish the stakeholder and context, evidence for the need, intended outcome, boundaries, assumptions, dependencies, risks, and acceptance conditions. A story that carries none of this information is not lightweight; it transfers analytical work into memory and makes later review unreliable.

The role should describe a meaningful class of actor, not whoever is operating the keyboard. "As the system" usually hides a trigger or business reason. "As a user" hides differences in authority and outcome. Enabling work can be expressed directly: "Provide an auditable address-verification service so that customer-facing channels apply the same authorized rule." Forcing it into a fictional person's voice adds ceremony without clarity.

The value clause explains why the capability matters and allows prioritization. "So that I can click submit" merely restates the function. A stronger clause identifies the outcome: "so that I know my evidence was received and can refer to it during follow-up." Acceptance criteria define observable boundaries and examples; they do not replace system-wide quality, data, security, or policy requirements. Those remain linked and reusable rather than copied into every story.

Splitting tests whether a smaller increment can produce safe learning or value. A vertical slice may support one rule variation from interaction through data and feedback. A horizontal slice that builds only a database table may be necessary technical work but should not be misrepresented as user value. When a split postpones error handling, accessibility, security, or audit, the apparent smallness is achieved by hiding required scope.

### Worked example - repairing a template-compliant story

"As a user, I want a dashboard so that I can see information" follows the familiar form but contains no useful decision. Discovery identifies the actual role and outcome: an operations supervisor needs to identify cases likely to breach the response commitment early enough to reallocate work. Evidence shows that age alone is misleading because paused cases and high-risk cases follow different rules.

The story becomes a conversation about a prioritized queue view, rule definitions, freshness, access, explanations, and intervention. Acceptance examples cover paused cases, missing risk data, equal-age cases, stale feeds, and unauthorized viewers. The dashboard is no longer the requirement; it is a candidate means of supporting a decision.

### Limits

A backlog can hide system-wide constraints, domain rules, transitions, and relationships. Use supporting models, rule catalogs, interface specifications, and trace links. "As a user" is usually evidence that stakeholder analysis is incomplete.

## BA-03-C02-T06 - Business Rules

### Concept

A business rule is an authoritative statement that defines or constrains business structure, decisions, behavior, or derivation. Rules apply across processes and systems and should be managed independently from the particular workflow or screen that currently implements them.

### Rule forms

Structural rules define terms and relationships. Operative rules require, prohibit, or permit behavior. Decision rules determine an outcome from conditions. Derivation rules calculate or infer a value. Each rule needs an identifier, precise statement, vocabulary, source, authority, jurisdiction or scope, effective period, exceptions, rationale, status, and trace links.

Decision tables and decision models help reveal missing, overlapping, or contradictory combinations. Natural-language rules should avoid undefined qualifiers such as normally, appropriate, recent, or high unless their interpretation is governed. Separate policy intent from executable logic so that implementation detail does not silently redefine authority.

### Separate decision logic from process sequence

A process describes when work occurs and who participates. A business rule constrains or derives what is permitted or concluded. Embedding a rule only inside a process step makes it difficult to reuse, test, version, and trace. For example, "assessor checks whether the applicant is eligible" identifies an activity but not the eligibility logic. A rule catalogue or decision model identifies inputs, outcomes, source authority, and effective dates independently from the workflow.

Decision tables are useful when conditions combine. Every row should represent a permitted combination or clearly indicate that it is impossible or incomplete. Overlapping rows may produce more than one answer; missing rows leave behavior undefined. Hit policy—the rule for how multiple matches are handled—must be explicit when using a formal notation. OMG describes DMN as a notation for specifying business decisions and business rules that can complement process models [3]. The notation can improve precision, but ownership and source authority remain organizational questions.

Rules have temporal and jurisdictional scope. A threshold may change on a future date; a policy may differ by region; an existing case may continue under the earlier rule. The requirement model should distinguish the event date, decision date, and effective rule version. Retrospectively applying a new rule can create incorrect outcomes even when the implementation is internally consistent.

### Worked example - exposing an incomplete eligibility rule

A policy summary says, "Applicants under 25 with low income receive the concession." The analyst asks how age is calculated, which income period applies, what counts as income, whether dependants change the threshold, which residency rules apply, and what happens when evidence conflicts. A decision table shows several undefined combinations, including an applicant who turns 25 during assessment and an applicant with incomplete income evidence.

The policy owner clarifies the effective date and referral conditions. The analyst records each rule with a source and version, links the decision service to the process, and creates examples for permitted, denied, and referred outcomes. The implementation can now be tested against authorized logic rather than a developer's interpretation of one sentence.

### Change and compliance

Rules may come from law, regulation, contract, policy, risk decisions, or operational practice. The analyst identifies the claimed source but qualified owners interpret legal or specialist obligations. Version rules by effective date and assess existing cases, data, communications, controls, and downstream systems when a rule changes.

## Integrated elicitation model

Begin with a discovery question and evidence plan. Decompose the need enough to expose capabilities, actors, events, and information. Use scenarios to understand context and variation. Use cases organize interaction with a defined system boundary. User stories create negotiable delivery conversations. Business rules remain controlled, reusable sources of constraint and decision logic. Confirm the resulting information with stakeholders and documentary evidence, recording both agreement and unresolved conflict.

No single representation is complete. A story can link to a use-case flow, which invokes a governed decision rule, consumes defined data, satisfies a stakeholder outcome, and supports a business requirement. The analyst's job is to preserve that network without creating documentation that costs more than the decisions it supports.

### A controlled transformation chain

Raw elicitation evidence should remain distinguishable from its interpretation. An interview statement, observation note, policy clause, and transaction measure are source records. Findings synthesize patterns in those records. Models organize the findings. Candidate requirements express needed outcomes or conditions. Approved requirements carry decision status and traceability. Moving through this chain requires judgment, so the analyst records uncertainty and does not rewrite an interpretation as if a stakeholder said it directly.

Confirmation occurs at the appropriate level. A participant can confirm that an interview summary reflects their view. A domain owner can confirm a rule interpretation. A product or business owner can accept a requirement within delegated authority. A specialist can confirm a security, privacy, legal, or accessibility constraint. These are different acts; one general "stakeholder sign-off" should not collapse them.

The practical objective is a coherent evidence network. A reviewer should be able to start with a requirement and locate its rationale, source, affected stakeholder, governing rule, related scenario, and acceptance evidence. The network can live in linked documents or a requirements tool. Its quality depends on stable identity and meaningful relationships, not a particular platform.

## Common misconceptions

- Elicitation is not simply asking stakeholders what they want.
- Decomposition does not require every requirement to have exactly one parent.
- A scenario is not a substitute for precise acceptance conditions.
- A use case is not a user-interface script.
- A user-story sentence is not a complete specification.
- A business rule is not whatever behavior the existing system happens to contain.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Discovery can expose personal data, operational vulnerabilities, commercial information, and unequal power. Collect only what is necessary, define access, protect participants from retaliation, use accessible methods, and state when evidence is incomplete. Never manufacture consensus by excluding difficult perspectives. Refer legal, privacy, security, safety, and specialist interpretations to authorized experts while retaining traceability to their decisions.

## Advanced synthesis

Elicitation quality depends on triangulation and transformation. Triangulation compares sources and methods; transformation turns raw statements into models and requirements without erasing provenance. Mature practice treats every representation as a lens: useful for some questions, incomplete for others, and governed through explicit links.
