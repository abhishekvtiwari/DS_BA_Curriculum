## Purpose

Requirements elicitation is disciplined discovery, not passive note-taking. It combines existing evidence, stakeholder knowledge, observation, models, questions, and iterative confirmation to reveal needs, rules, assumptions, exceptions, and constraints. This chapter develops six complementary ways to shape elicited information into analyzable requirements.

The chapter assumes the stakeholder and elicitation foundations from BA-M02. Its concern is how discovery becomes requirements knowledge while preserving sources and uncertainty. It contains Phase 1 theory only.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- plan discovery around decisions, stakeholders, evidence, risk, and unknowns;
- decompose broad outcomes without losing end-to-end value;
- use scenarios and use cases to expose normal, alternate, and exception behavior;
- use user stories without treating their sentence pattern as a complete specification;
- express business rules independently from processes and interfaces;
- confirm elicitation results and preserve disagreement, assumptions, and provenance.

## BA-03-C02-T01 - Discovery

### Concept

Discovery is the purposeful investigation of a need and its context before and during requirement development. It seeks enough shared understanding to make the next responsible decision; it is not a promise that uncertainty can be eliminated before delivery.

### Discovery frame

Define the decision to support, scope boundary, stakeholders and affected groups, known evidence, hypotheses, constraints, risks, unknowns, and confirmation route. Combine methods because each has blind spots: interviews reveal perspective, workshops expose interaction, observation reveals actual work, document analysis reveals formal rules, data reveals patterns, and prototypes reveal interpretation.

Discovery outputs may include a problem frame, glossary, process or context model, evidence register, assumptions, stakeholder needs, rules, candidate requirements, conflicts, and open questions. Preserve provenance. A statement from one interview is evidence of that person's view, not automatic proof of an organization-wide need.

### Iteration and stopping

Stop or narrow discovery when decision-critical questions have credible answers, major affected perspectives are represented, high-risk assumptions have a validation route, and remaining uncertainty is accepted by the correct authority. Deadlines do not transform unknowns into facts; record residual uncertainty and the cost of learning later.

## BA-03-C02-T02 - Decomposition

### Concept

Decomposition breaks a broad need, outcome, process, capability, feature, rule set, or requirement into smaller elements that can be understood, owned, prioritized, and verified. It is useful only when the relationships among the parts remain visible.

### Approaches

Decompose by outcome, capability, stakeholder, process stage, event, business rule, information object, geography, channel, risk, or system boundary. Choose the dimension that clarifies the current question. A capability hierarchy may support scope; an event model may expose missing behaviors; a data model may reveal semantic gaps.

Each child should contribute to its parent, and the set of children should cover the parent's intended scope without unjustified overlap. This does not require a mathematically perfect tree. Requirements often have many-to-many relationships, so trace links and models are more honest than forcing every item under one parent.

### Failure modes

Over-decomposition produces fragments with no user value or decision context. Under-decomposition hides incompatible rules and makes verification vague. Other errors include splitting by organizational ownership when the customer journey crosses teams, duplicating shared requirements, and losing non-functional or transition concerns during feature breakdown.

## BA-03-C02-T03 - Scenarios

### Concept

A scenario is a contextual narrative of how an actor, organization, or system moves from an initiating situation toward an outcome. Scenarios help participants reason about time, context, decisions, exceptions, and impact in language that can bridge business and technical perspectives.

### Scenario anatomy

State the purpose, actors, trigger, preconditions, context, main sequence, variations, exceptions, outcome, postconditions, information used, relevant rules, and unresolved questions. Include unsuccessful and recovery paths. A "happy path" alone systematically hides the conditions that often create cost and harm.

Scenarios can be current-state, future-state, misuse, failure, accessibility, support, or operational scenarios. They are analytical devices, not proof that every possible event has been found. Use frequency and impact to select additional paths, and avoid inventing a fictional user whose convenience erases diverse real users.

### From narrative to requirements

Extract candidate functions, rules, data, quality constraints, transition needs, and acceptance conditions. Trace them back to the scenario and confirm them separately. Do not copy narrative sentences into a requirement set without removing ambiguity and establishing ownership.

## BA-03-C02-T04 - Use Cases

### Concept

A use case describes interactions between an actor and a system boundary that achieve a goal. It organizes functional behavior around observable value and explicitly separates the main success flow from alternate and exception flows.

### Essential elements

Record identifier, goal, scope, level, primary actor, supporting actors, trigger, preconditions, minimal guarantees, success guarantees, main flow, extensions, business rules, data, special requirements, and open issues. The system boundary matters: if it shifts, actor responsibilities and requirements may change.

Use cases are strongest for interaction-rich behavior and weaker for broad strategy, data semantics, quality attributes, organizational change, or continuous algorithms. Complement them with other models. A use case should not become a click-by-click screen script unless interface detail is genuinely constrained.

### Quality

Flows should use observable actor intentions and system responsibilities, not hidden implementation. Extensions must state where they branch and how the use case ends or resumes. Confirm authorization, invalid input, dependency failure, cancellation, timeout, repeat submission, and recovery where relevant.

## BA-03-C02-T05 - User Stories

### Concept

A user story is a concise placeholder for a conversation about a small increment of stakeholder value. A common form identifies a role, capability, and benefit, but the sentence is not a complete requirement and is not valuable merely because it follows the template.

### Good use

A story should connect to an outcome, identify the real role, state a meaningful goal, carry acceptance criteria, expose dependencies and quality constraints, and be small enough to discuss and deliver responsibly. The "so that" clause explains value and should not restate the feature. Technical or enabling work may require a different expression rather than a fictional user.

Stories can be split by workflow step, rule variation, data variation, operation, scenario, risk, interface, or quality level. Splitting should preserve a coherent vertical slice where possible. Do not split in a way that delivers an unusable interface, bypasses security, or postpones accessibility indefinitely.

### Limits

A backlog can hide system-wide constraints, domain rules, transitions, and relationships. Use supporting models, rule catalogs, interface specifications, and trace links. "As a user" is usually evidence that stakeholder analysis is incomplete.

## BA-03-C02-T06 - Business Rules

### Concept

A business rule is an authoritative statement that defines or constrains business structure, decisions, behavior, or derivation. Rules apply across processes and systems and should be managed independently from the particular workflow or screen that currently implements them.

### Rule forms

Structural rules define terms and relationships. Operative rules require, prohibit, or permit behavior. Decision rules determine an outcome from conditions. Derivation rules calculate or infer a value. Each rule needs an identifier, precise statement, vocabulary, source, authority, jurisdiction or scope, effective period, exceptions, rationale, status, and trace links.

Decision tables and decision models help reveal missing, overlapping, or contradictory combinations. Natural-language rules should avoid undefined qualifiers such as normally, appropriate, recent, or high unless their interpretation is governed. Separate policy intent from executable logic so that implementation detail does not silently redefine authority.

### Change and compliance

Rules may come from law, regulation, contract, policy, risk decisions, or operational practice. The analyst identifies the claimed source but qualified owners interpret legal or specialist obligations. Version rules by effective date and assess existing cases, data, communications, controls, and downstream systems when a rule changes.

## Integrated elicitation model

Begin with a discovery question and evidence plan. Decompose the need enough to expose capabilities, actors, events, and information. Use scenarios to understand context and variation. Use cases organize interaction with a defined system boundary. User stories create negotiable delivery conversations. Business rules remain controlled, reusable sources of constraint and decision logic. Confirm the resulting information with stakeholders and documentary evidence, recording both agreement and unresolved conflict.

No single representation is complete. A story can link to a use-case flow, which invokes a governed decision rule, consumes defined data, satisfies a stakeholder outcome, and supports a business requirement. The analyst's job is to preserve that network without creating documentation that costs more than the decisions it supports.

## Common misconceptions

- Elicitation is not simply asking stakeholders what they want.
- Decomposition does not require every requirement to have exactly one parent.
- A scenario is not a substitute for precise acceptance conditions.
- A use case is not a user-interface script.
- A user-story sentence is not a complete specification.
- A business rule is not whatever behavior the existing system happens to contain.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Discovery can expose personal data, operational vulnerabilities, commercial information, and unequal power. Collect only what is necessary, define access, protect participants from retaliation, use accessible methods, and state when evidence is incomplete. Never manufacture consensus by excluding difficult perspectives. Refer legal, privacy, security, safety, and specialist interpretations to authorized experts while retaining traceability to their decisions.

## Advanced synthesis

Elicitation quality depends on triangulation and transformation. Triangulation compares sources and methods; transformation turns raw statements into models and requirements without erasing provenance. Mature practice treats every representation as a lens: useful for some questions, incomplete for others, and governed through explicit links.
