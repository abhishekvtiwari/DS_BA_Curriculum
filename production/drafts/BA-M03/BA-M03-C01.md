## Chapter overview

Requirements engineering turns an identified need into a controlled body of information that can guide decisions, design, delivery, verification, and change. This chapter establishes the vocabulary needed to do that work precisely. It explains the difference between a need and a requirement, separates major requirement classes, and shows how the classes connect without treating them as a rigid document hierarchy.

The focus is the meaning and quality of requirements, not a particular template or delivery method. The same concepts apply whether information is stored in a BRD, backlog, model, specification, repository, or linked set of records. This chapter contains theory only: no exercises, assessment questions, implementation labs, projects, or case studies.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this chapter, the learner should be able to:

- distinguish a problem or opportunity from a requirement and a proposed design;
- classify business, stakeholder, functional, non-functional, data, and transition requirements;
- explain how requirement levels trace from organizational intent to solution behavior;
- identify necessary quality attributes such as necessity, clarity, feasibility, verifiability, and traceability;
- recognize boundary errors, including premature design, unsupported requirements, and mixed requirement types.

## The requirements chain

```text
Need -> business outcome -> stakeholder outcome -> solution requirement
                                              |-> functional behavior
                                              |-> quality constraint
                                              |-> data rule
Change from current to future state -> transition requirement
```

The chain is a reasoning structure, not a promise that discovery is linear. A detailed requirement may expose a misunderstood need; feasibility may force an outcome to be reconsidered; stakeholder conflict may require a governance decision. Every item should nevertheless have a defensible source, purpose, owner, and relationship to the value being pursued.

The classification used in this chapter follows the IIBA distinction among business, stakeholder, solution, and transition requirements. Functional and non-functional requirements are two forms of solution requirement [2]. Data requirements are treated separately here because their semantic, lifecycle, quality, and governance concerns deserve explicit analysis, even though a project may classify them under functional, non-functional, or information requirements. Classification is a thinking aid: it should clarify ownership and reasoning, not force every statement into a document template.

| Analysis question | Requirement concept that provides the primary answer | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Why should anything change? | Need and business requirement | Performance data, policy obligation, strategy, observed harm |
| Whose outcome must improve? | Stakeholder requirement | User research, operational evidence, stakeholder analysis |
| What observable behavior must the solution provide? | Functional requirement | Scenarios, rules, process models, interface analysis |
| How well and under which limits must it operate? | Non-functional requirement | Risk analysis, service targets, accessibility and security standards |
| What information must be governed? | Data requirement | Data definitions, lineage, quality profiles, retention obligations |
| What temporary work enables safe adoption? | Transition requirement | Migration analysis, training needs, cutover and rollback plans |

ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 remains the published international requirements-engineering standard, although ISO now marks it as scheduled for revision. It defines requirements engineering across the lifecycle and emphasizes both the processes and information items that support requirements work [1]. This chapter therefore teaches durable reasoning principles while making the edition and revision status visible rather than presenting a standard as timeless.

## BA-03-C01-T01 - Need

### Concept

A need is a problem to be addressed, an opportunity to be realized, or a capability that must be established. It explains why change deserves attention. A need is not automatically valid because a senior stakeholder states it, and it should not be confused with the first proposed feature.

### Analysis

Define the affected population, current condition, desired difference, evidence, urgency, constraints, and consequence of inaction. Ask whether the condition is a root problem, a symptom, or an assumed cause. A statement such as "we need a chatbot" names a solution. "Customers cannot obtain reliable answers outside service hours, producing avoidable abandonment" expresses a need that can be tested and may have several solutions.

Needs can conflict. Faster access may increase fraud exposure; standardization may reduce local flexibility. The analyst records these tensions rather than hiding them inside a broad goal. A need should also identify who experiences the problem and who receives the value, because sponsor value and public or user value may differ.

### From a signal to a justified need

A signal is the starting observation: complaints rose, a regulator issued a direction, an opportunity appeared, or a measure moved outside tolerance. A signal is not yet a complete need. The analyst tests its provenance, scale, distribution, timing, and relationship to other conditions. If customer complaints rose by 30 percent, the useful questions are not limited to "What feature should we add?" They include which customers complained, what they were trying to accomplish, whether the classification changed, which channels were affected, and whether the change is sustained.

Evidence for a need can be quantitative, qualitative, documentary, observational, or regulatory. Different sources answer different questions. Transaction logs can reveal where abandonment occurs but not always why. Interviews can reveal experience and reasoning but not population frequency. Policy text can establish an obligation but may not explain an operational design. A defensible need statement records what is known, what remains uncertain, and which decision the available evidence is sufficient to support.

The analyst must also distinguish the affected person from the requester. A director may request automation because operating cost is increasing; a customer may need a predictable decision; an employee may need a safe way to handle exceptions; a regulator may require explainable outcomes. These needs can coexist, and their relative authority cannot be inferred from job seniority alone. The GOV.UK Service Manual similarly advises teams to base user needs on research, focus on the outcome rather than a proposed solution, and continue validating needs through delivery [5].

### Worked example - diagnosing failed appointment attendance

A clinic reports that 18 percent of booked appointments are missed and asks for an SMS reminder system. The analyst first defines the signal: missed appointments increased from 11 to 18 percent over six months, especially for early-morning appointments and patients referred from two districts. Interviews reveal unreliable transport, confusing rescheduling rules, language barriers, and reminders sent after the cancellation deadline. The need is not simply "send SMS reminders." A stronger need statement is: "Patients who face transport, language, or scheduling barriers need enough notice and a usable rescheduling route so they can attend or release appointments before clinical capacity is lost."

This statement preserves several solution options: earlier reminders, translated messages, assisted rescheduling, transport coordination, different slot allocation, or policy change. It also suggests measures such as attendance, timely cancellation, rescheduling completion, and unequal impact across patient groups. If the team later chooses SMS, that choice can be evaluated against the need instead of being mistaken for it.

### Quality test

A useful need statement has evidence, affected stakeholders, context, desired outcome, and a boundary. Common failures are solution bias, unsupported urgency, vague language, missing affected groups, and measures that merely count activity.

## BA-03-C01-T02 - Requirement

### Concept

A requirement is a usable representation of a need: a condition or capability necessary to achieve an outcome or satisfy an obligation. It can be expressed in text, a model, a rule, an interface definition, or another controlled form. The form is acceptable only when its audience can interpret and verify it.

### Attributes and quality

A controlled requirement normally has an identifier, statement, type, rationale, source, owner, priority, status, acceptance or verification method, dependencies, version, and trace links. Useful quality characteristics include necessary, singular, unambiguous, feasible, consistent, complete enough for its purpose, verifiable, and traceable.

Requirements should state what must be achieved and the essential conditions, while design describes how a solution will achieve it. The separation is contextual: "encrypt stored personal data" can be a mandatory security requirement, while naming a particular encryption library is usually a design choice unless an authorized standard makes it compulsory.

### Requirement, design, and implementation detail

Requirements and designs are related representations, not enemies. IIBA describes requirements as usable representations of needs and designs as usable representations of solutions; the same artifact may contain both if their purposes and relationships remain clear [2]. A wireframe can expose a requirement about information visibility. A prototype can reveal an accessibility need. An API schema can contain both required information behavior and a chosen technical structure. The analyst should label the status of each statement instead of relying on the document name to communicate it.

The requirement-design boundary depends on authority and constraint. "Use the enterprise identity provider" may look like design, but it becomes a legitimate constraint when an approved security architecture mandates it. Conversely, "provide secure authentication" is too broad to guide delivery unless risk owners define the required assurance, supported users, recovery behavior, and evidence of conformity. The useful test is not whether a sentence contains technology; it is whether the specified detail is necessary, authorized, and traceable to a need or obligation.

A requirement record should carry enough metadata to survive conversation and change. The statement communicates intent, while the rationale explains why it exists. The source identifies where the claim came from; the owner identifies who can make the relevant decision. Priority expresses relative sequencing or necessity under a defined method. Verification identifies how conformity will be demonstrated. Status and version prevent a discarded proposal from being treated as current. Trace links connect the item to upstream needs, downstream design and tests, and related risks.

| Record element | Question it answers | Defect when omitted |
|---|---|---|
| Identifier and version | Which controlled item is being discussed? | Reviews and changes target the wrong statement |
| Rationale and source | Why does it exist, and what supports it? | Attractive but unnecessary requirements survive |
| Owner and decision status | Who may approve, reject, or change it? | Consensus is assumed where authority is unresolved |
| Verification method | What evidence will show conformity? | "Done" becomes subjective |
| Trace links | What does it support and what depends on it? | Impact analysis misses consequences |

### Failure analysis - a polished but unusable requirement

"The portal shall provide fast and convenient access to account information" sounds professional but combines several undefined qualities. Which users and accounts are included? What information is visible? What does fast mean, at which percentile and load? Does convenient mean fewer steps, accessible interaction, or user satisfaction? Which privacy rules restrict access? A repair separates observable functions from measurable qualities: authenticated customers can view the current balance and the previous 24 months of transactions; 95 percent of successful balance requests complete within two seconds under the stated production workload; supported interactions conform to the approved accessibility criteria. The repair is longer because the original hid multiple decisions, not because length itself creates quality.

### Quality test

Ask: Why is this necessary? Who requires it? What exactly is in scope? How will conformity be determined? What conflicts with it? What changes if it is removed? If these questions cannot be answered, the item is not ready for approval merely because it is grammatically polished.

## BA-03-C01-T03 - Business Requirement

### Concept

A business requirement expresses an outcome, objective, capability, or obligation at the organizational or change level. It defines the value the initiative must enable, not the screen, field, workflow, or component to build.

### Structure

A strong business requirement connects the need to a measurable future condition. It may describe reduced processing time, increased service reach, compliance with an obligation, improved decision quality, or an enabled business capability. Measures need a baseline, target, population, time horizon, data source, and owner. "Improve customer service" is too broad; "reduce the median time from complete claim submission to decision from ten days to four days for eligible claims" is governable, subject to validation of the target and safeguards.

Business requirements constrain lower levels without dictating every solution decision. Several stakeholder and solution requirements may support one business outcome, and one solution requirement may support several outcomes. Traceability must preserve these many-to-many relationships.

### Turning strategy into a governable outcome

A strategy statement may express direction—enter a market, reduce risk, improve access, or simplify operations—without providing enough precision for an initiative. Business requirements translate that direction into outcomes that can guide investment and later evaluation. The translation needs a defined population, baseline condition, desired movement, time horizon, safeguards, and accountable benefit owner. It should also expose whether the target is mandatory, aspirational, or a hypothesis to be tested.

The analyst checks vertical alignment and horizontal coherence. Vertical alignment asks whether the requirement genuinely supports an authorized objective. Horizontal coherence asks whether it conflicts with other outcomes at the same level. A target to reduce average handling time may conflict with a target to increase first-contact resolution. Both may be valid, but the organization needs a rule for balancing them. Without that rule, delivery teams inherit an unresolved executive trade-off and may optimize the easiest measure.

Measures can be gamed or misread. An average can improve while the experience of a vulnerable group deteriorates. A conversion rate can rise because the denominator changed. A cost saving can shift work to customers or another department. Business requirements should therefore pair primary outcomes with safeguards and define the data lineage behind each measure. The outcome owner, not the delivery team alone, is accountable for deciding whether the observed result represents value.

### Worked example - service outcome rather than project output

A university proposes: "Implement a case-management platform by December." That is a delivery commitment, not a complete business requirement. Analysis shows that students currently wait a median of twelve working days for hardship-fund decisions, 14 percent of applications require avoidable rework, and applicants receive no status information. The business requirement becomes: "By the end of the next academic year, reduce the median time from complete hardship-fund application to authorized decision from twelve to five working days, while keeping incorrect-payment and appeal rates within approved tolerances."

The platform remains one possible design. Stakeholder requirements can now describe what students, assessors, finance staff, and auditors must accomplish. Functional and data requirements can follow. If the platform launches but decision time does not improve, the project has delivered an output without satisfying the business requirement.

### Failure modes

Common errors include relabeling project outputs as business requirements, treating revenue as the only value, omitting adverse impacts, setting targets without baselines, and accepting contradictory objectives without an authority decision.

## BA-03-C01-T04 - Stakeholder Requirement

### Concept

A stakeholder requirement describes what a particular stakeholder or stakeholder group must be able to achieve, experience, provide, or comply with in order to support the business requirement. It bridges organization-level outcomes and solution detail.

### Representation

The stakeholder label must not be limited to executives or direct users. Operators, customers, administrators, regulators, support teams, auditors, downstream systems, and people indirectly affected may have legitimate requirements. Representation should be evidence-based and accessible; a proxy should not silently replace the affected person's voice.

For example, a business requirement to shorten claim decisions may lead to stakeholder requirements for customers to know submission completeness, assessors to see verified evidence, and supervisors to monitor queues. These are outcomes for roles, not yet a final interface design.

### Representation, legitimacy, and level

Stakeholder requirements are not transcripts of stakeholder statements. A statement from an interview is evidence that must be interpreted, checked, and reconciled with other evidence. People may describe a preferred screen because the existing process limits their imagination, or request unrestricted access because the consequences of access are invisible to them. The analyst preserves the underlying outcome and constraint while distinguishing it from the proposed implementation.

The stakeholder group must be defined at a useful level. "Users need reporting" hides different jobs and authority. An operational manager may need a daily queue view to reallocate work; an auditor may need immutable evidence of decisions; an applicant may need understandable status and a correction route. Combining them produces either a vague requirement or a solution designed for the most powerful voice. Splitting groups too narrowly can create an unmanageable catalogue. The appropriate grouping is based on materially different goals, context, risk, or interaction—not demographic labels that do not affect the service.

Representation also raises legitimacy. Who can speak for a child, a patient without capacity, a person exposed to retaliation, or a user who cannot attend a workshop? Proxies may be necessary, but the record should state whose evidence is direct, whose is represented, and which gaps remain. The analyst should plan accessible research and alternative participation rather than interpreting absence as lack of interest. Requirements that affect rights, safety, privacy, or statutory entitlement require the appropriate accountable and specialist owners even when most participants prefer another outcome.

### Worked example - separating three stakeholder outcomes

A retailer wants to reduce fraudulent refunds. Store managers ask for every refund above a threshold to require regional approval. Cashiers warn that customers become aggressive when decisions are delayed, while customers need legitimate refunds resolved without repeated evidence. The analyst creates separate stakeholder outcomes: fraud investigators need sufficient evidence to identify suspicious patterns; store colleagues need a safe and time-bounded escalation route; legitimate customers need a clear decision and correction process. Regional approval is recorded as an option, not embedded in every requirement.

The resulting analysis reveals that a single approval rule would treat three different needs as one. A risk-scored route, documented exceptions, staff safety procedure, and customer appeal mechanism may satisfy the outcomes more effectively. The decision remains with authorized owners, supported by evidence about loss, delay, false positives, and safety.

### Conflict and authority

Stakeholder requirements can conflict. The analyst exposes the conflict, evidence, impacts, and decision rights. Majority preference does not automatically override law, safety, accessibility, privacy, or the needs of a seriously affected minority.

## BA-03-C01-T05 - Functional Requirement

### Concept

A functional requirement specifies behavior a solution must perform: a service, calculation, validation, transformation, decision, response, or interaction. It describes observable behavior under defined conditions.

### Construction

Good functional requirements identify the actor or trigger, preconditions, inputs, rule or behavior, output, state change, exceptions, and observable result. "The system shall validate the application" is incomplete unless validation rules, timing, failure response, and responsible source are known. Functional behavior can be represented through statements, use cases, process models, decision tables, state models, API contracts, or user stories with acceptance criteria.

Functional does not mean user-interface only. Scheduled jobs, calculations, audit events, integrations, notifications, administrative actions, and recovery behavior are functions. Avoid decomposing so far that relationships disappear; a list of atomic statements still needs a model of sequence and state.

### Choose a representation that exposes behavior

No single sentence form is sufficient for every function. A short declarative statement works for a simple obligation. A use case exposes actor goals, preconditions, normal flow, alternate flow, and failure behavior. A decision table is stronger when several conditions combine into rules. A state model is appropriate when behavior depends on lifecycle status. A data-flow or interface contract helps when information crosses boundaries. The analyst selects the representation that makes omissions and contradictions visible to its reviewers.

Precision requires conditions, not merely verbs. "The service sends a confirmation" omits the trigger, recipient, channel, information, timing, failure response, and privacy constraints. A controlled version might state that after an authenticated applicant successfully submits a complete application, the service records the submission identifier and time, displays the identifier, and sends a confirmation to the verified contact channel within the defined period. Separate requirements can govern content, delivery retry, accessibility, audit, and retention. This avoids a sentence so overloaded that no reviewer can reason about it.

Decomposition should follow observable responsibility. If one requirement includes validation, calculation, persistence, notification, and authorization, a failure cannot be isolated and tests become difficult to trace. Yet purely atomic statements can hide the user journey. Maintain both views: a scenario or process model for coherence and individually identified requirements for control. Trace them to each other instead of forcing one artifact to do both jobs.

### Worked example - decision logic for an application

An initial statement says, "The system shall approve eligible applications." Analysis identifies three conditions: identity verified, income within the permitted threshold, and required evidence complete. A decision table exposes four outcomes: approve when all conditions pass; request evidence when completeness fails; refer for manual assessment when income evidence conflicts; reject only when an authorized rule is definitively not met. The table also identifies which rules come from policy and who may change them.

This representation reveals that "eligible" was not a single fact. It combined data, policy, uncertainty, and decision authority. Functional requirements can now define evaluation, referral, notification, and audit behavior. Non-functional requirements can define response and reliability. Data requirements can define the evidence and provenance. Tests can cover each permitted combination rather than one happy path.

### Quality test

Verify normal, alternate, exceptional, and boundary behavior. Ensure the requirement does not contradict business rules or quality constraints and that a suitable test or inspection can observe the result.

## BA-03-C01-T06 - Non-functional Requirement

### Concept

A non-functional requirement specifies a quality attribute or constraint on the solution or its delivery. It describes how well, under what limits, or under what conditions functions must operate. The term is common but imperfect because these requirements are essential and often determine whether functionality is usable.

### Categories and measures

Categories include performance, availability, reliability, recoverability, scalability, security, privacy, usability, accessibility, maintainability, portability, interoperability, supportability, and regulatory constraints. Each needs an operational measure. "The dashboard must be fast" is vague; a measurable statement identifies the interaction, response threshold, workload, percentile, environment, measurement point, and exception conditions.

Quality attributes interact. Stronger authentication may affect task time; extensive logging may affect privacy and storage; availability may raise cost. Requirements should expose these trade-offs and define thresholds rather than promising unlimited quality.

### Quality is conditional, not an adjective

A measurable quality statement defines the subject, measure, operating conditions, threshold, and observation method. For performance, this may include transaction type, percentile, concurrent load, dataset size, network boundary, and measurement point. For availability, it may include service hours, exclusions, calculation window, recovery time, and maintenance policy. For accessibility, a standard conformance target must be combined with testing involving relevant users and assistive technologies; conformance alone does not prove that a complete service is usable [5].

Quality requirements should be scenario based. "The service is resilient" is not verifiable. A resilience scenario identifies a disruption, affected component, expected continuity, acceptable data loss, recovery time, degraded behavior, and evidence. "The solution is secure" hides threat, asset, actor, control objective, and residual risk. Security and privacy specialists may define controls, but the analyst still ensures that the business context, protected information, affected users, and verification responsibility are explicit.

Trade-offs belong in governance, not in accidental implementation choices. A target of sub-second response may require infrastructure cost; detailed audit logging may create storage and privacy consequences; a long session timeout may improve continuity while increasing exposure. The requirement set should identify these interactions and the owner who accepts the balance. Unlimited language—always, instant, zero risk, all devices—usually indicates an unstated context or an impossible promise.

### Worked example - making "available" testable

A team states, "The claims portal must always be available." Investigation distinguishes customer submission, staff assessment, document upload, and status viewing. The business can tolerate planned maintenance for staff assessment at night but not loss of submitted evidence. The revised requirement defines 99.9 percent monthly availability for customer submission, excludes an approved maintenance window announced in advance, requires recovery within thirty minutes after a qualifying incident, and permits no more than five minutes of acknowledged submission data loss. Separate requirements define status communication and a fallback route.

The resulting specification does not guarantee perfection. It exposes the service promise, calculation method, recovery expectation, exclusions, and operational response. Risk owners can decide whether the cost and residual risk are acceptable, and testers can design meaningful evidence.

### Quality test

Every quality requirement should state the object, metric, target, operating condition, measurement method, and authority. Avoid adjectives without scales, universal targets without risk justification, and metrics that cannot be observed in production or a representative environment.

## BA-03-C01-T07 - Data Requirement

### Concept

A data requirement defines the information a solution must capture, receive, derive, store, use, exchange, retain, protect, archive, or dispose of, together with its meaning and quality rules. Data requirements connect business semantics to system behavior and governance.

### Elements

Specify the data concept, definition, source, owner or steward, permitted values, format, unit, precision, relationships, validation, lineage, quality threshold, classification, access, retention, residency, consent or lawful basis where applicable, and disposal. Separate facts from derived values and preserve calculation logic and effective dates.

Collecting data "in case it is useful" creates cost and risk. Necessity and purpose should be explicit, especially for personal, sensitive, regulated, or model-training data. A field-level specification without a shared business definition can produce consistent syntax and inconsistent meaning.

### Meaning before fields

Data analysis begins with business concepts and decisions, not column names. "Customer," "active account," "application date," and "revenue" can have several legitimate definitions. A database may store them consistently while different reports interpret them differently. The requirement therefore needs a controlled definition, applicable population, time basis, source of truth, and relationship to other concepts before a physical schema is treated as authoritative.

Cardinality and temporality matter. Can a customer have several addresses? Can an application change owner? Is a status overwritten or preserved as history? Does a price mean the amount offered, accepted, invoiced, or paid? These questions affect functionality, reporting, audit, migration, and privacy. A conceptual data model can reveal them earlier than a field list. Physical design can then represent the agreed meaning within technical constraints.

Quality is fitness for a use, not a universal label. A mailing address may be complete enough for correspondence but unsuitable for geospatial risk analysis. A daily inventory feed may be timely for finance reporting and dangerously stale for checkout. The analyst links each quality threshold to the decision or behavior it supports. When a source cannot meet the threshold, the requirement should trigger an explicit option: improve capture, reconcile sources, change the decision, disclose uncertainty, or accept risk through the proper authority.

Lifecycle controls must accompany collection. Purpose limitation, access, retention, correction, lineage, sharing, and disposal affect trust and compliance. Data requirements should minimize collection while preserving necessary evidence. They should not place sensitive values, credentials, exploitable system details, or personal examples into widely circulated requirement documents. Such details belong in appropriately protected records linked through controlled identifiers.

### Worked example - one metric, three definitions

A subscription business asks for a churn dashboard. Marketing defines churn as customers who request cancellation during the month. Finance counts accounts whose paid service ends. Operations counts accounts disabled in the platform. All three numbers are internally consistent and answer different questions. The analyst records the business decisions each measure supports, defines the population and time window, identifies late payments and reactivations, and assigns a metric owner.

The dashboard requirement then distinguishes cancellation intent, revenue churn, and service deactivation rather than publishing one disputed figure. Data requirements specify sources, effective dates, reconciliation, and quality thresholds. A semantic disagreement that looked like a visualization problem becomes a controlled business-definition decision.

### Quality test

Check completeness, validity, accuracy, consistency, timeliness, uniqueness, and lineage according to the decision the data supports. Data quality targets should be proportionate; perfection is rarely feasible, while unbounded tolerance makes the requirement meaningless.

## BA-03-C01-T08 - Transition Requirement

### Concept

A transition requirement describes a temporary capability or condition necessary to move safely from the current state to the future state. It exists because change must be adopted, migrated, reconciled, trained, communicated, or operated in parallel.

### Scope and retirement

Examples include data cleansing and migration, historical conversion, cutover sequencing, temporary interfaces, parallel operations, training, communications, access provisioning, reconciliation, rollback, and decommissioning. A transition requirement must have entry conditions, owner, timing, dependencies, success evidence, and an exit or retirement condition.

Transition work is not secondary. A correct future-state function can still fail if users lack access, data is corrupted, responsibilities are unclear, or legacy obligations are abandoned. Conversely, a temporary workaround must not become permanent by default. Track expiry and residual obligations.

### Design the journey between states

A future-state requirement describes enduring capability; a transition requirement manages the journey to it. Confusing the two creates two common defects. First, temporary migration logic becomes embedded permanently in the product. Second, teams assume that deployment itself will create adoption, clean data, new responsibilities, and operational readiness. The analyst separates temporary work, assigns owners, and defines evidence for both activation and retirement.

Transition analysis covers people, process, information, technology, suppliers, controls, and service continuity. Data migration needs mapping, cleansing, reconciliation, exception handling, retention, and rollback. Training needs role-specific competence and support, not only attendance. Cutover needs entry criteria, decision authority, communications, monitoring, and abort conditions. Parallel running needs a rule for resolving conflicting records. Decommissioning needs archival, access, contractual, security, and audit decisions.

Every transition requirement should have a lifecycle. Its trigger explains when it becomes applicable. Its completion evidence explains how the team knows the temporary capability worked. Its retirement condition prevents indefinite operation. Its residual-obligation record identifies anything that survives, such as archived records, customer commitments, unresolved cases, or regulatory retention. A transition item without an exit condition is a likely source of permanent workaround and hidden cost.

### Worked example - migration without false confidence

A council replaces a licensing system containing fifteen years of records. The first proposal says, "Migrate all data before launch." Profiling shows duplicate applicants, obsolete codes, scanned evidence with uncertain ownership, and cases still under appeal. The analyst divides the transition requirements: map controlled fields; define which history must remain searchable; reconcile open balances; route ambiguous records for review; preserve evidence required for appeal; obtain a signed migration result; and retain a read-only legacy route until exit criteria are met.

The cutover requirement states who can authorize go-live, which defect thresholds block it, how failed batches are reversed, and how frontline staff report missing information. The retirement requirement closes the legacy route only after reconciliation, statutory retention, operational acceptance, and security confirmation. "All data migrated" is replaced by observable controls that acknowledge quality and legal differences among records.

### Quality test

Ask whether the requirement is truly temporary, how completion will be verified, what happens on failure, which records must be retained, and who authorizes retirement. Link it to both the current-state risk and the future-state capability.

## Integrated classification model

Consider a service organization seeking faster eligibility decisions. The need is delayed access caused by fragmented evidence. The business requirement sets a justified decision-time outcome. Stakeholder requirements describe what applicants, assessors, supervisors, and auditors need to accomplish. Functional requirements define evidence submission, validation, routing, decisions, and notifications. Non-functional requirements set response, availability, accessibility, security, and recovery thresholds. Data requirements govern identity, evidence, status, provenance, retention, and quality. Transition requirements control migration, training, parallel processing, reconciliation, and legacy retirement.

Classification improves questions but should not fragment meaning. Every lower-level requirement must trace upward, and every approved outcome must have adequate supporting requirements or an explicit reason why none is needed.

### Classification under uncertainty

An item can change classification as understanding improves. "Customers need immediate decisions" may begin as stakeholder evidence, become a business outcome with a justified time target, and produce functional and performance requirements. The history should show that refinement rather than pretending the final wording existed at discovery. Classification disagreements are often useful because they reveal missing rationale, authority, or level.

The analyst should avoid duplicating the same requirement across BRD, PRD, FRD, backlog, and test artifacts. One controlled item can be presented in several views, but its identity, owner, status, and trace relationships should remain stable. If a business outcome is rewritten as a feature in another document, drift becomes likely. Cross-document alignment is developed later in this module; the foundational rule is that information ownership matters more than the number of files.

INCOSE's requirements-writing guidance distinguishes characteristics of individual statements from characteristics of a set [4]. This distinction is crucial. A statement may be singular and verifiable while the collection is incomplete or inconsistent. Conversely, a coherent model may contain one ambiguous sentence. Review therefore operates at three levels: the individual item, the related set, and the traceable chain from need through design and evidence.

### End-to-end worked example - regulated address change

A financial institution observes repeated fraud and customer complaints during address changes. The need is a safe, understandable way for legitimate customers to update contact details while preventing unauthorized redirection. A business requirement sets outcomes for successful completion, fraud loss, handling time, and accessibility. Stakeholder requirements distinguish customers, contact-center staff, fraud investigators, compliance reviewers, and downstream correspondence teams.

Functional requirements describe authentication, address validation, risk-based referral, notification to old and new channels, effective-date handling, and reversal. Non-functional requirements establish response, availability, accessibility, audit integrity, and recovery thresholds. Data requirements define address meaning, provenance, verification status, retention, and permitted access. Transition requirements control migration of pending requests, staff training, parallel monitoring, and retirement of the old form.

Each requirement type contributes different information. None can substitute for the others, and no document title proves that the chain is sound. The quality test is whether authorized reviewers can follow the evidence from the original need to the implemented behavior and determine whether the intended outcomes occurred.

## Common misconceptions

- A requirement is not simply anything a stakeholder requests; it must be analyzed and governed.
- A business requirement is not a list of system features.
- Functional and non-functional requirements are interdependent, not competing categories.
- Data requirements are not limited to database columns.
- Transition requirements are temporary but still require ownership, verification, and change control.
- Agile delivery does not remove requirements engineering; it changes timing, representation, and level of detail.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Requirements distribute benefit, burden, access, risk, and decision power. The analyst should identify affected groups, surface exclusions, minimize unnecessary data, distinguish evidence from preference, and escalate mandatory legal, safety, security, privacy, or accessibility questions to qualified owners. The analyst can structure and trace requirements but cannot personally certify every specialist obligation.

## Advanced synthesis

Requirements quality is relational. A perfectly worded statement can still be wrong if it lacks a valid need, conflicts with another item, or cannot be governed. Mature requirements engineering optimizes the integrity of the whole chain: justified outcomes, represented stakeholders, coherent behaviors and constraints, controlled data, safe transition, and evidence of satisfaction.
