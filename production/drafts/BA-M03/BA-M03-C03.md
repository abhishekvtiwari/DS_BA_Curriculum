## Purpose

Requirements are useful only when people can reach a justified conclusion about whether they are understood, agreed, implemented, and satisfied. This chapter explains acceptance criteria and five connected quality concerns: testability, ambiguity, completeness, and consistency, with Given-When-Then as one structured expression. Quality is treated as risk-based analysis rather than proofreading.

This chapter contains Phase 1 theory only. It explains review and verification concepts but does not contain test scripts, exercises, assignments, or assessment questions.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- distinguish acceptance criteria from requirements, tests, approval, and value realization;
- write and review Given-When-Then statements without reducing all requirements to that format;
- judge whether a requirement has an observable verification method;
- locate lexical, structural, contextual, and scope ambiguity;
- assess completeness against purpose and risk rather than an impossible absolute;
- identify internal, external, vertical, and temporal inconsistency.

## BA-03-C03-T01 - Acceptance Criteria

### Concept

Acceptance criteria are the conditions used to decide whether a requirement, feature, increment, or solution outcome is acceptable to an authorized party. They make important expectations explicit before acceptance and provide a bridge between stakeholder intent and verification.

### Construction

Criteria should cover relevant normal, boundary, alternate, failure, quality, data, control, and authorization conditions. Each criterion should have a clear subject, trigger or context, expected observable result, and source. The set should be sufficient to protect the intended value and material risks without attempting to enumerate every possible test.

Acceptance criteria are not the same as test cases. A criterion states the condition of acceptability; a test case specifies data, steps, environment, and expected results used to evaluate it. Nor does meeting feature criteria prove the business outcome has been realized. Operational performance and value measures may be assessed later.

### Governance

Identify who proposes, reviews, and accepts the criteria, and what evidence is required. Late criteria can become disguised scope change; early criteria can still change through controlled learning. Approval must not override mandatory security, safety, privacy, accessibility, or legal controls.

## BA-03-C03-T02 - Given-When-Then

### Concept

Given-When-Then is a structured way to express behavior through context, event, and expected outcome. "Given" establishes relevant state, "When" identifies the event or action, and "Then" states an observable result.

### Good expression

Use business language and describe behavior rather than interface mechanics. Keep one principal event per scenario, make preconditions meaningful, and make outcomes observable. Add separate scenarios for boundaries and exceptions. Avoid vague outcomes such as "works correctly" and avoid embedding a long sequence of actions in the Given clause.

Example: Given an application is complete and the applicant has permission to submit, when the applicant submits it, then the service records the submission time, assigns an immutable reference, changes status to Submitted, and makes confirmation available. Additional scenarios would cover duplicate submission, dependency failure, revoked permission, and accessibility of confirmation.

### Limits

Given-When-Then is useful for event-driven behavior but less natural for data definitions, broad quality targets, organizational outcomes, continuous controls, transition obligations, or complex decision tables. It should complement, not replace, models and specifications. Tool syntax must not dictate business meaning.

## BA-03-C03-T03 - Testability

### Concept

A requirement is testable when a feasible method can produce evidence that distinguishes conformity from nonconformity. The broader term verifiable includes testing, inspection, analysis, demonstration, measurement, or review.

### Verification design

Define the object, condition, expected result, tolerance, measurement point, environment, data, timing, and responsible evaluator. "Available 99.9 percent" is incomplete without the service boundary, measurement window, excluded downtime, data source, and rounding rule. Some requirements require statistical evidence rather than a single deterministic result.

Trace the requirement to a verification method early. If no feasible method exists, the statement may be subjective, inaccessible to observation, or disproportionate in cost. This does not mean every human outcome must be reduced to a crude number. Qualitative criteria can use a governed rubric, independent review, or evidence protocol.

### Risks

Tests can pass while the wrong need is solved. Avoid verification bias by linking criteria upward to outcomes and adverse impacts. Production monitoring may be necessary for workload, reliability, fairness, or rare events, but monitoring itself creates data and privacy requirements.

## BA-03-C03-T04 - Ambiguity

### Concept

Ambiguity exists when a statement or model supports more than one materially different reasonable interpretation. It can arise from words, grammar, scope, references, units, timing, models, or unstated context.

### Detection

Look for vague qualifiers such as fast, appropriate, recent, normal, sufficient, and user-friendly; weak modal verbs such as should or may; pronouns with unclear references; hidden conjunctions; undefined terms; missing units; uncertain boundaries; and mixed responsibilities. Ask reviewers to paraphrase the requirement and compare interpretations. Examples and counterexamples can reveal disagreement that ordinary approval misses.

A glossary helps only if terms are used consistently and governed. Models can remove one ambiguity while introducing another through undefined notation or boundary. Precision should be proportionate: excessive legalistic detail can reduce comprehension and still leave important assumptions hidden.

### Resolution

Clarify the decision, define terms and scope, split compound statements, add measurable conditions, identify exceptions, and confirm the interpretation with affected and implementing parties. Preserve legitimate flexibility explicitly rather than relying on accidental vagueness.

## BA-03-C03-T05 - Completeness

### Concept

Completeness means that a requirement set contains enough necessary information and coverage for its stated purpose at a point in the lifecycle. It is relative to scope, decision, risk, and level of detail; no finite specification describes every possible fact.

### Dimensions

Check vertical coverage from need through stakeholder and solution requirements; horizontal coverage across actors, events, data, rules, qualities, exceptions, and transitions; lifecycle coverage from creation through retirement; and governance coverage of sources, owners, status, priority, verification, and traceability. Models, checklists, domain taxonomies, and trace matrices expose different gaps.

Completeness includes negative and failure space: unauthorized action, invalid data, unavailable dependency, cancellation, recovery, audit, and disposal. It also includes affected groups who may not be direct users. However, adding low-value detail can obscure material requirements and delay learning.

### Exit judgment

State the purpose of the completeness review, known exclusions, open issues, assumptions, and authority accepting residual gaps. "Complete" without a baseline or intended use is an unsafe status label.

## BA-03-C03-T06 - Consistency

### Concept

Consistency means requirements and related information do not contain unresolved contradictions in meaning, rule, value, timing, priority, responsibility, or interface. Uniform wording alone is not consistency; differently worded statements may be compatible, while identical terms may hide different meanings.

### Types and analysis

Internal consistency concerns items within a requirement set. External consistency compares requirements with policy, contract, law, architecture, operations, and other authoritative sources. Vertical consistency checks that lower-level requirements support higher outcomes. Temporal consistency considers versions and effective dates.

Use trace links, glossaries, rule catalogs, interface definitions, model checks, and cross-functional reviews. When two sources conflict, do not silently choose the newest or most senior statement. Establish authority, applicability, version, impact, and decision. Some differences are valid because jurisdictions, products, or periods differ; record the applicability condition.

### Change

Consistency must be re-evaluated after changes. A locally correct edit can invalidate acceptance criteria, data rules, reports, training, and downstream integrations. Baselines and impact analysis are therefore quality controls, not administrative overhead.

## Integrated quality model

Start with the purpose and source of a requirement. Review necessity and classification, then clarify language and models. Define acceptance conditions and a feasible verification method. Examine completeness across context, behavior, data, qualities, exceptions, and transition. Compare connected items for consistency. Record defects, decisions, and residual uncertainty; do not convert an unresolved concern into approval by changing its label.

## Common misconceptions

- Acceptance criteria do not replace requirements or business outcomes.
- Given-When-Then is not mandatory for every requirement type.
- A measurable requirement can still measure the wrong thing.
- More words do not guarantee less ambiguity.
- Completeness is purpose- and risk-relative, not absolute.
- Consistent formatting does not prove semantic consistency.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Quality gates can exclude less powerful stakeholders if review formats are inaccessible or deadlines silence dissent. Include affected perspectives, disclose assumptions, and distinguish a lack of response from approval. Specialist verification may be required for legal, safety, security, privacy, accessibility, statistical, or AI concerns. The analyst coordinates evidence but does not claim competence or certification they do not hold.

## Advanced synthesis

Acceptance and quality form a feedback system. Criteria expose ambiguity; verification design reveals missing measures; completeness analysis finds absent scenarios; consistency checks reveal conflicting sources. Defects are not merely editorial. They are evidence that the shared model of the change needs refinement.
