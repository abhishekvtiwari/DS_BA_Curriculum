## Chapter overview

Requirements are useful only when people can reach a justified conclusion about whether they are understood, agreed, implemented, and satisfied. This chapter explains acceptance criteria and five connected quality concerns: testability, ambiguity, completeness, and consistency, with Given-When-Then as one structured expression. Quality is treated as risk-based analysis rather than proofreading.

This chapter contains Phase 1 theory only. It explains review and verification concepts but does not contain test scripts, exercises, assignments, or assessment questions.

## Learning outcomes

The learner should be able to:

- distinguish acceptance criteria from requirements, tests, approval, and value realization;
- write and review Given-When-Then statements without reducing all requirements to that format;
- judge whether a requirement has an observable verification method;
- locate lexical, structural, contextual, and scope ambiguity;
- assess completeness against purpose and risk rather than an impossible absolute;
- identify internal, external, vertical, and temporal inconsistency.

## BA-03-C03-T01 - Acceptance Criteria

### Concept

Acceptance criteria are the conditions used to decide whether a requirement, feature, increment, or solution outcome is acceptable to an authorized party. They make important expectations explicit before acceptance and provide a bridge between stakeholder intent and verification.

### Construction

Criteria should cover relevant normal, boundary, alternate, failure, quality, data, control, and authorization conditions. Each criterion should have a clear subject, trigger or context, expected observable result, and source. The set should be sufficient to protect the intended value and material risks without attempting to enumerate every possible test.

Acceptance criteria are not the same as test cases. A criterion states the condition of acceptability; a test case specifies data, steps, environment, and expected results used to evaluate it. Nor does meeting feature criteria prove the business outcome has been realized. Operational performance and value measures may be assessed later.

### Governance

Identify who proposes, reviews, and accepts the criteria, and what evidence is required. Late criteria can become disguised scope change; early criteria can still change through controlled learning. Approval must not override mandatory security, safety, privacy, accessibility, or legal controls.

### From intent to an acceptance model

Acceptance begins by naming the object being accepted: a requirement statement, a backlog item, a release, a migrated dataset, an operational service, or a realized business outcome. Each object has a different evidence horizon. A feature can satisfy its delivery criteria while the service fails to improve the outcome that justified it. Conversely, an outcome can improve for reasons unrelated to the delivered feature. The acceptance model therefore links feature evidence to requirement evidence and keeps later outcome evaluation separate.

Criteria should sample the decision space deliberately. Normal examples confirm the central path; boundary examples test thresholds; negative examples establish prohibited outcomes; alternate and recovery examples clarify resilience; and quality examples define conditions such as response time, accessibility, or auditability. The analyst should identify the source and owner of each material condition. A tester may help make a condition observable, but cannot invent a business policy merely to make the test convenient.

### Worked example - accepting a refund capability

A weak criterion says, "The customer can receive a quick refund." A controlled set states that an authorized agent can approve an eligible refund up to the delegated limit; the service returns an immutable reference; the ledger records the amount, currency, reason, policy version, actor, and time; duplicate submission does not create a second refund; and a failed payment-provider call leaves the case recoverable without falsely showing completion. A separate service criterion defines the response-time percentile and measurement window. Later benefits measures examine complaint rate and handling effort. The result distinguishes functional acceptance, quality acceptance, and outcome evaluation.

## BA-03-C03-T02 - Given-When-Then

### Concept

Given-When-Then is a structured way to express behavior through context, event, and expected outcome. "Given" establishes relevant state, "When" identifies the event or action, and "Then" states an observable result.

### Good expression

Use business language and describe behavior rather than interface mechanics. Keep one principal event per scenario, make preconditions meaningful, and make outcomes observable. Add separate scenarios for boundaries and exceptions. Avoid vague outcomes such as "works correctly" and avoid embedding a long sequence of actions in the Given clause.

Example: Given an application is complete and the applicant has permission to submit, when the applicant submits it, then the service records the submission time, assigns an immutable reference, changes status to Submitted, and makes confirmation available. Additional scenarios would cover duplicate submission, dependency failure, revoked permission, and accessibility of confirmation.

### Limits

Given-When-Then is useful for event-driven behavior but less natural for data definitions, broad quality targets, organizational outcomes, continuous controls, transition obligations, or complex decision tables. It should complement, not replace, models and specifications. Tool syntax must not dictate business meaning.

### Scenario grammar and state

The three clauses carry different logical responsibilities. Given clauses establish facts that are already true and relevant to the behavior; they should not conceal the action being tested. When names the event under examination, normally one business-significant action or occurrence. Then describes externally observable consequences, including state, outputs, records, or notifications. An And clause extends the meaning of the preceding keyword; using many And clauses may indicate that the scenario is testing several responsibilities at once.

State matters more than prose form. "Given the user clicks Submit" is not a precondition; it is an action misplaced in the setup. "Then the database row is updated" exposes an implementation choice unless the database is the agreed observation boundary. Prefer outcomes visible at the system or service boundary, such as status, reference, balance, message, or audit evidence. Background setup can reduce repetition in executable examples, but business readers still need to know which conditions affect the result.

### Worked example - threshold boundaries

Suppose free delivery applies when the eligible basket value is at least 50 units after item discounts and before delivery charges. One scenario at 50 confirms inclusion of the boundary; another at 49.99 confirms exclusion; a third with an ineligible gift card shows how the basket is calculated. If the rule varies by currency or market, separate examples name that applicability rather than hiding it in shared setup. The examples clarify a governed rule; they do not replace the rule catalogue or currency precision requirements.

## BA-03-C03-T03 - Testability

### Concept

A requirement is testable when a feasible method can produce evidence that distinguishes conformity from nonconformity. The broader term verifiable includes testing, inspection, analysis, demonstration, measurement, or review.

### Verification design

Define the object, condition, expected result, tolerance, measurement point, environment, data, timing, and responsible evaluator. "Available 99.9 percent" is incomplete without the service boundary, measurement window, excluded downtime, data source, and rounding rule. Some requirements require statistical evidence rather than a single deterministic result.

Trace the requirement to a verification method early. If no feasible method exists, the statement may be subjective, inaccessible to observation, or disproportionate in cost. This does not mean every human outcome must be reduced to a crude number. Qualitative criteria can use a governed rubric, independent review, or evidence protocol.

### Risks

Tests can pass while the wrong need is solved. Avoid verification bias by linking criteria upward to outcomes and adverse impacts. Production monitoring may be necessary for workload, reliability, fairness, or rare events, but monitoring itself creates data and privacy requirements.

### Designing verification before approval

Early verification design is a diagnostic technique. For each requirement, ask what evidence would be convincing, where it can be observed, which environment and data are required, what tolerance applies, and who is qualified to judge it. Inspection can verify a required document attribute; analysis can verify a calculated capacity; demonstration can show an operational sequence; and testing can compare controlled inputs with observable outputs. Some claims need several methods. A safety control may require design inspection, failure injection, and operational monitoring.

Statistical requirements require special discipline. An average hides tail behavior, so a response-time requirement may specify the 95th percentile, population, window, exclusions, and minimum sample. A classification model may need separate performance and harm measures by relevant group, confidence intervals, drift thresholds, and a human-review path. The requirement should not promise certainty that the evidence cannot support. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 treats verifiability as a quality characteristic of requirements information [1].

### Failure analysis - a measurable but invalid target

A call center specifies that every agent must close eight cases per hour. The target is measurable, but it rewards premature closure and disadvantages complex or accessibility-related cases. Verification succeeds while service quality deteriorates. Reframing begins with the intended outcome, segments case types, includes resolution quality and reopening, and observes adverse effects. Testability is necessary, not sufficient: the measure must still represent the right need.

## BA-03-C03-T04 - Ambiguity

### Concept

Ambiguity exists when a statement or model supports more than one materially different reasonable interpretation. It can arise from words, grammar, scope, references, units, timing, models, or unstated context.

### Detection

Look for vague qualifiers such as fast, appropriate, recent, normal, sufficient, and user-friendly; weak modal verbs such as should or may; pronouns with unclear references; hidden conjunctions; undefined terms; missing units; uncertain boundaries; and mixed responsibilities. Ask reviewers to paraphrase the requirement and compare interpretations. Examples and counterexamples can reveal disagreement that ordinary approval misses.

A glossary helps only if terms are used consistently and governed. Models can remove one ambiguity while introducing another through undefined notation or boundary. Precision should be proportionate: excessive legalistic detail can reduce comprehension and still leave important assumptions hidden.

### Resolution

Clarify the decision, define terms and scope, split compound statements, add measurable conditions, identify exceptions, and confirm the interpretation with affected and implementing parties. Preserve legitimate flexibility explicitly rather than relying on accidental vagueness.

### Ambiguity as an interpretation test

Ambiguity reviews should produce competing interpretations, not just lists of suspect words. Ask two reviewers to paraphrase a statement independently, sketch the implied boundary, and supply an example that passes and one that fails. Differences reveal whether the ambiguity concerns vocabulary, actor, quantity, sequence, authority, or applicability. A controlled glossary can settle lexical meaning; a context diagram can settle boundary; a decision table can settle rule combinations; and a state model can settle lifecycle behavior.

Pronouns and passive voice often hide responsibility: "When it is approved, it is sent to them" leaves three identities unresolved. Conjunctions can hide atomicity: "validate and store the request" may describe behavior with different failure conditions. Quantifiers need populations: "all transactions are checked" must clarify historical imports, retries, reversals, and administrative corrections. Time needs a clock and calendar: "within two days" could mean 48 elapsed hours or two local working days.

### Worked example - one sentence, four interpretations

"The service should promptly notify high-value customers when unusual activity occurs" leaves authority and observability undefined. Reviewers disagree about whether high value describes the customer or transaction, whether unusual is a fraud score or a manual judgment, whether notify means attempt or successful delivery, and whether promptly means seconds or a business day. The analyst records the decisions separately: governed customer segment, risk-rule identifier and version, event that triggers notification, permitted channels, retry behavior, delivery evidence, and time objective. Precision follows the real decisions instead of adding decorative detail.

## BA-03-C03-T05 - Completeness

### Concept

Completeness means that a requirement set contains enough necessary information and coverage for its stated purpose at a point in the lifecycle. It is relative to scope, decision, risk, and level of detail; no finite specification describes every possible fact.

### Dimensions

Check vertical coverage from need through stakeholder and solution requirements; horizontal coverage across actors, events, data, rules, qualities, exceptions, and transitions; lifecycle coverage from creation through retirement; and governance coverage of sources, owners, status, priority, verification, and traceability. Models, checklists, domain taxonomies, and trace matrices expose different gaps.

Completeness includes negative and failure space: unauthorized action, invalid data, unavailable dependency, cancellation, recovery, audit, and disposal. It also includes affected groups who may not be direct users. However, adding low-value detail can obscure material requirements and delay learning.

### Exit judgment

State the purpose of the completeness review, known exclusions, open issues, assumptions, and authority accepting residual gaps. "Complete" without a baseline or intended use is an unsafe status label.

### Coverage models and stopping rules

Different models reveal different omissions. A context model exposes missing external actors and interfaces. A process model exposes triggers, handoffs, delays, and exceptions. A state model exposes invalid transitions and terminal states. A data model exposes definitions, cardinality, ownership, and lifecycle. A decision table exposes missing combinations. A stakeholder or journey view exposes needs that a system-centered model overlooks. Traceability exposes higher-level needs without realization and lower-level items without justification.

Completeness also depends on the maturity of the decision. A discovery brief may be complete enough to authorize research while deliberately leaving product scope open. A baseline used for supplier contracting needs stronger boundary, acceptance, interface, and change controls. A release decision needs evidence about implementation, operations, support, migration, and residual risk. Declaring every early artifact incomplete until final detail exists would prevent learning; declaring it complete without naming its purpose would conceal risk.

### Worked example - finding the missing lifecycle

A membership specification covers sign-up, payment, and profile editing. A lifecycle review asks how accounts are suspended, restored, merged, transferred after organizational change, retained after cancellation, and deleted after the applicable period. The missing requirements affect finance, privacy, support, analytics, and identity integrations. No amount of detail in the sign-up flow could compensate for that absent lifecycle coverage. The corrected set identifies states, retention authority, transition rules, evidence, and owners.

## BA-03-C03-T06 - Consistency

### Concept

Consistency means requirements and related information do not contain unresolved contradictions in meaning, rule, value, timing, priority, responsibility, or interface. Uniform wording alone is not consistency; differently worded statements may be compatible, while identical terms may hide different meanings.

### Types and analysis

Internal consistency concerns items within a requirement set. External consistency compares requirements with policy, contract, law, architecture, operations, and other authoritative sources. Vertical consistency checks that lower-level requirements support higher outcomes. Temporal consistency considers versions and effective dates.

Use trace links, glossaries, rule catalogs, interface definitions, model checks, and cross-functional reviews. When two sources conflict, do not silently choose the newest or most senior statement. Establish authority, applicability, version, impact, and decision. Some differences are valid because jurisdictions, products, or periods differ; record the applicability condition.

### Change

Consistency must be re-evaluated after changes. A locally correct edit can invalidate acceptance criteria, data rules, reports, training, and downstream integrations. Baselines and impact analysis are therefore quality controls, not administrative overhead.

### Contradiction is contextual

Automated comparison can flag duplicated terms, conflicting numbers, broken links, or different modal verbs, but semantic consistency needs applicability. "Retain records for six years" and "delete profile data after two years" may conflict, or they may govern different data classes under different authorities. The resolution records the data categories, event that starts each period, legal basis, exceptions, and relationship between source records and derived profiles. Harmonizing the number without understanding the context could create a compliance defect.

Vertical consistency asks whether detail preserves intent. A business requirement to reduce exclusion may be contradicted by a functional requirement that supports only one inaccessible channel, even though the sentences share no vocabulary. Horizontal consistency examines sibling requirements and interfaces. Temporal consistency examines which rule version applies to an event or case. Model-text consistency checks whether diagrams, tables, examples, and prose describe the same behavior.

### Worked example - resolving a cross-document conflict

A product brief permits users to correct an address until dispatch. An interface specification rejects all edits after payment, while support guidance tells agents to make manual corrections at any time. The analyst identifies three different boundaries and decision owners, models the order lifecycle, and measures operational consequences. The approved decision permits self-service until allocation, controlled agent correction until carrier handoff, and a reroute request afterward. Each document references the same states and rules, and affected tests and training are updated.

## Integrated quality model

Start with the purpose and source of a requirement. Review necessity and classification, then clarify language and models. Define acceptance conditions and a feasible verification method. Examine completeness across context, behavior, data, qualities, exceptions, and transition. Compare connected items for consistency. Record defects, decisions, and residual uncertainty; do not convert an unresolved concern into approval by changing its label.

Quality review is most effective as a sequence of different questions rather than one general request to "check the requirements." First, validation asks whether the requirement represents a justified stakeholder or business need. Verification then asks whether the statement has the characteristics needed for its intended use. Acceptance design asks what evidence will permit an authorized decision. Coverage analysis asks what relevant space has not been represented. Consistency analysis compares the result with connected sources and artifacts. IIBA distinguishes verification of requirements quality from validation of alignment with business goals, which prevents a well-written but unnecessary requirement from passing merely because it is precise [2].

A review record should preserve the item identifier, issue, consequence, evidence, originator, owner, disposition, rationale, and affected version. Severity is based on consequence rather than editorial appearance. A missing comma may be harmless; an undefined "customer" may cause unauthorized disclosure. The disposition can be accepted, corrected, rejected with rationale, deferred with owner and date, or transferred to an identified decision authority. Closing a comment without recording the resulting change or decision destroys auditability.

Consider a digital application service preparing for baseline. The quality review begins with its outcome and affected groups. Acceptance examples cover eligible and ineligible decisions, incomplete evidence, duplicate requests, timeouts, assisted channels, and recovery. Verification analysis identifies which evidence comes from automated tests, accessibility evaluation, security testing, operational rehearsal, and policy-owner inspection. Completeness models expose missing withdrawal and retention behavior. Consistency checks find that the notification text promises a response sooner than the service-level requirement. The team corrects the source requirements and trace links before authorizing the baseline.

Not every uncertainty must be eliminated before work proceeds. The decision authority may accept an open question when the consequence is understood, a safe learning method exists, and later change remains affordable. The record should distinguish accepted uncertainty from overlooked omission. For example, a team may baseline a limited pilot with an explicit volume ceiling while performance evidence is gathered. It should not claim that full-scale performance is verified. This produces an honest, usable baseline instead of either false certainty or indefinite analysis.

Given-When-Then examples can support this system without becoming the system itself. Cucumber's reference emphasizes that Then describes an observable outcome rather than an internal mechanism [3]. That boundary keeps examples meaningful to stakeholders and leaves legitimate implementation choice. Other requirements may need equations, decision tables, state models, prose rules, or quality scenarios. Quality is achieved when these representations agree, carry sources and ownership, and enable proportionate evidence—not when every item is forced into the same syntax.

The final release question is therefore not "Did the document pass the checklist?" It is "Is the controlled information sufficiently valid, clear, complete, consistent, feasible, and verifiable for this decision, and are the remaining risks visible to the people authorized to accept them?" A checklist helps reviewers remember perspectives; it cannot make the judgment or confer authority. The answer applies to a named version, purpose, scope, and time. New evidence or changed context reopens the analysis through controlled change.

## Common misconceptions

- Acceptance criteria do not replace requirements or business outcomes.
- Given-When-Then is not mandatory for every requirement type.
- A measurable requirement can still measure the wrong thing.
- More words do not guarantee less ambiguity.
- Completeness is purpose- and risk-relative, not absolute.
- Consistent formatting does not prove semantic consistency.

## Ethics, limitations, and professional boundaries

Quality gates can exclude less powerful stakeholders if review formats are inaccessible or deadlines silence dissent. Include affected perspectives, disclose assumptions, and distinguish a lack of response from approval. Specialist verification may be required for legal, safety, security, privacy, accessibility, statistical, or AI concerns. The analyst coordinates evidence but does not claim competence or certification they do not hold.

## Advanced synthesis

Acceptance and quality form a feedback system. Criteria expose ambiguity; verification design reveals missing measures; completeness analysis finds absent scenarios; consistency checks reveal conflicting sources. Defects are not merely editorial. They are evidence that the shared model of the change needs refinement.
