# Analyst to Architect — going to market

*Coordinator, 20 September 2026. Built around the two decisions you made today: self-publish, digital first; and rupee and dollar tiers from day one.*

---

## 1. The first thing to face: this is not one book

| | |
|---|---|
| Written so far | 56 chapters, **639,318 words** |
| Still to write | 27 chapters (25–27, 60–83, the appendices) |
| Projected finished size | **around 950,000 words**, at the density the book has actually been written to |
| Study time the book itself declares | **762 hours** across the chapters that state one |
| Code blocks | 1,272, every printed output verified |
| Figures | 135 and counting, all generated by script |
| Companion data | six seeded generators, five databases, a dbt project, a Python package |

A normal technical book is 100,000 to 150,000 words. This is six to nine of them. The blueprint said 497,000 words; the book is running at roughly twice that, because the chapters were written to teach rather than to summarize, which was the right call and is the reason it is worth selling.

But a single 950,000-word product cannot be sold, printed, or finished by a reader. **The first strategic decision is to stop calling it a book and start treating it as a curriculum sold in volumes.**

## 2. What it actually is

Three things, and the order matters for how it is sold:

1. **A career path with no gap in it.** Almost every data book starts at "you know Python". This one starts at "what is data" and ends at architecture and leadership. The unbroken ladder is the product.
2. **A book where every number is real.** 1,272 code blocks, every printed output produced by running the code against a real database, with the check scripts shipped so a reader can re-run them. Four verification tools, one per language. This is rare and it is provable, which makes it marketable in a way that "comprehensive" never is.
3. **An interview playbook attached to the teaching.** Part VIII's fifteen question banks answer back into the chapters that taught the material. That is the difference between a course and something someone buys three weeks before an interview.

The weakness to plan around: it is enormous, it is unfinished, and it is written by an author the market has not heard of yet. Sections 6 and 8 deal with both.

## 3. Positioning

**For** someone in India or elsewhere who wants a data career and does not have a computer science degree,
**who** is tired of tutorials that skip the middle,
**this is** the complete path from "what is data" to data architect,
**unlike** a course library or a stack of single-topic books,
**because** every chapter runs on one real company's data, every number in it was computed rather than invented, and the whole route is one continuous ladder.

One line: **"From your first spreadsheet to designing the platform. One company's data, 83 chapters, nothing skipped."**

What not to lead with: the word count. It impresses nobody and frightens beginners. Lead with the ladder and the proof.

## 4. The product ladder

| Tier | What it is | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|
| **Free — The On-Ramp** | Part 0 entire: Chapters 1–6, ~54,000 words, with the companion setup files | The lead magnet, and the proof of quality. Six full chapters is a big give; it is also the strongest argument the book can make about itself |
| **Volume 1 — The Analyst** | Parts I and II: Chapters 7–27. Spreadsheets, SQL, Python, cleaning, visualization, BI, automation, statistics, business acumen | The volume most people buy. Complete on its own: a reader finishes it employable as an analyst |
| **Volume 2 — The Specialist** | Parts III and IV: Chapters 28–44. Advanced SQL, analytics engineering, inference, causal inference, machine learning end to end | For the reader who finished Volume 1, and for the working analyst who wants the next rung |
| **Volume 3 — The Builder** | Parts V and VI: Chapters 45–59. Ingestion, pipelines, quality, scale, streaming, cloud, deep learning, generative AI, MLOps | The highest-paying material, and the most likely to be bought on its own by people already working |
| **Volume 4 — The Architect** | Part VII plus the closing: Chapters 60–67, 83 | The smallest volume and the one with the least competition |
| **The Interview Playbook** | Part VIII: Chapters 68–82, fifteen question banks | Sells on its own, to a reader with a deadline. Highest urgency, shortest sales cycle |
| **The Complete Path** | Everything, plus the companion repository, the datasets, and free updates | The anchor. Most revenue comes from bundles once more than two volumes exist |
| **The Companion** | The repository: generators, databases, checks, verifiers, project scaffolds | Bundled with every paid tier, never sold alone. It is what makes the book usable, and what makes piracy less attractive than buying |

Two things this ladder does. It lets you **ship before the book is finished** (Volume 1 can go on sale while Part VIII is being written), and it lets one reader buy four times.

## 5. Pricing

Tiered from day one, as you chose: one product, two currencies, regional detection.

| Tier | India (₹) | International (US$) |
|---|---|---|
| The On-Ramp | free | free |
| Any single volume | 1,499 – 2,499 | 39 – 59 |
| The Interview Playbook | 1,999 – 2,999 | 49 – 69 |
| Two volumes | 2,999 – 3,999 | 79 – 99 |
| The Complete Path | 5,999 – 8,999 | 149 – 249 |
| The Complete Path, founding price (pre-finish) | 3,999 | 99 |

The reasoning, so you can move these with your eyes open:

- **The India tier is not a discount, it is the main market.** The Riverstone examples, the rupee figures, the Indian job market in Chapter 8: this book is most useful to an Indian reader, and that reader compares its price to a coaching course, not to an American ebook.
- **The international tier is priced against a course, not a book.** At 762 hours of material with verified code and data, the comparison a buyer makes is with a bootcamp, and any of these numbers is a fraction of one.
- **Sell the founding price before the book is finished.** Early buyers get every volume as it lands, at a price that will not exist later. This funds the remaining work and, more importantly, produces the first readers, who are the only source of the testimonials and corrections the book needs.
- **Never discount below the founding price afterwards.** The people who backed it early must stay the ones who paid least.

## 6. The platform arithmetic, which favours your own site

This is where the self-publish decision pays, and it is worth seeing the numbers.

**Gumroad** charges 10% plus payment processing, which works out around **13.2% per sale** in 2026. On a ₹5,999 sale you keep roughly ₹5,200.

**Amazon KDP** is worse for this book specifically, for three reasons. Its 70% royalty band runs from $2.99 to $12.99, Amazon raised the ceiling from $9.99 on 7 July 2026, its first change since 2007, and anything priced above $12.99 drops to **35%**. A volume of this book priced at $49 would earn 35%, not 70%. Worse, the 70% tier deducts a delivery fee of about **$0.15 per megabyte**, and this book is 135 figures and hundreds of thousands of words. And the change is opt-in: existing titles priced between $10 and $12.99 stay at 35% until manually updated.

So the shape is:

- **Your own landing page plus a checkout (Gumroad, Lemon Squeezy or an Indian processor such as Razorpay for rupee payments)** is the primary channel. You keep roughly 87%, set any price, run purchasing-power tiers, and ship a corrected edition the same day you fix a chapter: which matters enormously for a book whose subject changes yearly.
- **Amazon KDP is a discovery channel, not a revenue channel.** Put one volume there, priced inside the 70% band, as a way for strangers to find you. Treat the royalty as an advertising cost.
- **Print later, or never.** A 950,000-word print book is four physical volumes and a logistics problem. Revisit only if demand proves it.

## 7. The launch sequence

**Phase 1: before anything is for sale (now, while Part VII is written).**
Put the On-Ramp behind an email address. Nothing else. The only goal is a list and the first honest feedback from real beginners. If Chapter 1 does not hold a beginner, nothing downstream matters.

**Phase 2: the founding release.**
When the coherence pass has repaired Parts 0 to II, sell **Volume 1** and **The Complete Path at the founding price**. Everything else is promised, dated, and delivered as it lands. Write the delivery dates down and keep them.

**Phase 3: volume by volume.**
Each volume ships to founding buyers and goes on sale at full price. Every release is a reason to talk to the list again.

**Phase 4: the Interview Playbook.**
Ship it near a hiring season. It is the tier with the shortest decision time and the most obvious urgency, and it pulls buyers back into the volumes.

**Phase 5: the second edition.**
The pre-publication refresh list already exists. Tool versions, prices and job-market figures go stale; a book that visibly updates is worth more than one that does not, and on your own platform an update costs nothing to distribute.

## 8. The marketing this particular book can honestly do

Skip anything that requires you to be a known name. Use what the book already contains.

- **The verification story.** "Every number in this book was recomputed from a real database, and here are the scripts." Publish the check scripts publicly. No competing book can say this, and it is an interesting thing to write about.
- **Chapters as public artifacts.** Chapter 5's issue tree, Chapter 4's compound-growth trap, Chapter 45's watermark that silently misses two orders, Chapter 23's revenue-drop decomposition. Each is a self-contained post that teaches something real and ends with the book.
- **The failure demonstrations.** The book's best habit is showing the broken version first: the pipeline that doubles the daily flash to ₹77,420, the model that scores 98% because of a leaked column, the ID watermark that misses orders 10174 and 10175. These are the most shareable things in it.
- **The Riverstone dataset as a free public good.** Release the generators for anyone to practise on, book or no book. It costs you nothing, it is useful to people, and every person who uses it has the book's name in front of them.
- **Your own position.** A business analyst reporting to a CEO, writing the book he needed. That is a more credible author than another course seller, and it should be on the page in two sentences, not hidden.

## 9. What is missing that you need before selling

1. **A title decision and an author name on the cover.** Still open in the blueprint.
2. **A landing page** with the promise, the ladder, sample chapters, and the proof.
3. **The free On-Ramp as a finished artifact**: the Part 0 volume PDF exists; it needs a cover, a front matter and a download.
4. **An email list.** Nothing else in this plan works without one.
5. **The companion repository, public.** It is also the fix for the problem you already have: the project's knowledge store is nearly full and every part chat is passing zips by hand.
6. **Five to ten real readers**, before any launch, who read the On-Ramp and tell you where they stopped.
7. **A licence and a piracy position.** A 950,000-word book will be copied. The counter is not enforcement; it is updates, the companion repository and being the place readers come back to.

## 10. What I would not do

- **Do not wait for all 83 chapters.** Volume 1 is a complete, sellable product now, and shipping it produces the only thing the book still lacks, which is readers.
- **Do not lead with Amazon.** The royalty arithmetic above is the reason.
- **Do not cut the book to fit a publisher.** A traditional deal would demand roughly a 150,000-word book, which would remove exactly the thing that makes this one different: it does not skip the middle.
- **Do not sell it as an interview-cram product.** Part VIII sells that way; the book does not. The promise is a career, and the interview bank is what makes it credible.
- **Do not announce a date you have not tested.** Twenty-seven chapters remain, plus two repair passes. Founding buyers forgive slow; they do not forgive missed dates they were given.
